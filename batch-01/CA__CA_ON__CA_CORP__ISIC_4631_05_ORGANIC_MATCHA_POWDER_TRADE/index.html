Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA / Agence du revenu du Canada), including the Business Registration Online portal at https://www.canada.ca/en/services/taxes/business/business-number.html.

E-government filing portal

A foreign-owned corporation incorporated under the Canada Business Corporations Act or the Ontario Business Corporations Act must obtain a federal Business Number (BN) from the CRA. The non-resident corporation submits Form RC1 or files online through the Business Registration Online (BRO) portal, providing the certificate of incorporation, registered office address, authorized representative or agent in Canada, and a description of taxable activities. The BN is the single identifier for income tax, GST/HST, customs, payroll, and provincial corporate tax. The GST/HST program account is added separately; Ontario corporate income tax is administered by the CRA on behalf of Ontario through the T2 corporate income tax return.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation — A foreign-owned corporation incorporated under the Canada Business Corporations Act or the Ontario Business Corporations Act must obtain a federal Business Number (BN) from the CRA. The non-resident corporation submits Form RC1 or files online through the Business Registration Online (BRO) portal, providing the certificate of incorporation, registered office address, authorized representative or agent in Canada, and a description of taxable activities. The BN is the single identifier for income tax, GST/HST, customs, payroll, and provincial corporate tax. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not have a U.S.-style check-the-box regime. A federal/Ontario business corporation is mandatorily taxed at the entity level as a separate taxpayer. Limited statutory elections are available, including: (i) designation as a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) for small business deduction purposes; (ii) subsection 216(4) election for non-residents earning Canadian property income; (iii) late-filing or retroactive elections under Income Tax Act subsection 220(2.1); and (iv) hybrid-mismatch rules are statutory disallowances rather than elective exceptions.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies in a single calendar quarter or over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters (the small supplier threshold). A non-resident registrant carrying on business in Canada must register once this threshold is exceeded with respect to supplies made in Canada, unless making only zero-rated exports. Simplified GST/HST registration is also required for foreign suppliers of digital services/intangible supplies to Canadian consumers once Canadian taxable revenues exceed CAD 30,000.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A zero-rated export applies to tangible personal property, including organic matcha powder, supplied to a non-resident recipient and exported from Canada in continuous transit as soon as is reasonably possible after the supply. The exporter must not deliver, consume, or use the goods in Canada. The supply is zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act. Goods may be shipped by the supplier or on the supplier's behalf, and evidence must demonstrate that title and possession passed to the non-resident outside Canada.

  • Canada Border Services Agency export reporting document, including CBSA B13 Export Declaration or Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) data where required
  • Commercial invoice containing matcha grade, organic certification references, HS classification, quantity, unit price, total consideration and explicit zero-rating reference
  • Bill of lading or air waybill showing consignor, consignee, routing, and destination outside Canada
  • Signed proof of delivery or freight forwarder records showing physical export and overseas delivery
  • Bank remittance evidence, including SWIFT MT103, Inward Remittance Certificate or equivalent payment confirmation, plus purchase order and sales contract
  • Packing list, insurance certificate, and any organic lot/certification documentation supporting the commercial shipment

Canada does not impose real-time fiscal clearance, compulsory digital stamping, or state-run transaction-level API reporting. The CRA requires registered entities to issue GST/HST invoices containing prescribed information, retain electronic records in a readable and auditable format, and file periodic GST/HST returns through electronic channels. Voluntary Peppol-based e-invoicing is being adopted but remains permissive, not mandatory.

The CRA will scrutinize zero-rating claims by cross-checking CERS export data, bills of lading, insurance schedules, freight forward records and banking flows. Missing, contradictory, or fabricated documentation will lead to reassessment converting the sale to taxable HST at 13%, cancellation of input tax credits/refunds, and imposition of arrears interest. Failure to remit, false statements, or gross negligence may attract civil penalties of 25% to 50% of assessed tax, and criminal tax evasion prosecution remains possible. The normal GST/HST reassessment limitation period is four years, but it may be extended indefinitely where a waiver was filed or where there is misrepresentation or fraud.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current and capital account convertibility. The Bank Act and Currency Act impose no exchange controls or surrender requirements. Profit remittances to non-residents are legally unrestricted, subject to (i) Part XIII Canadian withholding tax (statutory 25% unless reduced by treaty), (ii) arm's-length transfer pricing under T106 filings, and (iii) documentary proof of earnings and source-of-funds. No exchange licence or central bank approval is required for inbound investment or outbound trade settlement.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender obligation and no mandatory conversion ratio under Canadian law. Export proceeds may be held, converted, or reinvested in any currency. FINTRAC reporting applies to cross-border wire transfers of CAD 10,000 or more (or equivalent). CBSA export declarations apply to physical matcha shipments; all foreign-currency positions must be fairly reflected in the books for CRA and OSFI purposes.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), with prudential FX oversight through the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and cross-border transaction compliance through the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium with legal constraints. Under the Bank Act, only chartered banks may accept deposits in Canada. Offshore digital EMIs are not permitted to take Canadian deposits but may operate as registered Money Service Businesses (MSBs) under FINTRAC for payment processing. A matcha trading company may use an EMI for FX conversion and supplier payments only if the EMI maintains a Canadian-registered MSB licence, holds funds through a Canadian Schedule I bank, and does not market itself as a deposit-taking institution. Higher operational risk and cash-flow latency make bank-native clearing preferable.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: T106 filing threshold: C$1 million for non-arm's-length cross-border goods transactions in a fiscal year; C$1 million for services, interest, royalties, and other transactions; Canada has not codified formal Local/Master File tiers, but CRA expects contemporaneous transfer pricing records for transactions exceeding C$1 million; country-by-country reporting is mandatory for multinational groups with consolidated group revenue equal to or exceeding C$750 million in the prior fiscal year

Economic substance scope: Canada applies a substance over form doctrine through the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) in section 245 of the Income Tax Act; transactions must have a real economic substance, business purpose, and cannot be designed primarily to obtain a tax benefit; Canada has no filing of Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to the OECD's ESR in certain low-tax centers, but CRA can recharacterize arrangements lacking commercial substance, deny treaty benefits, and impose penalties; a purely paper procurement company with no employees, office, or operational risk will fail economic substance and create expose under GAAR and treaty-shopping provisions

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Under Income Tax Act section 239(1), conviction on indictment: fine of 50% to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment up to 5 years; summary conviction: fine up to C$25,000 and imprisonment up to 12 months; CRA may also assess gross negligence penalties under subsection 163(2) equal to the greater of C$100 and 50% of the understated tax

Director personal liability: Directors can be collectively liable for the corporation's unremitted source deductions, GST/HST, and under certain circumstances withholding taxes under section 227.1; travel bans and seizure of personal assets can follow if a known failure to remit is proven and the director acted knowingly or negligently
Shell Company / Economic Substance Failure

Statutory sanction: CRA may deem the corporation non-resident or a conduit, disallow treaty benefits under Article 29 of the Canada-US treaty, reassess for the full tax amount without deduction of intercompany payments, and apply the GAAR with a 50% gross-negligence penalty if reasonable efforts to document substance were absent

Director personal liability: Directors of a shell company can be liable for the full amount of unremitted Part XIII tax and for any false statements made in T106 filings; the court may impose a director-level fine and a prohibition on approving banking transactions in cases of conspiracy with the offshore parent
AML / Anti-Money Laundering

Statutory sanction: Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act penalties: administrative monetary penalty up to C$500,000 for corporations, or prosecution on indictment with fines up to C$1,000,000 and imprisonment of staff involved up to 2 years; CRA can later use revenue inconsistencies as a net-worth audit trigger

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who knowingly fail to implement AML policies or who direct employees to avoid reporting are personally liable for the corporate penalty; FINTRAC can impose a travel ban preventing departure from Canada if funds are suspected to be proceeds of crime
Transfer Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: Section 247 of the Income Tax Act permits CRA to adjust profits to arm's-length levels, apply notional interest on understated tax, and impose a 10% penalty on the aggregate of the transfer pricing adjustment where documentation was not prepared contemporaneously and reasonable efforts to comply are absent; if the case escalates to wilful misrepresentation, the penalty increases to 50%

Director personal liability: Directors who sign the T2 return and knowingly approve false transfer prices can be assessed under section 239 as parties to tax evasion; personal fraud claims from shareholders or creditors may pierce the corporate veil, and directors could face extradition proceedings from treaty partners for intentional financial misconduct
DTA Abuse / Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: The exclusion from OECD BEPS Action 6 surplus treaty benefits (unless the principal purpose test is satisfied) and Canada's GAAR deny the treaty rate, resulting in a 25% Part XIII reassessment on the entire cross-border payment; CRA may access a non-resident person under section 245.5 and issue a notice of determination with a penalty of up to C$25,000

Director personal liability: Directors of a Canadian entity that signed the conduit arrangement are jointly and severally liable for the unpaid Part XIII withholding; a provincial court may declare the director personally liable under fraudulent preference legislation and impose an accounting order against personal bank accounts
