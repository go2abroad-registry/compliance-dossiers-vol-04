Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital or escrow account is required for an Ontario limited partnership. Capital contributions are fixed by the Limited Partnership Agreement; paid-in amounts are credited to the limited partner's capital account, and failure to fully contribute exposes the limited partner to creditor claims under s. 13 of the Act. For banking or immigration evidence purposes, contributors typically wire at least CA$1; no escrow mandate applies.

Reg 851/90 (Industrial Establishments): machine guarding, lockout/tagout, ventilation and exposure control plan, WHMIS 2015 SDS library, and a WSIB employer account in good standing as a precondition to payroll operations.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); principal online portal is Business Registration Online (BRO) at canada.ca, supplemented by CRA My Business Account (MyBA) for ongoing GST/HST and BN administration. Ontario Ministry of Finance administers Ontario-only taxes but does not issue federal GST/HST registrations.

E-government filing portal

1. Ensure the Ontario limited partnership is duly registered under the Ontario Limited Partnerships Act and hold a valid Ontario business identification number/registration document. 2. Apply to the CRA for a Business Number (BN) using Business Registration Online or paper Form RC1, selecting the 'GST/HST account' option and indicating non-resident partner status where applicable. 3. If no place of business exists in Canada, appoint and identify an agent for service of process acceptable to the CRA. 5. Use this BN-RT account for all GST/HST registrations, returns, adjustments, and election filings; maintain updated partner information through CRA's business account or Form RC1 amendments.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada has no general check-the-box election equivalent to the U.S. regime. An Ontario limited partnership is automatically fiscally transparent under the Income Tax Act. Available statutory elections affecting cross-border LPs include: (i) ITA s. 216 election allowing non-resident partners to file Canadian returns and be taxed on a net basis on Canadian real-property rental income; (ii) treaty-based Part XIII withholding relief via CRA forms such as NR301/NR302; (iii) partnership fiscal-period elections under ITA s. 249.1; and (iv) Excise Tax Act elections enabling a non-resident supplier to register as a simplified 'non-resident registrant' in appropriate circumstances. Hybrid mismatch rules in ITA ss. 18.15–18.24 may override the default pass-through result in cross-border mismatched payment arrangements.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in gross taxable supplies made in Canada during a single calendar quarter or cumulative over the four preceding consecutive calendar quarters; non-resident suppliers carrying on business in Canada are subject to the same threshold, and voluntary registration is available below threshold to claim input tax credits.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply of tangible personal property, including designer blind-box toys, is zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act when the supplier exports the goods from Canada as part of the supply, or delivers them to a common carrier or customs broker for export, and retains proof that the goods were in fact exported within a reasonable time, generally within 60 days of shipment. The supplier must have no reason to believe the goods will be diverted to the Canadian market. Services supplied to a non-resident, such as product design, licensing, or royalty arrangements, may be zero-rated where the service is performed outside Canada or the benefit is not consumed in Canada. Although Canada does not impose a statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline, CRA practice treats 180 days as the customary outer evidentiary window for correlating export documentation with foreign currency receipts; later remittance does not itself invalidate zero-rating where independent proof of export exists.

  • CBSA Export Declaration (Form B13A) or Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) export transaction record
  • Commercial invoice and comprehensive packing list
  • Bill of lading or airway bill naming the Canadian supplier and the foreign consignee/destination
  • Proof of actual export issued by the carrier or customs broker, including highway manifests, terminal receipts, or border release documentation where applicable
  • Foreign exchange/bank remittance confirmation showing proceeds received from the non-resident purchaser in the invoiced currency

Canada does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction controls, or digital stamping. There is no statutory API-based transaction clearance requirement. Suppliers must issue and retain invoices containing the prescribed information under section 221.1 of the Excise Tax Act and the Input Tax Credit Information Regulations. Returns and payments are filed electronically through GST/HST NETFILE and CRA My Business Account; electronic books and records must be retained in an accessible format. Voluntary EDI or PEPPOL e-invoicing may be used but does not displace CRA's record-keeping requirements.

CRA may reassess export zero-rating claims within four years of the filing date, and without limitation where misrepresentation occurs through neglect, carelessness, or willful default. If proof of export is inadequate or internally inconsistent, the supply is recharacterized as taxable at the Ontario HST rate of 13%, with arrears interest and applicable penalties. Civil penalties under the Excise Tax Act include a false-statement penalty equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 25% of the tax understated (ETA s. 280(1)) and a 50% gross-negligence penalty (ETA s. 285.1). Criminal prosecution under ETA s. 282 for willful evasion or fraudulent export arrangements can result in fines of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to two years. CRA may also claw back previously claimed input tax credits or refunds, seize records, and require future export claims to be supported by enhanced due diligence and documented shipment evidence.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible with no current-account or capital-account exchange controls. Cross-border capital contributions, operating remittances, loan repayments, and partnership distributions may be freely routed in CAD or foreign currency without Bank of Canada approval. Profit remittance is not structurally hindered; however, a non-resident limited partner carrying on business in Canada through the Ontario limited partnership may be subject to Canadian income tax under Part I of the Income Tax Act, with treaty relief available under the applicable tax treaty, and all cross-border electronic transfers remain subject to FINTRAC reporting and record-keeping obligations.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender or mandatory conversion of export proceeds. Exporters may retain foreign-currency receipts in resident foreign-currency bank accounts or convert them at will. There is no prescribed timeline requiring receipt into Canada, although all receipts settled by electronic funds transfer are subject to FINTRAC reporting thresholds and internal bank compliance. Currency transaction reports are mandatory for cash transactions above CAD 10,000 in a single or linked day; e-commerce trade settlements are trackable through normal bank audit trails and should be supported by export invoices, shipping logs, and customs records.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), supported by the Minister of Finance for the open-currency framework and by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing administration under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). Canada has no separate foreign-exchange administration; the Currency Act and Bank Act delegate currency and banking supervision respectively.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: LOW as the sole treasury banking rail; MEDIUM-to-HIGH as an ancillary collection and payment facilitator. Offshore digital e-money institutions and foreign EMIs do not hold Canadian banking licences and cannot accept insured Canadian deposits. They may legally provide cross-border payment initiation through locally registered Money Service Businesses or partnerships with Schedule I chartered banks. Payment service providers, including Shopify Payments and Stripe, are suitable as front-end collection channels for e-commerce sales but must settle into a Canadian bank operating account under the Bank Act and the Retail Payment Activities Act.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital from non-resident limited partners should be routed by authenticated SWIFT MT103 wire to the Ontario LP's capital escrow operating account at the chosen Schedule I bank. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 aggregate non-arm's length cross-border transactions triggers mandatory Form T106 filing and contemporaneous TP documentation expectations; CBCR (Country-by-Country Report) required for MNE groups with consolidated group revenue of at least C$750,000,000.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain an Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) filing regime equivalent to the UAE. However, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA s.245) and common law substance-over-form principles apply: transactions lacking a real business purpose, economic substance, or that are solely designed to obtain treaty or deduction benefits will be re-characterized. An Ontario-based designer blind-box toy LP that maintains physical premises, employees in manufacturing/assembly, storage inventory, order fulfillment, and substantive decision-making has adequate substance.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Suppression of Sales

Statutory sanction: Reassessment with gross negligence penalties under ITA s.163(2) equal to 50% of understated tax; criminal conviction under ITA s.239: fines up to 200% of evaded tax and imprisonment up to 5 years; CRA may seize assets under s.462.33.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners who directed or acquiesced to suppression are personally jointly and severally liable for the corporate/partnership tax debt under ITA s.160; criminal directors face imprisonment, travel bans, and civil restitution orders.
Shell Company / Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty benefits, GAAR re-characterization of income as directly received by the ultimate beneficial owner, retroactive Part XIII withholding assessments (25% on gross amounts) plus interest and penalties up to 50%; cancellation or revocation of extra-provincial registration in Ontario.

Director personal liability: Limited partners lose liability protection if they participate in control of the LP; general partners face unlimited personal liability for the LP's unpaid withholding tax, civil restitution, and potential criminal exposure for fraudulent treaty benefit claims.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust price under ITA s.247(2); if contemporaneous documentation is absent, s.163(3) transfer-pricing penalty applies at 10% of the net adjustment amount; T106 penalties may reach CAD 24,000 per failure; foreign affiliate surplus rules may recharacterize deferred income.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for T106 penalties and remittances if they deliberately participated in mispricing; the CRA can certify directors' liability under the Federal Debt Collection Procedures, including seizure of bank accounts and passport retention through Federal Court travel ban applications.
Money Laundering / FINTRAC Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1,000,000 for corporations and CAD 100,000 for individuals; criminal money laundering under Criminal Code s.462.31: imprisonment up to 10 years; proceeds-of-crime confiscation via Ontario civil forfeiture.

Director personal liability: Directors may be prosecuted for directing or permitting laundering, incurring penal liability, personal travel bans, forfeiture of personal assets used to facilitate the offense, and lifetime disqualification from serving as director.
Non-Remittance of Payroll / GST-HST / Part XIII Withholdings

Statutory sanction: 10% failure-to-remit penalty plus daily interest (current prescribed rates, now 9% per annum compounded daily); corporate/partnership failure to file T5013 triggers CAD 25/day penalties; CRA director liability certificates under ITA s.227.1; freezes on the LP's bank accounts.

Director personal liability: Directors and partners who managed or oversaw the LP are jointly and severally liable for the unremitted withholdings, without any corporate veil; CRA may pursue personal guaranteed director liability, apply for an order prohibiting travel, and if funds transferred abroad, seek asset recovery via tax treaties.
