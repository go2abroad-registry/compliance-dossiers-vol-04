Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC), with the My Business Account digital portal at https://www.canada.ca/my-cra-business-account. Ontario corporate income tax is administered by the CRA under the federal-provincial Tax Collection Agreement, with the Ontario Ministry of Finance as the provincial statute owner. Customs and export controls are administered by the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) through the CBSA Assessment and Revenue Management (CARM) and Canadian Export Reporting System (CESR) platforms.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Obtain a federal or Ontario Business Number (BN) from the CRA by submitting Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) or using Business Registration Online (BRO), attaching the articles of incorporation, registered office proof, and a non-resident attestation where applicable. Step 2: In the same registration, activate program accounts: RT (federal and Ontario corporate income tax), RC (GST/HST under section 240 of the Excise Tax Act), RM (import/export account registered with CBSA/CARM for customs declarations), and RP (payroll if staff are hired in Ontario). Step 3: Confirm Ontario Ministry of Finance registration is satisfied through the RT account, because CRA collects Ontario corporate tax under the Tax Collection Agreement; verify whether Ontario Employer Health Tax applies where payroll exceeds the C$1,000,000 exemption threshold. Step 4: For export operations, obtain an exporter profile in the CBSA CESR portal in order to file electronic B13A export declarations. Step 5: Where non-resident shareholders or intercompany cross-border payments exist, file the applicable information returns (e.g., Form T106A), and preserve treaty documentation (e.g., Form NR301) to support reduced statutory withholding rates.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation. A corporation incorporated under the Canada Business Corporations Act or the Ontario Business Corporations Act is deemed resident in Canada under subsection 250(4)(a) of the Income Tax Act, so it is taxable on worldwide income at both the federal level (15% general corporate rate; reduced 9% rate on small business income up to C$500,000 only if the Canadian-controlled private corporation test and the commercial activity rules are met) and the Ontario level (10% general corporate rate). Canada has no check-the-box pass-through regime; the entity is not a disregarded entity and cannot flow losses to shareholders. Territorial scope: for resident Canadian corporations the scope is worldwide; for non-resident counterparties the Canadian PE threshold applies under applicable tax treaties. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation. A corporation incorporated under the Canada Business Corporations Act or the Ontario Business Corporations Act is deemed resident in Canada under subsection 250(4)(a) of the Income Tax Act, so it is taxable on worldwide income at both the federal level (15% general corporate rate; reduced 9% rate on small business income up to C$500,000 only if the Canadian-controlled private corporation test and the commercial activity rules are met) and the Ontario level (10% general corporate rate). Canada has no check-the-box pass-through regime; the entity is not a disregarded entity and cannot flow losses to shareholders. Territorial scope: for resident Canadian corporations the scope is worldwide; for non-resident counterparties the Canadian PE threshold applies under applicable tax treaties. No tax-exempt zone status is available for an Ontario wholesale distribution activity.

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a US-style check-the-box election; foreign fiscally transparent entities are nevertheless assessed under CRA policy and relevant treaty articles (e.g., US LLC transparency). Available Canadian statutory elections include: Income Tax Act section 85 rollover (tax-deferred transfer of eligible property to the corporation), section 256(9) associated-corporation elections, sections 216 and 219 elections for non-residents (rental income and branch tax treaty relief), and Excise Tax Act section 156 election (zero-rating of non-commercial supplies between closely related registrants), section 167 election (deemed supply on closely related transfers), and section 179 drop-shipment attestation for goods delivered to a registered recipient that subsequently exports them. Each election requires a signed agreement or CRA prescribed form filed by the due date, and the burden of proof remains on the taxpayer.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

C$30,000 - a person is a small supplier and is not required to register where the aggregate value of taxable supplies made in Canada during the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters and the current quarter does not exceed C$30,000; for a non-resident person without a permanent establishment in Canada, the same threshold applies based on taxable supplies made in Canada. Registration becomes mandatory once the C$30,000 threshold is exceeded, and a registered supplier must charge and collect HST on all taxable supplies except zero-rated exports.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating eligibility for diagnostic ELISA reagents flows from Schedule VI, Part V, section 7 of the Excise Tax Act: a supply of goods is zero-rated where the supplier transfers possession, and the goods are delivered outside Canada by the supplier or at the supplier's direction. For domestic sales to a registered Canadian recipient who will itself export the goods, section 179 (drop-shipment) zero-rates the supply if the recipient is a registrant, the goods are not delivered in Canada for consumption, and the exporter obtains the prescribed documentation and, where necessary, a signed attestation. Separately invoiced technical and assay-validation services supplied to non-resident laboratories are zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V, section 20 where the recipient is a non-resident, the service is not performed in relation to real property located in Canada, and the performance is not physically rendered in Canada. The 0% rate is claimed on line 103 of the GST/HST return, and input tax credits on related Canadian purchases remain fully recoverable; exporters with refund positions may file monthly or quarterly and receive interim refunds.

  • Commercial Invoice and Packing List describing the assay reagents, units, lot numbers, destination, total value, and Incoterms

Canada has no real-time fiscal clearance, no digital stamping, and no government API pre-authorization mandate for commercial invoices. CRA accepts paper, PDF, XML, and EDI invoices provided the full tax information set under section 221(1) of the Excise Tax Act is present, including the supplier's Business Number, date, description, quantity, unit price, and HST rate. However, trade documentation is electronically controlled: exports of goods valued above C$2,000 must be reported through the CBSA CESR portal (B13A filing), including a G7 Report for US-bound cargo, and the CBSA CARM single-window system governs release and duty accounting for imports. Ontario introduces no additional e-invoicing clearance requirement.

CRA may reassess within the normal four-year limitation period, which becomes unlimited where misrepresentation, fraud, or negligence is shown, and it may retroactively convert zero-rated exports into taxable supplies at 13% HST with arrears interest. A gross negligence penalty may be imposed under section 285.1 of the Excise Tax Act equal to 50% of the net tax understated, and section 280 adds a 1% per-month late-filing penalty capped at 12%. Where the B13A, bill of lading, or destination proof is missing, contradictory, or after-dated, CRA applies the substance-over-form test, requiring proof of physical export at the time of supply and imposing an immediate clawback. Criminal sanctions under section 239 of the Income Tax Act and section 327 of the Excise Tax Act may reach fines of C$250,000 and imprisonment for up to five years in cases of document fabrication or evasion. Canada imposes no statutory FX repatriation deadline; the 180-day figure is applied here as a prudent internal benchmark, because CRA requires that export files demonstrate commercial substance and full payment within the period used for credit-granting and evidence retention. Mitigation: maintain a contemporaneous export evidence checklist, preserve records for six years, and ensure each B13A is reconciled to the corresponding invoice and remittance record.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full current-account and capital-account convertibility. Canada is an IMF Article VIII member and maintains no balance-of-payments or exchange-control restrictions on trade payments, dividends, royalties, loan interest, or repatriation of invested capital. The Canadian dollar floats freely. Profit remittance is taxed, not restricted: Part XIII withholding tax at the statutory 25% on non-resident dividends, interest, and royalties (treaty-reduced, for example to 5% on dividends paid to a US corporate shareholder owning 10% or more of the voting shares), and Part XIV branch tax at 25% (15% where a tax treaty so provides) on after-tax branch profits. Capital-account hurdles are limited to: (i) notification under the Investment Canada Act when a non-Canadian establishes an Ontario business or acquires control of a Canadian business; value-based review applies only to acquisitions of existing businesses above the indexed WTO-investor enterprise-value threshold (approximately C$1.08 billion for 2023 and C$1.33 billion for 2024) or in sensitive sectors; (ii) Canadian thin-capitalization rules (subsection 18(4) of the Income Tax Act) restricting interest deductions on non-arm's-length debt beyond a 1.5:1 debt-to-equity ratio; and (iii) FINTRAC reporting of every international electronic fund transfer of C$10,000 or more. No central-bank permit, currency license, or repatriation approval is required.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. There is no statutory timeline for surrender of export proceeds and no mandatory conversion ratio. CAD settlement is freely floating, and exporters may hold foreign currency (USD, EUR) in Canadian bank accounts indefinitely. The only obligations are indirect: (i) the bank must file an international electronic fund transfer report (IETR) with FINTRAC for each transaction of C$10,000 or more; (ii) the exporter must report foreign accounts and foreign property exceeding an aggregate cost of C$100,000 on Form T1135; (iii) foreign-exchange gains and losses on export receivables are recognized for Canadian income tax under subsection 39(1) of the Income Tax Act; and (iv) the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) may require export reporting (G39 or permit-based reporting) where the goods are controlled; IVD reagents are generally not on the Export Control List unless they meet chemical or biological dual-use criteria. No surrender, conversion, or retention ratio applies.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), Canada's central bank; the federal foreign-exchange policy authority is the Department of Finance Canada; prudential oversight of Schedule I banks is carried out by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); anti-money-laundering supervision is carried out by FINTRAC (Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada). Canada has no dedicated foreign-exchange-control agency; the wartime Foreign Exchange Control Act was repealed in 1951.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility: Medium for ancillary settlement flows, Low as the primary treasury or escrow account. Offshore digital EMIs licensed as e-money institutions (e.g., UK FCA EMI or EU PSD2 EMI) do not automatically obtain a right to provide payment services in Canada. A foreign payment service provider servicing Canadian clients must register with FINTRAC as a Money Services Business (MSB) or Foreign MSB and, for retail payment services, may be subject to the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) registration regime supervised by the Bank of Canada. Established cross-border platforms (Wise Business, Airwallex, Payoneer, OFX) are usable for low-value operational payments and marketplace collections because they typically operate through Canadian bank partners or FINTRAC-registered MSBs; however, they cannot issue commercial letters of credit, provide CAD settlement through Lynx or the Real-Time Rail, or hold statutory capital escrow funds for an Ontario business corporation, and they expose the treasury to sanction-screening and two-step settlement risk. Recommended policy: route at least 90% of corporate treasury flows through Tier-1 chartered banks and retain regulated EMIs only for niche micro-payments.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility: Medium for ancillary settlement flows, Low as the primary treasury or escrow account. Offshore digital EMIs licensed as e-money institutions (e.g., UK FCA EMI or EU PSD2 EMI) do not automatically obtain a right to provide payment services in Canada. A foreign payment service provider servicing Canadian clients must register with FINTRAC as a Money Services Business (MSB) or Foreign MSB and, for retail payment services, may be subject to the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) registration regime supervised by the Bank of Canada. Established cross-border platforms (Wise Business, Airwallex, Payoneer, OFX) are usable for low-value operational payments and marketplace collections because they typically operate through Canadian bank partners or FINTRAC-registered MSBs; however, they cannot issue commercial letters of credit, provide CAD settlement through Lynx or the Real-Time Rail, or hold statutory capital escrow funds for an Ontario business corporation, and they expose the treasury to sanction-screening and two-step settlement risk. Recommended policy: route at least 90% of corporate treasury flows through Tier-1 chartered banks and retain regulated EMIs only for niche micro-payments.
