Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital or escrow/trust account is required for a business corporation in Canada/Quebec (minimum_capital_requirement: false); shares may be issued for nominal consideration and capitalization is fixed by the directors. No escrow account is triggered for passive wholesale distribution of MLCCs and resistors; escrow or fiduciary trust rules would apply only if the corporation were to hold client funds as a securities, real-estate, or deposit-taking intermediary, which is not the case here.

Funding & unlock path: Occupational health and safety clearance under the Act respecting occupational health and safety, CQLR c S-2.1, including CNESST (Commission des normes, de l'équité, de la santé et de la sécurité du travail) employer registration before first hire. Fire safety certification is governed by Chapter VIII of the Quebec Building Code (Fire Safety Code) — inspection of egress routes, fire-resistance ratings for racked storage, sprinkler and standpipe requirements, and emergency lighting must be completed before occupancy. Structural load certification for warehouse racking systems must be signed by a qualified engineer under Chapter I of the Quebec Building Code. Workplace clearance includes written evacuation procedures, adequate ventilation and lighting for the storage hall, and a CNESST prevention program if five or more employees are assigned.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full convertibility on both current and capital account transactions. There is no exchange-control statute limiting cross-border fund flows; dividend distributions, branch remittances, liquidation proceeds, and intercompany payments are freely transferable in CAD or any foreign currency. No prior central-bank approval is required. The only material hurdles are Canadian-source dividend withholding tax under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (federal) and Quebec's corresponding withholding obligations, plus FATCA/CRS account reporting and FINTRAC large-transaction reporting thresholds of CAD 10,000. Capital-account treaty benefits may require a Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) application in form NR301 to reduce domestic Part XIII rates.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender period, mandatory conversion ratio, or repatriation timeline applies to export receipts in Canada. A Quebec business corporation may retain export proceeds indefinitely in CAD or foreign-currency accounts at any authorized financial institution. Obligations are limited to transfer-pricing documentation for cross-border intercompany balances under the Income Tax Act, and classic B.S. 14/ACI customs reporting for physical imports. For financing intercompany receivables or payables, no FOREX intervention consent is required; however, quarterly CRA form T106 reporting for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions may apply if the payment is made to a related non-resident.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as monetary authority; foreign exchange policy is administered jointly with the Department of Finance Canada under the Currency Act, with AML/CFT transaction oversight by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium. Canada does not maintain a licensed 'EMI' (electronic money institution) category; foreign-regulated digital EMIs may operate as money services businesses (MSBs) only after registration with FINTRAC if they solicit Canadian clients, and they face concentration risk when using sponsor-bank rails for CAD settlement. For a Quebec-held corporation in passive electronic inventory distribution, a top-tier Schedule I bank remains more robust for account stability, credit lines, and Swift-based trade flows. Fintech EMIs can serve as auxiliary disbursement utilities but are not viable as the primary treasury bank because of recurring compliance reviews, freezes under PCMLTFA, and lack of Quebec-based credit underwriting.

Mandatory Audit Threshold Mandatory Audit for All Entities

Turnover: Compulsory statutory audit is triggered when: (1) the corporation is a distributing or public-issuer entity; (2) the shareholders do not unanimously waive the auditor requirement; (3) the entity is a bank, trust, insurer or other regulated financial company; or (4) CRA, Revenu Québec or the applicable corporate statute demands audited financial statements. A non-distributing private corporation under the CBCA or the Quebec Business Corporations Act may dispense with the independent audit by unanimous shareholder consent, unless a shareholder objects or the corporation is a public issuer.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CRA administrative practice under IC 87-2R requires a Canadian entity to prepare a Local File documentation package when aggregate controlled cross-border transactions exceed CA$1,000,000, and a Master File when the entity's gross revenue exceeds CA$10,000,000 or the parent group is above the country-by-country reporting threshold of CA$750,000,000 consolidated revenue.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not apply separate Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) of the Dubai/offshore type; however, the Income Tax Act's General Anti-Avoidance Rule under section 245 can recharacterize transactions lacking real economic substance, and the transfer pricing rules under section 247 require that the contractual allocation of risk and functions be supported by actual business conduct. A passive electronics distributor with negligible physical presence, no operational substance, and circular intercompany invoicing may be treated as a sham for allocation of profit to a Canadian permanent establishment.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Willful Blindness on False Returns

Statutory sanction: Under section 239(1) of the Income Tax Act, and section 40 of the Quebec Taxation Act, on conviction the corporation faces fines of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for executive personnel up to 5 years; the corporation is also liable for full tax arrears, penalties, and compound interest.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, or senior company personnel who directed, assented to, or acquiesced in the filing of a false return are personally joint and severally liable for unpaid taxes under section 227.1 of the Income Tax Act. In aggravated fraud cases, personal asset seizure, travel bans, and custodial sentences apply.
Unreported Foreign Income and T1135 Omission

Statutory sanction: Penalty of CA$2,500 per instance for each failure, escalating to CA$25,000 for a deliberate omission; interest runs from the date the tax should have been paid. If the omission results in tax evasion, the penal consequences mirror those of tax evasion under section 239.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who retained direct or indirect control of the foreign bank account or signed the non-disclosing return may be personally assessed for the penalty under a gross-negligence theory. CRA may levy director assets and, in flagrant cases, seek extraordinary remedies including travel bans.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing / Sham Intercompany Contracts

Statutory sanction: CRA will re-determine the arm's-length price under section 247(2); the resulting adjustment is assessed with interest and, where documentation was not provided or reasonable effort was absent, a penalty of 10% of the amount of the adjustment (subject to the minimum thresholds under section 247(3)).

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly signed transfer-pricing false pricing statements or caused the sham intercompany structure may be treated as participants in a misrepresentation giving rise to joint and several liability for the corporate penalty; in egregious cases, criminal exposure under section 239 applies.
Dividend Stripping / Shell Company Veil Piercing

Statutory sanction: Sections 212.1 and 245 of the Income Tax Act empower recharacterization of such stripping schemes; the payer is assessed the full 25% withholding tax plus interest and penalties of up to 200% of the amount withheld if the transaction is deemed a benefit to a shareholder.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil may be pierced under section 227.1 to make directors and officers personally liable for unpaid Part XIII withholdings and penalties where they directed the capital distributions. Directors involved in the artificial scheme face civil restitution, travel restrictions, and possible imprisonment for fraud or conspiracy.
