Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: the Business Corporation (Inc./Corp.) is a separate taxpayer under Part I of the Income Tax Act, with no pass-through or transparency for shareholders; a corporation incorporated in Canada is deemed resident under s. 250(4) and taxed on worldwide income, subject to foreign tax credits; Ontario imposes a parallel provincial corporate income tax collected through the CRA under the Ontario Corporations Tax Act (provincial component now administered federally). The small business deduction applies only if the corporation is a Canadian-controlled private corporation with active business income, subject to the CAD 500,000 business limit and reduction by passive investment income.

Available election forms

No U.S.-style check-the-box or elective entity-classification regime exists under Canadian law; classification is statutory. Relevant elections and statutory mechanisms for this grocery retail group include: (i) the s. 156 Excise Tax Act election between closely related corporations to treat qualifying intercompany supplies as nil for GST/HST; (ii) the GST/HST Quick Method election available to small retailers for simplified net-tax remittance; (iii) the Excise Tax Act non-resident rebate provisions permitting recovery of GST/HST paid on imported goods and exhibition/trade-show property where applicable; (iv) Income Tax Act s. 111 loss-utilization rules permitting non-capital loss carry-back two years and carry-forward twenty years, with statutory agreements to allocate losses among associated corporations; (v) Income Tax Act ss. 256(1)-(3) elections to reallocate taxable income among associated corporations; and (vi) the mandatory hybrid mismatch rules in ss. 18.2, 18.3 and 163.1 of the Income Tax Act, which operate as anti-arbitrage provisions with only limited transitional grandfathering and cannot be overridden by a check-the-box election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — the GST/HST small-supplier threshold. Registration is mandatory once the person's worldwide taxable supplies (other than zero-rated exports and certain financial services) exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over the previous four consecutive calendar quarters. Until the threshold is exceeded, the operator is a 'small supplier' and may elect to register voluntarily. Because a physical supermarket with daily point-of-sale sales will exceed CAD 30,000 on opening day, GST/HST registration must be effective before the first taxable supply is made. Non-resident suppliers selling into Canada are subject to the same threshold for GST/HST purposes.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act, supplies of goods exported from Canada are zero-rated at 0% GST/HST where the supplier sells the goods and exports them 'as soon after the sale as is reasonable in the circumstances', retaining satisfactory evidence of export to the CRA standard set out in GST/HST Memorandum 17-15. For this Asian supermarket grocery hub, eligible zero-rating channels include cross-border e-commerce fulfillment to non-resident customers, direct wholesale export sales to foreign buyers, and supplies to diplomatic missions with proper certification. If the goods are not physically removed from Canada, the supply is taxable at 13% HST in Ontario and any zero-rating claim is reversed. Services performed on tangible personal property exported from Canada for a non-resident (s. 7, Part V, Schedule VI) may also be zero-rated. By contrast, zero-rating of 'basic groceries' sold domestically in the store (Part III, Schedule VI) is an intrinsic food zero-rating that requires no export substantiation and does not involve blocked ITC recovery.

Canada does not currently operate a real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control (CTC), digital stamping, or government-mandated API clearance regime at the federal or Ontario level. GST/HST invoices must contain the prescribed information under Regulation 238 of the Excise Tax Act, including the supplier's BN/RT registration number, date of supply, description, and tax amount charged. Retail point-of-sale receipts must disclose the HST component. Quebec has enacted a phased mandatory B2B e-invoicing and e-reporting mandate beginning in 2025-2027, which may affect invoices transmitted to Quebec customers or head offices; Ontario has announced alignment with the Pan-Canadian e-invoice framework, but no binding statutory API clearance duty is currently in force for Ontario HST filers.

The CRA and the Canada Border Services Agency jointly audit export zero-rating and input tax credit claims. Export zero-rating claims are subject to reassessment within the normal four-year limitation period, which is unlimited where the filing involves misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, or willful default; the CRA may claw back input tax credits under s. 296(2.1) of the Excise Tax Act and recompute 13% Ontario HST on any supply not validly exported. Canada does not impose a statutory 180-day FX repatriation deadline; the 180-day window in this framework is a due-diligence benchmark, and unrepatriated export proceeds may be examined as unremitted income under s. 9 of the Income Tax Act or as a shareholder benefit under ss. 15 and 246. Civil penalties include ETA s. 280 (false statements: the greater of CAD 250 and 10% of the tax evaded) and ETA s. 281 (gross negligence: the greater of CAD 500 and 25% of the tax), plus interest under s. 280.1. Criminal tax evasion is punishable under ETA s. 327 by fines of up to twice the tax evaded and/or imprisonment up to five years. The CBSA may additionally issue Administrative Monetary Penalty System (AMPS) penalties of up to CAD 25,000 per false export declaration and may seize goods under the Customs Act and the Export and Import Permits Act.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is freely convertible on both current and capital accounts, and Canada maintains no general exchange-control approval regime. Capital injections, dividend distributions, intercompany loan repayments, royalty remittances, and reinvested earnings may flow without central-bank licensing. Practical statutory frictions are limited to: (a) FINTRAC reporting of every cross-border electronic funds transfer of CAD 10,000 or more within a 24-hour period; (b) CBSA declaration of physical import/export of currency or bearer monetary instruments having a value of CAD 10,000 or more; (c) Part XIII withholding tax at the domestic rate of 25% (treaty-reduced where a tax treaty applies) imposed on dividends, interest, royalties, and certain management fees paid to non-residents; (d) source-of-funds and beneficial-ownership verification demanded by the account-holding bank; and (e) substantial Canadian-resident reporting of foreign property and transactions through T1135 and T106 filings where applicable. Foreign-currency deposits held at Canadian bank branches are not CDIC-insured because CDIC protection is limited to Canadian-dollar eligible deposits. A functional-currency tax election under subsection 261(1) of the Income Tax Act (Canada) is available for USD reporting, but the entity may maintain only one functional currency for income-tax purposes and must keep adequate books, records, and reconciliations.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no mandatory surrender requirements, no statutory conversion ratios, and no repatriation timelines on export proceeds. A Canadian exporter may lawfully retain foreign-currency receipts indefinitely in an authorized foreign-currency clearing account or convert them at its own commercial discretion. The residual compliance obligations are: (a) report each export shipment with a value above CAD 2,000 to the CBSA through the Canadian Export Reporting framework (B13A declaration or the CBSA Export Reporting tool) using the exporter's Business Number and the applicable HS code; (b) value export transactions in CAD or in a stated foreign currency using the Bank of Canada daily exchange rate for GST/HST and income-tax accounting; (c) allow FINTRAC monitoring of inbound settlement wires of CAD 10,000 or more; and (d) ensure that export proceeds flow into the corporately-owned Canadian bank account rather than an offshore nominee account, preserving transfer-pricing integrity and tax transparency. No Bank of Canada authorization is required for export-financing arrangements, supplier advance payments, or retained earnings held in USD.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) is the central monetary authority; prudential supervision of FX positions, liquidity, and bank solvency is exercised by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); cross-border physical currency movement reporting is administered by the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA); and cross-border electronic funds transfer monitoring is enforced by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) pursuant to the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. The Minister of Finance retains residual policy authority under the Currency Act, but Canada operates freely without a dedicated foreign-exchange control authority.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Feasibility for ancillary use is Medium, with material legal constraints. Wise Canada, Payoneer, OFX, and similar online cross-border payment firms must be registered as Money Services Businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC and must also register with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) where they provide retail payment services; the RPAA registration and supervision regime is being phased in over 2024-2025. An offshore EMI that lacks Canadian MSB/RPAA registration cannot lawfully provide Canadian payment services to this Ontario corporate entity. EMIs may be used as supplementary disbursement rails for small vendor payments (12-month rolling exposure under CAD 50,000 per transaction) but cannot: provide CDIC-insured deposits, issue Canadian transit-number accounts enabling Interac Direct Payment or ACSS batch clearing, open documentary letter-of-credit facilities, or replace the principal Schedule I operating account. The legal and treasury architecture must therefore clear all material trade and payroll flows through a domestic Canadian bank account, with EMIs confined to nano-import payouts and emergency cross-border remittances.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility for ancillary use is Medium, with material legal constraints. Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined general corporate rate (15% federal basic rate after general rate reduction plus 11.5% Ontario general corporate income tax rate); reduced to 12.2% combined for qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) active business income up to CAD 500,000.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder is 25% of the gross dividend, unless reduced by an applicable tax treaty. Under the Canada-US tax convention, the rate is generally 5% for a US corporation that beneficially owns at least 10% of the voting stock, and 15% for all other dividend recipients; other treaty jurisdictions may provide rates of 10%, 15%, or 25% by bilateral agreement.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Form T106 (Information Return for Non-Arm's Length Transactions with Non-Residents) must be filed and contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation should be prepared where the aggregate fair market value of all transactions with a non-resident related entity in a taxation year exceeds CAD 250,000. CRA may require a complete Local File under transfer pricing audit where operational adjustments exceed the greater of 10% of gross revenue or CAD 5,000,000; however, no formal statutory Local File / Master File threshold exists in Canada beyond the T106 reporting threshold. Country-by-Country (CbC) reporting is mandatory for Canadian ultimate parent corporations or surrogate parents of MNE groups with consolidated group revenue of EUR 750 million or more (approximately CAD 1.1 billion).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not impose a stand-alone Economic Substance Regulation akin to the UAE or certain other jurisdictions; instead, economic substance is enforced indirectly through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (subsection 245(2)), which disallows a tax benefit from an avoidance transaction where the transaction misuses or abuses the provisions of the Income Tax Act. Transfer pricing recharacterization under subsection 247(2) permits CRA to reconstruct or disregard artificial transactions lacking economic substance and replace them with arm's length terms. Treaty shopping restrictions under the OECD Multilateral Instrument as ratified by Canada, including the principal purpose test, further deny treaty withholding benefits to entities that lack business purpose and substance.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Returns

Statutory sanction: Upon conviction under subsection 239(1) of the Income Tax Act: a fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, plus imprisonment for a term not exceeding five years, or both. CRA may additionally assess civil penalties under subsection 163(1) for failure to report income and subsection 163(2) for knowing false statements, each equal to 50% of the understated tax.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly authorized or participated in the evasion are subject to director's liability assessments for unremitted source deductions and GST/HST under subsection 227.1(1); where the corporate veil is used as a concealment device or a fraudulent sham, CRA may pierce the veil and assess directors personally. Criminal bail conditions may include surrender of passports and travel bans; a final criminal conviction carries the risk of personal incarceration.
Transfer Pricing Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Subsection 247(3) authorizes a transfer pricing penalty equal to 10% of the net adjustment amount where the adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 and 10% of gross revenue; the penalty applies in addition to the tax assessment and interest. The taxpayer may avoid the penalty only if contemporaneous documentation was prepared and provided to CRA within a reasonable period.

Director personal liability: Directors who approve deliberate mispricing or who cause false intercompany books may be exposed to the 10% penalty and related interest if CRA treats the conduct as gross negligence; where fraud is established, the corporate veil may be pierced, and directors may be personally assessed for the diverted funds under common law breach of fiduciary duty.
Shell Company and Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: Under the principal purpose test in Canada's treaty application and the General Anti-Avoidance Rule, the treaty benefit is denied; the full 25% Part XIII withholding tax is applied, and GAAR penalty under subsection 245(6) of 50% of the tax benefit may be imposed if the transaction was not fully disclosed on a prescribed form. The CRA may also impose gross negligence penalties under subsection 163(2).

Director personal liability: Directors who cause the Canadian corporation to rely on a sham foreign entity can be held jointly and severally liable for the resulting withholding tax deficiency if the corporation is insolvent; where the foreign shell is a facade designed to conceal beneficial ownership and evade reporting, CRA can disregard the vertical corporate structure and assess the individuals who act as directing minds, with potential criminal exposure for conspiracy to defraud the Crown.
Failure to Remit Payroll Withholdings and GST/HST

Statutory sanction: The corporation is liable for the unremitted amounts plus prescribed interest; a penalty of 10% of the unremitted GST/HST may be imposed under subsection 280(1) of the Excise Tax Act, and payroll failure may trigger a penalty of 10% of the unremitted source deductions under subsection 227(9) of the ITA. Willful non-remittance may be prosecuted as tax evasion leading to a fine and imprisonment up to five years.

Director personal liability: Under subsection 227.1(1) and subsection 323(1) of the Excise Tax Act, directors are jointly and severally liable for unremitted GST/HST and payroll deductions unless they exercised the degree of care, diligence, and skill of a reasonably prudent person. CRA may issue a director's liability certificate, file liens in personal property registries, freeze personal bank accounts, garnish directors' wages, and initiate civil recovery; directors who abscond may be subject to travel bans as bail conditions during prosecution and to seizure of personal assets under the Crown's enforcement procedures.
