Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency / Agence du revenu du Canada (CRA), Business Registration Online (BRO) portal at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/registering-your-business/register.html; Ontario Ministry of Finance for Ontario corporate tax and provincial HST administration (collected by CRA); My Business Account portal for program account management.

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity establishes its tax identity by obtaining a 9-digit Business Number (BN) with program accounts: RT (GST/HST), RC (corporate income tax), RP (payroll), and RD (import/export). Step 1: submit Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) or register online via BRO using the non-resident pathway. Step 2: CRA validates the entity's legal name, articles of incorporation (federal/or provincial), registered office, and foreign address; a non-resident corporation without a Canadian registered office may appoint an authorized representative and provide a Canadian contact address for service. Step 3: open the RT account specifically for the ghost kitchen's GST/HST collection on Ontario food-delivery sales; the RC account is opened for federal Part I tax and Ontario provincial corporate income tax (Ontario taxes its 'allocated share' via the CRA-administered Ontario Corporate Income Tax return, T2COJ). Step 4: register for a customs account (RM) if any cross-border goods or equipment are imported. Timeline is typically 5-10 business days; retroactive effective dates are available. Non-resident corporations are also required to file an annual T2 return once a BN is issued, regardless of taxable income.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no U.S.-style check-the-box election; entity classification follows the Income Tax Act, Canadian corporate law, and jurisprudential tests (e.g., trust-vs-corporation indicia). Key elections available to an Ontario business corporation: (i) Income Tax Act s.85 rollover election for tax-deferred transfer of property on incorporation; (ii) s.89(11) capital dividend account election; (iii) s.216 election for non-resident rental income on a net basis; (iv) s.217 election for non-resident remuneration; (v) Excise Tax Act s.172.1 Quick Method election for GST/HST (available to registrants with worldwide taxable supplies not exceeding CAD 400,000); (vi) ETA s.155 annual GST/HST filing election for non-resident registrants with no Canadian permanent establishment; (vii) ETA s.156 offset election for closely related corporations; (viii) Ontario's associated-corporations allocation election under the Corporations Tax Act regulations. Hybrid mismatch rules (ITA ss.18.2, 18.3, 21.2) are mandatory anti-avoidance provisions, not elective.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies (small supplier threshold under ETA s.123/240); mandatory GST/HST registration once net worldwide taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 or in any calendar quarter when the threshold is exceeded. A ghost kitchen in Ontario selling prepared meals through UberEats or DoorDash is unquestionably engaged in a commercial activity in Canada and must register for the RT (GST/HST) account upon exceeding CAD 30,000 in world-wide gross revenues from taxable supplies; voluntary registration is available below the threshold. Non-resident digital suppliers of remote services and intangibles are subject to mandatory simplified registration without a small-supplier exemption under the 2021 digital economy rules, but in-store and delivery food services in Ontario are supplied by the Canadian entity and follow the standard threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available under Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act: (i) goods exported from Canada where proof of export in a prescribed form is obtained; (ii) services supplied to a non-resident person where the service is performed wholly outside Canada and is not supplied to a related resident-entity risk; and (iii) certain international services (e.g., consulting, delivery logistics) rendered to non-residents and not consumed in Canada. For a ghost kitchen operating ISIC 5610 / NAICS 722513, prepared-meal sales delivered and consumed in Ontario are taxable at 13% HST and cannot be zero-rated; zero-rating applies only to genuinely exported services or goods leaving Canada (e.g., franchise-consulting deliverables to a US affiliate or proprietary recipes licensed to a foreign virtual brand operator). The zero-rated supply must be separately invoiced and the supplier must maintain evidence of the non-resident status of the recipient and the extra-Canadian consumption of the service.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A or electronic equivalent in the Canadian Export Reporting System - CES)
  • Bill of Lading or Airway Bill evidencing physical departure of exported goods
  • Foreign Exchange Inward Remittance Confirmation (bank wire/receipt from the non-resident buyer) or common-law equivalent such as payment order, SWIFT MT103, or widely accepted payment processing record
  • Commercial invoice and packing list in the name of the Canadian exporter
  • Delivery confirmation or contract evidencing service performed outside Canada (for zero-rated services)

No statutory real-time fiscal clearance, pre-approval, or digital stamping regime is in force in Canada as of 2025. CRA accepts invoices in paper, PDF, EDI, and structured JSON/XML formats. Mandatory electronic filing applies to GST/HST returns for registrants with total taxable annual revenues over CAD 1 million (GST/HST NETFILE or WEBFILE). The Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Standard is being adopted voluntarily on the PEPPOL network, with the federal government requiring PEPPOL-based e-invoicing for central government suppliers in phases beginning FY2025-26. For ghost kitchens, the delivery platforms (UberEats Marketplace, DoorDash Drive) issue transactional documents; the operator must retain platform settlement reports, merchant payout statements, and its own sales summaries to substantiate the 13% HST remitted on taxable restaurant food sales and any zero-rated supplies of basic groceries if sold un-prepared.

CRA audit programs target aggressive zero-rating and input tax credit claims. The normal reassessment period is four years from the date of the return (ETA s.298); this limitation is unlimited where misrepresentation, neglect, or fraud is established. Penalties under ETA s.280.1 (failure to file), s.285 (false statements), and gross-negligence penalties of 25% of the tax sought to be evaded apply; interest accrues at the prescribed rate. Criminal prosecution under ETA s.327 carries fines of up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment of up to five years. Fraudulent export zero-rating claims expose the taxpayer to: denial of refunds, clawback of the full zero-rating previously applied, revocation of the GST/HST registration, and penalty assessments jointly against the corporation and, in cases of wilful participation, directors and officers. Ontario provincial measures under the Ontario Taxpayer Protection Act and CRA's Offshore Compliance Division increase scrutiny on cross-border fund flows.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current-account and capital-account convertibility under IMF Article VIII; there is no exchange control legislation, no foreign investment approval for typical operating capital, and no central bank authorization for cross-border remittances. Non-resident shareholders may receive dividends, loan repayments, and liquidation proceeds without Bank of Canada approval; the principal hurdles are (i) Part XIII withholding tax on dividends, interest, and royalties (statutory 25%, typically reduced to 5%–15% under the Canada–US tax treaty or other applicable treaties), (ii) Income Tax Act Section 116 clearance certificates for eventual share dispositions, and (iii) routine FINTRAC reporting of electronic funds transfers exceeding CAD 10,000, which applies to the reporting financial institution, not the corporate client. The Canadian dollar is a freely floating currency with no convertibility bands or surrender requirements.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no requirement to repatriate or surrender export proceeds. A ghost kitchen's cross-border receipts (e.g., platform payouts from the United States via UberEats or DoorDash) may lawfully remain in foreign currency or be converted at commercial rates. For financial reporting under IFRS/ASPE, a CAD functional-currency entity will remeasure foreign-currency receivables at each reporting date and recognize FX gains or losses in taxable income under Income Tax Act subsection 39(2). Treasury policy should nonetheless establish a same-day or T+1 conversion cadence to minimize balance-sheet exposure.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada). The Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) supervises all federally chartered banks and authorized foreign bank branches. The Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) administers the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) for AML/CFT compliance. As of November 16, 2024, payment service providers must register with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Medium (supplementary rail) / Low (sole primary treasury bank). Offshore digital EMIs (Wise Platform, Payoneer, Airwallex, Stripe Treasury, Intergiro) are legally permissible as collection and disbursement conduits for platform payouts, but cannot substitute for a Schedule I chartered bank. Legal constraints: (i) a foreign MSB operating in Canada must register with FINTRAC as a Money Services Business, appoint a designated compliance officer, and maintain a Canadian program; approved as a registered ‘foreign MSB’ only if it has a physical place of business in Canada; (ii) under the Retail Payment Activities Act, payment service providers must be registered with the Bank of Canada before performing payment functions (end-to-end clearing, settlement, or fund transfer); (iii) offshore EMIs do not have access to Lynx (Canada's RTGS system) and therefore cannot settle CAD large-value payments natively; (iv) platform agreements with UberEats and DoorDash typically require settlement to a bank account in the payout jurisdiction, and an EMI lacking a Canadian banking partner may trigger sponsor-bank rejection. Recommended structure: use an EMI for cross-border sub-ledger FX conversion and contractor disbursements, while all regulatory capital, tax payments, payroll, and platform settlements flow through a Tier-1 Schedule I bank account.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant routing for a foreign shareholder capital contribution or subordinated loan: (1) originating bank sends an SWIFT MT103 designating the beneficiary as the Ontario corporation; (2) Field 70 (Remittance Information) must state clearly ‘SHARE CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION' or 'SUBORDINATED LOAN,’ plus the shareholder resolution or loan agreement reference; (3) the beneficiary Schedule I bank receives the funds via its U.S. or London correspondent and credits the CAD operating account settled through Lynx (Canada's RTGS system); (4) pre-arrival compliance requires submission of the executed board resolution, subscription agreement (if equity), loan agreement (if debt), and a source-of-funds attestation; (5) FINTRAC EFT reporting obligations of the receiving bank are triggered at CAD 10,000, and the bank will request the relevant beneficial ownership and source-of-funds data; (6) funds of a CAD 10,000 or more incoming wire must be verifiable against the Canadian bank's EFT purpose code classification, typically 'K050 – Capital Payments,' to ensure accurate onward reporting. No Bank of Canada authorization or foreign-exchange permit is required; for debt injections, ensure the interest rate and terms align with the Income Tax Act thin-capitalization rules (Section 18(4)) and transfer-pricing guidelines to preserve Part XIII treaty benefits.Outbound disbursements for equipment, foodservice supplies, franchise software subscriptions, and U.S. or cross-border vendor payments utilize: (i) SWIFT MT103 for single foreign-wire payments above CAD 10,000, with proper purpose codes and the beneficiary's full banking details; (ii) documentary letters of credit issued under UCP600 via the bank's trade finance desk for high-value kitchen-equipment procurement (recommended when the vendor requires LC terms); (iii) standby LCs (ISP98) for lease guarantees and platform performance obligations; (iv) domestic clearing through Payments Canada's Lynx for same-day large-value CAD payments to Ontario suppliers, and the Automated Clearing Settlement System (ACSS) for batch EFT and payroll; (v) the bank's USD clearing corridor via the U.S. Fedwire system for payments from the entity's USD account. For outbound platform payouts (emerging-market riders or overseas software vendors), a Treasury policy should require integration of the bank-hosted payment APIs with the entity's ERP (NetSuite/Xero) so that every MT103 references the purchase order and that all wires are automatically reconciled against open payables.Outright foreign exchange forwards (USD/CAD and other convertible pairs) with OSFI-regulated Schedule I banks; tenors typically up to 24 months, executed under an ISDA 2002 Master Agreement or bank's standard FX Terms; no statutory underlying-trade documentation requirement, but the hedge should be designated under IFRS 9 to align with the entity's cash-flow hedging policy.Cross-currency swaps (CCIRS) for economically converting U.S.-dollar intercompany loans or non-resident shareholder debt into CAD liabilities, aligning interest rate and currency risk.Vanilla FX call and put options purchased from Schedule I banks to cap conversion costs on platform payout repatriation while preserving upside benefit from CAD strength.FX swap (swap-points) transactions for rolling short-term liquidity between CAD operating accounts and USD settlement accounts without incurring bid-offer spreads on spot conversions.Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs): permissible under Canadian law, but economically unnecessary for USD/CAD because the currency is fully convertible; accepted only for exposures in illiquid non-convertible emerging-market currencies that the bank's NDF desk supports, and subject to enhanced counterparty risk and collateral requirements.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility Rating: Medium (supplementary rail) / Low (sole primary treasury bank). Offshore digital EMIs (Wise Platform, Payoneer, Airwallex, Stripe Treasury, Intergiro) are legally permissible as collection and disbursement conduits for platform payouts, but cannot substitute for a Schedule I chartered bank. Legal constraints: (i) a foreign MSB operating in Canada must register with FINTRAC as a Money Services Business, appoint a designated compliance officer, and maintain a Canadian program; approved as a registered ‘foreign MSB’ only if it has a physical place of business in Canada; (ii) under the Retail Payment Activities Act, payment service providers must be registered with the Bank of Canada before performing payment functions (end-to-end clearing, settlement, or fund transfer); (iii) offshore EMIs do not have access to Lynx (Canada's RTGS system) and therefore cannot settle CAD large-value payments natively; (iv) platform agreements with UberEats and DoorDash typically require settlement to a bank account in the payout jurisdiction, and an EMI lacking a Canadian banking partner may trigger sponsor-bank rejection. Recommended structure: use an EMI for cross-border sub-ledger FX conversion and contractor disbursements, while all regulatory capital, tax payments, payroll, and platform settlements flow through a Tier-1 Schedule I bank account.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined effective statutory headline rate for a general Canadian corporation carrying on active business in Ontario (15% federal basic rate after provincial abatement and general reduction, plus 11.5% Ontario general corporate income tax rate).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% statutory Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid by Canadian-resident corporate taxpayers to non-residents; tax-treaty relief may reduce the rate to 15% for most arm's-length shareholders and to 5% for a US corporate shareholder owning at least 10% of voting shares (Canada-US Tax Convention).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: The mandatory T106 filing threshold is CAD 1,000,000 in aggregate fair market value of controlled transactions with a given non-resident counterparty in a single taxation year. Full contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation (Local File and Master File) is expected under CRA Information Circular IC-87-2R and forms the due-diligence defence for any controlled cross-border transaction; Country-by-Country Reporting (CbCR) is compulsory for MNE groups with annual consolidated revenue exceeding CAD 1.125 billion (€750 million).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not apply UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations. The general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under section 245 of the Income Tax Act operates to deny tax benefits from transactions or structures lacking real business purpose and economic substance, and CRA will recharacterize artificial intercompany arrangements, including treaty shopping, passive IP holding, and shell-brand fee structures in the ghost-kitchen/cloud-kitchen delivery space.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Statement / Unreported Digital-Revenue

Statutory sanction: Civil penalty under ITA section 163(2) of 50% of the understated tax; criminal tax-evasion penalties under ITA section 239 can reach 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for up to five years; under the Ontario Corporations Tax Act parallel reassessments and interest will accrue.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who participated in, authorized, or acquiesced to the evasion may face CRA director-liability assessments, civil restitution from personal assets, seizure of bank accounts and travel bans, and, in serious fraud cases, criminal indictment and imprisonment.
Failure to Withhold and Remit GST/HST and Payroll Source Deductions

Statutory sanction: CRA may assess unremitted GST/HST and payroll source deductions plus arrears interest, apply the deemed-trust rule over corporate assets, and impose third-party penalties; under the Excise Tax Act, GST/HST penalties of 5% plus interest and daily penalties may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for unreported GST/HST and payroll source deductions under ITA section 227.1 and Excise Tax Act section 323; active or passive directors with knowledge and effective control are subject to personal recovery, and CRA can obtain writs against personal property, travel bans for investigation, and imprisonment for egregious failure.
Transfer-Pricing Misstatement / Offshore Brand-Fee Sheltering

Statutory sanction: CRA can adjust taxable income back to the Canadian operator under ITA section 247(2); section 247(3) imposes a penalty equal to 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment (minimum CAD 5,000) where adequate documentation is not prepared; the resulting tax is charged with prescribed interest.

Director personal liability: Board members who approve bogus intercompany fees or sign incomplete T2 returns may be held personally liable for the corporate tax shortfall; criminal prosecution for procurement of a false invoice may lead to civil restitution, lifting of the corporate veil, passport seizure, and imprisonment.
Artificial Shell-Company Structure / GAAR Abuse

Statutory sanction: GAAR under section 245 disallows the tax benefit and CRA may reattribute income to the Canadian ghost-kitchen entity; false-statement penalties under section 163(2) apply if information is misleading; Ontario will assess tax on the true generating entity, with interest and penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors using a corporate veil for fraudulent avoidance may be personally assessed; courts can pierce the veil to recover income tax, GST/HST, and penalties; individuals may face criminal fraud charges under section 380 of the Criminal Code with restitution, travel bans, and imprisonment up to 14 years.
Late or Fraudulent T2 Filing / Deficiency and Statute of Limitations

Statutory sanction: Late-filing penalties as detailed in the filing anatomy; knowingly making false statements carries a civil penalty up to CAD 8,000 for false statements and/or 50% of tax; criminal prosecution may add fines and imprisonment; CRA has unlimited or extended loss-assessment periods for misrepresentation.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign or authorize false T2 returns are exposed to direct assessments, civil liability to CRA, disqualification as directors under the Ontario Business Corporations Act in proceedings, travel freedom limitations, and potential imprisonment for willful tax fraud.
