Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not maintain a U.S.-style check-the-box election for domestic corporations; a Business Corporation is always taxed as a separate entity under the Income Tax Act and the Quebec Taxation Act. Foreign hybrid entity classification is recognized on the basis of the relevant tax treaty and CRA administrative positions, for example Article IV(6) of the Canada-U.S. Tax Treaty with respect to U.S. LLC check-the-box elections. Domestic rollover elections are available under sections 85, 86, 87 and 88(2) of the Income Tax Act for transfers, reorganizations, amalgamations and wind-ups. Hybrid mismatch and branch mismatch deduction-denial rules are mandatory; no elective override or hybrid mismatch election mechanism is available.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST: CAD 30,000 in annual world-wide taxable supplies (small supplier threshold); QST: CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies (small supplier threshold, as applied by Revenu Québec). A Quebec fast-casual franchise operating physical restaurant premises will exceed these thresholds and is therefore required to register for both GST/HST and QST.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies to the following: (i) goods exported in commercial quantities by a registrant and delivered to a non-resident outside Canada, evidenced by transport documents showing the export movement; (ii) supplies of services to non-residents, provided the services are not performed in connection with Canadian real property or tangible personal property in Canada and are not otherwise consumed in Canada; and (iii) intangible property supplies such as franchise rights, trademarks and know-how supplied to non-resident franchisees, where the non-resident is not registered for GST/HST or QST in Canada and does not use the supply in carrying on a business through a Canadian permanent establishment. The Quebec QST zero-rating rules mirror these federal categories. For franchise royalty streams to be zero-rated, the records must demonstrate the non-resident status of the recipient and the absence of Canadian place-of-supply use.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A / CAED / CARM electronic declaration) where the export reporting threshold is met or goods are controlled
  • Bill of Lading / air waybill / international truck manifest
  • Commercial invoice / sales contract specifying exporter, consignee, Incoterms and export value
  • Packing list / container or vehicle report
  • Inward remittance certificate / bank credit advice evidencing receipt of export proceeds, if applicable for FX repatriation evidence

No real-time fiscal clearance, API-based clearance, or digital stamping mandate exists at the federal or Quebec level. E-invoicing is permitted but not compulsory. Registered businesses must file GST/HST and QST returns electronically using GST/HST NETFILE or Revenu Québec CLIC, and invoices must contain the prescribed GST/QST information. Restaurants must issue an itemized bill showing federal GST and Quebec QST where applicable.

CRA and Revenu Québec may reassess GST/HST/QST for a four-year period; this limitation period is unlimited in cases of misrepresentation or fraud. If a zero-rated export claim cannot be substantiated, the supply is reclassified as taxable at the combined 14.975% rate and input tax credits previously claimed on connected purchases may be denied or retrospectively clawed back. Civil penalties for gross negligence may reach 25% of the net tax misstated; tax evasion convictions expose the taxpayer to fines of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years. Revenu Québec imposes its own administrative penalties and may deny QST refunds where export documentation is incomplete. Franchise royalty zero-rating claims are scrutinized against the permanent establishment and place-of-supply rules.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada imposes no statutory differentiation between current and capital account convertibility. Profit remittance is unrestricted and not subject to prior exchange-control approval, but corporate law solvency and board-authorization requirements must be met. Non-resident dividends and similar distributions remain subject to Part XIII withholding tax under the Income Tax Act, but such tax is collected through the remittance process and does not preclude conversion or transfer. Cross-border capital movements, including shareholder loans and equity injections, are freely permitted and are not subject to Bank of Canada screening, provided the Canadian financial institution's normal client due diligence and FINTRAC reporting obligations are satisfied.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada operates no export-proceeds surrender regime. There is no statutory timeline requiring repatriation of export receipts, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no obligation to surrender foreign currency earned by a Canadian exporter. Export proceeds may be retained in foreign currency or converted at the exporter's discretion, subject only to requirements of corporate accounting records and the general obligation to accurately report foreign-source income under the Income Tax Act.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (monetary authority) and Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (prudential regulator); Canada maintains no dedicated foreign exchange administration or exchange control board, and the Canadian dollar is fully convertible under IMF Article VIII obligations.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility: Low for primary banking; Supplemental only. Offshore digital EMI and non-bank payment providers lack deposit-taking authority in Canada and cannot provide a core treasury settlement rail for statutory payroll, HST/QST remittance, or royalty escrow. A foreign EMI acting in Canada as a payment service provider or money services business must register with FINTRAC, comply with the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, and may still not have direct access to the Lynx high-value payment system. Use may be acceptable for low-value supplier facilitation but never as the primary custody for capital escrow or operational liquidity.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Transfers from China or elsewhere must be executed through licensed financial institutions using SWIFT MT103 messages with structurally precise payment purposes such as "Capital injection — equity contribution" or "Shareholder loan to operating subsidiary". The instructed sender must match the record of the shareholder or funding party. For CAD settlement, the receiving Canadian bank will credit the operating account through Lynx or correspondent rails. Every wire equal to or above CAD 10,000 is reported under FINTRAC electronic funds transfer rules, and the bank's compliance file must contain the supporting share subscription, corporate board resolution, capital call schedule, and evidence that the funds originate from a legitimate, licensed financial institution. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

For a corporation with a Quebec permanent establishment, the usual combined federal and Quebec general corporate income tax rate is 26.5%: federal 15% plus Quebec 11.5%. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) may generally obtain the federal 9% small-business rate and Quebec's 3.2% rate on eligible active business income, producing a combined rate of 12.2%, subject to the federal and Quebec business limits, eligibility tests, and associated-corporation rules. The applicable tax result depends on residency, permanent establishment, income character, and available deductions.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The domestic Part XIII withholding rate on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is generally 25%. An applicable income tax treaty may reduce the rate; common treaty outcomes include 15% for portfolio dividends and 5% for a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting ownership and other conditions. The Canada-U.S. Treaty has its own conditions and rates. Confirm the recipient's residence, beneficial ownership, shareholding, and treaty eligibility before applying a reduced rate.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose a general transaction-value threshold for the contemporaneous-documentation obligation under Income Tax Act section 247. Documentation must be prepared by the filing-due date and provided within three months after a CRA request. The transfer-pricing penalty generally requires a net adjustment exceeding the lesser of CAD 5 million and 10% of gross revenue, subject to statutory conditions and reasonable-efforts exceptions. Quebec generally applies the federal transfer-pricing framework to relevant transactions.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no general Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to certain offshore jurisdictions. Nevertheless, transactions must be supported by their legal rights and obligations, commercial purpose, conduct, and applicable Canadian tax doctrines. Transfer-pricing rules require arm's-length terms for relevant cross-border non-arm's-length transactions. General anti-avoidance, sham, beneficial-ownership, treaty anti-abuse, and specific anti-avoidance rules may apply. A Canadian corporation must maintain adequate books and records and demonstrate that deductible management fees, royalties, interest, and other related-party charges are genuine, properly priced, and attributable to its business.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion, false statements, or wilful failure to file

Statutory sanction: Federal Income Tax Act offences can result in fines of 50% to 200% of tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to two years on summary conviction; indictable offences can carry fines of 100% to 200% and imprisonment for up to five years. Additional civil tax, interest, gross-negligence penalties, and provincial sanctions may apply. Exact consequences depend on the offence and prosecution route.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for corporate income tax merely because they are directors. Personal exposure may arise for their own criminal conduct, statutory director-liability provisions, unpaid source deductions, or other legally specified obligations. Criminal conviction may result in fines and imprisonment. Travel restrictions are not an automatic tax penalty; a court order or separate legal basis is required.
Failure to remit payroll source deductions

Statutory sanction: The corporation may owe the unremitted amounts, interest, and penalties. Under federal statutory rules, directors may be jointly and severally liable for specified unremitted source deductions, subject to due-diligence and limitation-period rules. Criminal prosecution may apply to wilful failures.

Director personal liability: A director may face personal assessment for covered remittances if statutory conditions are met and the due-diligence defence is unavailable. Liability is not equivalent to automatic piercing of the corporate veil; imprisonment requires a separate offence and conviction.
Transfer-pricing non-compliance

Statutory sanction: The CRA may adjust income, impose interest, and assess the section 247 transfer-pricing penalty where the statutory threshold and conditions are met. Reassessments may be made under applicable extended reassessment rules. Quebec adjustments and penalties may also apply.

Director personal liability: A director is not ordinarily personally liable for the corporation's transfer-pricing adjustment solely by holding office. Personal exposure may arise from participation in fraud, false statements, or other independently applicable statutory offences.
Improper withholding on cross-border payments

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for the tax required to be withheld, interest, and applicable penalties, including penalties for failure to deduct, remit, or file information returns. Treaty relief may be denied if its conditions are not met.

Director personal liability: Director liability depends on the specific statutory provision and the director's conduct; it is not automatic for every withholding assessment. Knowingly participating in evasion may create separate civil or criminal exposure.
Corporate dissolution, asset distribution, or inadequate tax clearance

Statutory sanction: Tax authorities may pursue statutory recovery against recipients or responsible persons where the applicable legislation permits, reassess the corporation, and impose interest and penalties. A distribution is not a substitute for satisfying tax obligations.

Director personal liability: Recipient or director liability depends on statutory recovery provisions and the facts, including the value transferred and required knowledge or conduct. Civil recovery may be available; imprisonment or travel restrictions are not automatic consequences of a tax debt.
Shell-company or AML misuse

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include criminal prosecution, proceeds-of-crime forfeiture, regulatory penalties, account restrictions, and sanctions under the applicable federal or provincial laws. The specific offence and penalty depend on the conduct; incorporation or foreign ownership alone is not unlawful.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly participate, direct, or assist may face personal criminal and civil liability, including fines, imprisonment, and asset forfeiture where authorized by law. Travel bans require a separate statutory or judicial basis and are not an automatic corporate-tax sanction.
