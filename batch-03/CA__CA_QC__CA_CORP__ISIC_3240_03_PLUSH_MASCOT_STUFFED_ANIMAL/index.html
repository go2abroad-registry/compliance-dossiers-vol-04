Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) for federal corporate income tax and GST, portal: CRA My Business Account; Revenu Québec for Quebec corporate income tax and Quebec Sales Tax (QST), portal: Revenu Québec Mon dossier d'entreprise.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Obtain certificate of incorporation under the Canada Business Corporations Act or Quebec Business Corporations Act; the corporate registry transmits basic entity data to CRA and Revenu Québec, but the taxpayer must activate the 9-digit Business Number (BN) and tax accounts. Step 2: Register the BN and the Corporation Income Tax account with CRA through Business Registration Online; register the GST account (RT program account) when taxable worldwide supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in any four consecutive calendar quarters, or voluntarily to claim input tax credits. Step 3: For a business carrying on activities in Quebec, register with Revenu Québec for the QST and Quebec corporate tax accounts, obtaining the Quebec enterprise number (NEQ) and the QST registration number. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation / Territorial_Scope: A CBCA or QBCA business corporation is a separate taxable person resident in Canada; it is subject to federal Part I corporate tax and Quebec corporate tax on worldwide income, and no pass-through or tax-transparent treatment is available. A non-resident corporation not incorporated in Canada is taxable in Canada only on Canadian-source income and may be taxable in Quebec if it carries on business or has a permanent establishment in Quebec. The plush-toy OEM manufacturing activity is not located in a tax-exempt zone.

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box election and no elective pass-through exist in Canadian tax law. Ordinary corporate elections available include the s. 85(1) rollover on transferring property to the corporation, the s. 89(11) capital dividend account election, and the election by a non-resident corporation to compute Canadian-source business or rental income on a net basis under relevant provisions. Hybrid-mismatch elections are not available; Canada applies mandatory anti-hybrid rules and the excessive interest and financing expenses limitation (EIFEL) regime.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000: a person is a GST small supplier until total worldwide taxable supplies in a calendar quarter and the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters are CAD 30,000 or less; registration becomes mandatory once the threshold is exceeded. Quebec applies a parallel QST small-supplier threshold of CAD 30,000 of taxable supplies made in Quebec. A plush-toy OEM supplying B2B goods into Canada or Quebec above the threshold must register and charge GST and QST unless the supply qualifies as a zero-rated export.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

GST and QST are zero-rated on exports of tangible personal property exported from Canada as soon as reasonably possible after transfer of possession, including plush mascot dolls shipped by courier or freight to foreign buyers, provided the goods are not consumed or used in Canada before export. Contract manufacturing, embroidery, sewing, and ultrasonic seaming services performed in Canada on goods that are subsequently exported may also qualify for zero-rating where the evidence identifies the foreign recipient and the actual export movement. For separately supplied services to non-residents, zero-rating generally requires that the service be supplied to a non-resident and not be performed in connection with Canadian real estate or goods consumed in Canada. Because zero-rated exports are taxable supplies, the exporter may claim input tax credits and input tax refunds on inputs.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA B13A / Canadian Export Reporting System e-manifest record) for shipments exceeding CAD 2,000 or controlled goods
  • Commercial invoice and signed purchase order identifying the plush toy HS code 9503.00 and NAICS 339930
  • Bill of lading, air waybill, or courier consignment note showing Canadian origin and foreign destination
  • Packing list and certificate of origin where preferential tariff treatment is claimed
  • Proof of export delivery, such as carrier receipt, terminal receipt, or foreign customs entry evidence
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (IRC) or bank inward-remittance advice tying foreign-currency export proceeds to the invoice

There is no national real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or CRA invoice-clearance API in Canada. GST and QST invoices may be issued electronically in a readable format without CRA or Revenu Québec prior approval, provided they contain the prescribed information. Quebec has introduced an electronic-invoicing framework for QST registrants using structured e-invoices and XML/EDI formats; however no transaction-level clearance or digital stamp is currently operative. Suppliers must maintain invoice records in CRA and Revenu Québec acceptable electronic form for audit.

CRA and Revenu Québec may reassess and claw back zero-rating and related input tax credits or refunds where export documentation is incomplete or inconsistent with the invoice. The normal reassessment period is four years, extended to seven years for misrepresentation, with no limitation period in cases of fraud. False or grossly negligent export zero-rating claims may attract federal gross-negligence false-statement penalties under the Excise Tax Act, equivalent Quebec penalties, interest, and criminal tax-evasion prosecution with substantial fines and imprisonment. Export proceeds should be repatriated and matched to invoices within 180 days to support the evidentiary file and avoid heightened audit scrutiny.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible on both current-account and capital-account transactions; Canada has accepted Article VIII obligations under the IMF Articles of Agreement. There are no quantitative limits, permit systems, or central-bank approval gates on dividend distributions, royalty payments, interest remittances, management fees, or loan principal repayments to non-residents. Profit-remittance hurdles are purely fiscal: a non-resident shareholder of a Quebec Business Corporation faces withholding tax under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (Canada) at a 25% statutory rate, generally reduced to 10% on dividends under the Canada–China income tax treaty, and to 15% on arm's-length interest or royalties where the treaty applies; Quebec does not impose a separate provincial withholding on non-resident dividend remittances. Remittance mechanics require the corporation to file an NR4 information return with Canada Revenue Agency, remit the withheld amount electronically, and provide the payee with an NR4 slip; no exchange-control release certificate or CAD conversion authorization is required.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no export-proceeds surrender obligation and no mandatory statutory conversion ratio; a Canadian exporter may lawfully retain USD or other foreign currency in a foreign-currency bank account or convert to CAD at its sole discretion. The only binding reporting duties are: (i) presentation of a B13A Export Declaration to the Canada Border Services Agency for commercial exports by air, sea, rail, or land where the shipment value exceeds CAD 2,000, and (ii) statistical reporting of export payments for Statistics Canada balance-of-payments purposes — such reporting is purely informational and does not constitute a control or surrender mechanism. Under PCMLTFA, corporate records must preserve proof of export payments for five years; the deposit of any export proceeds in a Canadian CDIC-member institution is protected only up to CAD 100,000 per insured category.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) — monetary authority and lender of last resort; prudential banking regulation is administered by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) under the Bank Act (Canada); anti-money-laundering supervision is exercised by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) pursuant to the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act; Quebec-level provincial financial-sector conduct and non-bank deposit-taking oversight is vested in the Autorité des marchés financiers (AMF). Canada operates an open, liberalized FX regime with no statutory exchange-control authority requiring pre-authorization of cross-border capital movements.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Medium-High for ancillary payment aggregation and marketplace settlement; Low for deposit-taking, documentary credits, or sole treasury sovereignty. A foreign digital EMI such as Wise, Payoneer, or Stripe may lawfully onboard a Quebec Business Corporation as a payment service provider without a Canadian bank charter, receiving inbound receipts and distributing outbound supplier disbursements. However, a foreign EMI cannot accept deposits in the legal sense, cannot issue cheques, and cannot provide domestic Canadian payment accounts unless registered under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA), effective September 2024, which requires payment service providers with incidental Canadian activity to register with the Bank of Canada; an EMI offering money remittance must also register with FINTRAC as a money services business. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 annual aggregate fair market value of non-arm's-length cross-border transactions (Form T106 threshold); corporations above this threshold are required to maintain contemporaneous Local File/transfer pricing documentation to preserve 'reasonable efforts' defence against penalties and to substantiate Master File data under CRA/OECD guidance

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone Economic Substance Regulations statute; instead, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA s.245 and Quebec Taxation Act s.1079.5.1) applies and requires genuine commercial substance. For a plush-toy OEM, economic substance evidence includes actual employees, physical factory premises, inventory risk, equipment, management decision-making, and real OEM customer contracts; absence of these factors triggers GAAR recharacterization, denial of intercompany payments, and in severe cases treaty-shopping or sham arguments.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Conscious Omission in Corporate Return

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s.239(1): conviction for tax evasion carries a fine of up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years; Quebec Taxation Act imposes a 50% civil penalty for wilful false statements; CRA may reassess beyond the normal 3-year limitation period where misrepresentation is involved; interest and penalties accumulate daily.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign or cause false returns to be filed are personally exposed to criminal prosecution, personal fines, restitution orders and imprisonment up to 5 years; travel bans are commonly attached as bail conditions in cross-border tax prosecutions; if the corporation is found to be a sham or a façade, courts may pierce the corporate veil and hold directors personally liable for the corporation's tax, penalties and interest.
Transfer Pricing Documentation Failure and Unjustified Margin Erosion

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing penalty under ITA s.247(3) of 10% of the net upward TP adjustment; failure to file T106 incurs CAD 100 per day, maximum CAD 2,500; false statement gross negligence penalty under s.163.2 is 50% of the understated tax; extended reassessment period of 6 years for TP adjustments; Quebec parallel TP rules and penalties apply.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for transfer pricing penalties, but if directors sign false declarations or knowingly instruct intercompany pricing based on fabricated terms, they face civil restitution and gross-negligence penalties; knowing participation in a transfer-pricing sham may support criminal prosecution under s.239 and director exposure to the 100%-200% evasion penalty.
Shell Company / Artificial Jurisdiction Avoidance

Statutory sanction: GAAR allows the CRA and Revenu Québec to disallow the entire tax benefit and recharacterize the arrangement; offshore investment entity rules under ITA s.94 may deem certain foreign property income accrued; Part XIII withholding may be imposed on deemed dividends or management fees; false offshore reporting can trigger criminal tax evasion charges; corporate and provincial penalties can exceed 50% of the avoided tax.

Director personal liability: If the shell structure is a deliberate sham or a sham used to defeat creditors, courts will pierce the corporate veil under the Fenmount standard; directors become jointly and severally liable for corporate taxes, interest, penalties and restitution; laundering benefits through shell invoicing invites offences under Criminal Code s.462.31 with imprisonment up to 10 years and asset forfeiture.
AML / Proceeds of Crime Integration through OEM Payment Rails

Statutory sanction: Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act administrative penalties can reach CAD 1,000,000 per contravention for corporations; Criminal Code s.462.31 authorizes imprisonment up to 10 years for laundering proceeds of crime; criminal forfeiture of property under s.462.37; direct fines under controlled substances statutes when applicable.

Director personal liability: Directors who organize, instruct or participate in laundering transactions are personally subject to arrest, pre-trial travel bans, freezing orders, asset seizure, criminal fines and imprisonment up to 10 years; convictions may lead to disqualification from serving as directors under federal or Quebec corporate legislation and to civil restitution to victims and tax authorities.
