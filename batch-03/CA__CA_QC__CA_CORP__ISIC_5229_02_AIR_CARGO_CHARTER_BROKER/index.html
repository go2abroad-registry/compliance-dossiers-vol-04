Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no general United States-style check-the-box election for classifying a corporation as a partnership or disregarded entity. A corporation is generally taxed as a separate legal person under the Income Tax Act. Available elections are transaction- or regime-specific, including elections concerning GST/HST and QST fiscal periods, cash or accrual reporting where permitted, joint ventures, section 85 corporate reorganizations, amalgamations, wind-ups, and certain partnership or trust treatments. Canadian hybrid mismatch rules under the Income Tax Act can deny deductions, inclusion offsets, or other tax benefits where a mismatch results from differences in the legal or tax classification of an entity or instrument in Canada and another jurisdiction. The corporation must also assess treaty residence, permanent-establishment allocation, controlled foreign affiliate rules, thin-capitalization rules, transfer pricing, and foreign-entity reporting separately.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies for the general GST/HST small-supplier threshold, calculated over the applicable preceding four-quarter period or single-quarter period. Quebec generally uses a CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold for QST purposes. The federal GST rate is 5.0%; Quebec QST is generally 9.975%, producing a combined headline rate of 14.975% on supplies subject to both taxes. Zero-rated supplies remain taxable supplies for registration-threshold purposes but bear tax at 0%. Special rules apply to financial institutions, listed financial institutions, non-residents, agents, and supplies of international transportation services.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply may qualify for 0% GST/HST and corresponding QST treatment where the statutory zero-rating rules apply. For this business, the principal possibilities are: (1) an international freight transportation service involving transportation of goods from Canada to a destination outside Canada, or from outside Canada to a destination outside Canada where the statutory conditions are met; (2) a service of arranging or procuring a qualifying zero-rated freight transportation service; (3) qualifying services performed in respect of goods that are exported, where the statutory conditions and time limits are satisfied; and (4) certain supplies made to non-residents that are specifically zero-rated under the place-of-supply and service-export rules. A cargo charter broker must distinguish its brokerage or arranging service from the underlying charter or transportation service and retain evidence of the contractual role, route, destination, consignor and consignee, export status, and consideration. A non-resident customer alone does not automatically make a Canadian service zero-rated. Supplies performed in or connected with Canada may remain taxable. Input tax credits or QST input tax refunds generally require valid supporting documentation and must relate to commercial activities.

  • Commercial contract, charter agreement, brokerage or agency agreement, and purchase order identifying the parties, service, route, and responsibility for transportation
  • Air waybill, master air waybill, house air waybill, cargo manifest, flight records, or equivalent transport documentation
  • Canada Border Services Agency export declaration or other accepted export reporting evidence, where an export declaration is legally required
  • Commercial invoice, packing list, cargo description, and consignee documentation
  • Evidence of the foreign destination and delivery, such as carrier delivery confirmation, destination customs records, proof of receipt, or equivalent third-party evidence
  • Tax invoice showing the supplier's GST/HST and QST registration numbers where applicable and identifying the statutory zero-rating basis
  • Payment records, bank statements, remittance records, and customer account reconciliation; an inward remittance certificate is not generally a mandatory Canadian document
  • Evidence supporting any export-time or location-of-goods requirement applicable to services performed in respect of exported goods
  • Books and records sufficient to reconcile the zero-rated supply to the relevant GST/HST and QST returns and input tax credit or input tax refund claims

Canada and Quebec do not generally impose a universal real-time electronic invoice clearance or mandatory digital-stamping system for ordinary business-to-business GST/HST or QST invoices. Tax invoices must contain prescribed information, including supplier identity, GST/HST or QST registration details where applicable, date, description, consideration, and tax amount or applicable indication. Electronic records and invoices are acceptable if they remain reliable, accessible, and auditable. GST/HST and QST returns are generally filed electronically where mandatory or elected, and large businesses may have electronic filing obligations. Import and export customs declarations are submitted through the Canada Border Services Agency's electronic systems, including the CARM framework as applicable. Electronic invoicing, payment-record, customs, and books-and-records controls should be integrated even though transaction-level tax clearance is not generally required.

Canada does not generally impose a 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation requirement for claiming export zero-rating. Zero-rating depends on the statutory supply, destination, documentation, and timing rules, not on a general mandatory repatriation certificate. The CRA or Revenu Québec may reassess tax where the zero-rating conditions are not met, including GST/HST or QST on the supply, interest, denial or recovery of input tax credits or QST input tax refunds, and penalties for failure to register, collect, remit, file, maintain records, or exercise due diligence. False statements or omissions may attract gross-negligence penalties, generally up to 50% of the understated tax or improperly claimed amount, while knowingly false or deceptive conduct may create additional civil or criminal exposure. Unremitted collected GST/HST or QST can result in director-liability exposure subject to statutory conditions, collection action, and other enforcement measures. Export documentation should be retained for the applicable statutory record-retention period, generally at least six years from the end of the relevant tax year, unless a longer period or audit, objection, appeal, or investigation applies.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a free currency convertibility regime for current account transactions, enabling full conversion of CAD to foreign currencies for operational expenses and profit remittances without prior approval. Capital account transactions may trigger Bank of Canada review for investments exceeding thresholds, yet routine corporate profit repatriation encounters no convertibility restrictions. Profit remittance is subject to FINTRAC AML reporting and CRA tax compliance, with potential delays if source-of-funds documentation is insufficient.

Export proceeds surrender rules

For goods exports, proceeds must be repatriated to Canada within 90 days of receipt with conversion at prevailing market rates; no mandatory surrender ratio applies. For service-based receipts such as air charter fees, conversion occurs freely upon deposit with authorized dealers without statutory timelines or ratios.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Digital EMIs are feasible domestically but cross-border viability is constrained by provincial AML regulations and FATF standards; offshore digital wallets require comprehensive KYC and may face corporate usage restrictions.
