Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) is the federal tax authority; Revenu Quebec is the provincial tax authority for Quebec (QST, provincial corporate income tax). Primary portals are CRA My Business Account (canada.ca) and Revenu Quebec Business Online Services (CleNumerique).

E-government filing portal

Foreign entity must first obtain a Business Number (BN) by registering with the CRA using Form RC1 or through the Business Registration Online service, identifying the entity type and income tax account. If the entity will make taxable supplies in Canada, it must also open a GST/HST program account (RT). For provincial nexus with Quebec, registration with Revenu Quebec is required for QST via Form LM-101-V, and the Quebec Registraire d'entreprises will issue an enterprise number (NEQ) which integrates with the provincial TIN system. The BN serves as the federal TIN; the QST account number (TD-QST) serves as the provincial indirect tax identifier.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not use check-the-box elections. Canadian corporations are automatically treated as separate taxable entities. Statutory elections relevant to hybrid arrangements include the section 216 election (non-resident net-income election for rental and royalty income) and the section 142.4 mark-to-market election. For GST/HST, a joint election under section 150 may be used for certain financial services supplies. Hybrid mismatch rules under BEPS Action 2 apply by operation of law and are not elective.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 annual worldwide taxable supplies for GST/HST; the Quebec QST small-supplier threshold is likewise CAD 30,000. A non-resident DTC seller must register once total gross revenue from taxable supplies exceeds this threshold, subject to the simplified e-commerce rules for non-resident suppliers.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating under GST/HST applies to supplies of goods that are exported by the supplier, supplies of services performed wholly outside Canada for non-residents, and certain intellectual property transactions. For Quebec QST, similar zero-rating rules apply. A DTC webstore selling physical goods to foreign consumers qualifies when the goods are delivered to a destination outside Canada and proof of export is retained. Digital services supplied to non-residents are zero-rated where the recipient is outside Canada and the service is not performed for use primarily in Canada.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (B13A or electronic CARM declaration)
  • Bill of Lading and/or Air Waybill evidencing international carriage
  • Commercial invoice or sales contract
  • Proof of foreign currency receipt and inward remittance documentation
  • Carrier or freight forwarder certificates of export

Canada and Quebec do not currently mandate real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control, or digital stamping of invoices. Electronically issued invoices are valid if they meet statutory content requirements under the Excise Tax Act and Quebec QST Act. The Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing framework exists to facilitate interoperable B2B exchange, but adoption is voluntary and no prior tax authority authorization or transaction-level clearance is required.

Where export documentation is incomplete or non-compliant, CRA and Revenu Quebec may claw back zero-rated treatment, reassess GST/HST and QST at the full import-tax equivalent (through reverse charge in Quebec), and charge interest and penalties. False statement penalties of the greater of CAD 100 and 5% of the tax, and gross negligence penalties of 50% of the tax evaded, apply. Criminal tax evasion can result in fines of up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for up to five years. Revenu Quebec may impose parallel administrative penalties and pursue separate recovery.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada has accepted IMF Article VIII obligations: both current-account and capital-account transactions are fully and freely convertible. The Canadian dollar (CAD) is a free-floating currency with no exchange-control approval, quotas, or surrender requirements. Profit-remittance hurdles are tax-driven, not FX-driven: dividends, interest, royalties, and management fees paid to non-residents are subject to Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (Canada) withholding (statutory 25%; treaty-reduced to 5–15% under most DTAAs, including the Canada–US treaty for direct dividends and arm's-length interest), with Quebec's supplementary provincial withholding coordinated through the Canada–Quebec tax collection agreement. No prior regulatory clearance, balance-of-payments approval, or documentary endorsement is required before wiring out dividends, repaying shareholder loans, or remitting royalties. Operationally, all wire transfers of CAD 10,000 or more routed through Canadian banks are reported to FINTRAC as electronic funds-transfer reports (EFTRs); this is a supervisory reporting obligation, not a convertibility restriction.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada does not impose any statutory timeline for export-proceeds repatriation nor any mandatory conversion ratio for export or retail DTC receipts. Proceeds may be held in CAD or foreign-currency treasury accounts without penalty. Tax compliance, however, requires: (i) realized and unrealized FX gains on business receivables/payables are recognized as ordinary income under general business principles (ITA s. 9), while foreign currency held as capital property is governed by ITA s. 39; (ii) a corporation may elect a functional currency under ITA s. 261 to defer conversion noise, but must file consistently for Canadian tax purposes; (iii) inward EFTs over CAD 10,000 trigger the bank's FINTRAC EFTR filing; (iv) specified foreign property exceeding CAD 100,000 must be reported on Form T1135; and (v) if the Canadian entity holds 10% or more of a foreign affiliate, Form T1134 must be filed annually.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) as prudential banking regulator; FINTRAC as financial intelligence and AML/ATF supervisor; the Department of Finance as lead on monetary and exchange-rate policy. Canada has no standalone foreign-exchange administration body; Banking Act and Currency Act govern monetary settlement.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Rating: Medium as a supplementary collection/disbursement rail; Low as the sole treasury cash-management solution. Canada does not recognize the European 'Electronic Money Institution' (EMI) licence as a banking-equivalent class. Offshore EMIs such as Wise, Payoneer, and Airwallex may operate in Canada only by registering as Money Services Businesses (MSBs) under the PCMLTFA and by complying with the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA), which entered into force in phases in 2024 and requires payment-service providers to register with the Bank of Canada by September 2025. Legal constraints: (i) an offshore EMI cannot open a Canadian settlement account in the EMI's own name for card-network clearing; (ii) Shopify Payments, Stripe, and PayPal Business each require settlement into a Canadian-chartered bank account or their own branded balance; (iii) incoming CAD settlement from an EMI may be treated as a third-party transfer by the Canadian bank, triggering additional source-of-funds review; (iv) USD clearing is typically executed through Canadian correspondent banks, adding 1–2 business days' settlement lag. Recommended architecture: hold the primary operational and capital accounts at a Schedule I bank and use one registered EMI solely for marketplace receivables collection and high-frequency cross-border vendor payments under separate client money segregation.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound shareholder capital is wired via SWIFT MT103 directly from the non-resident shareholder's account to the Canadian entity's CAD operational account, referencing the share-subscription agreement and using the Canadian transit/account number plus the bank's SWIFT BIC. Use the IMF Balance of Payments purpose code for direct-investment equity ('Equity – direct investment') for share capital, and code 'Other investment – loans' for shareholder advances. The Canadian bank files an FINTRAC EFTR for any credit of CAD 10,000 or more; no prior Bank of Canada or FINTRAC approval is required. For equity injections, the issuer must contemporaneously issue shares and record paid-up capital; for shareholder loans, a written demand or term loan agreement with a market-rate interest coupon (substantiated under ITA ss. 15, 80.4, and 247) must be executed and retained. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Rating: Medium as a supplementary collection/disbursement rail; Low as the sole treasury cash-management solution. Canada does not recognize the European 'Electronic Money Institution' (EMI) licence as a banking-equivalent class. Offshore EMIs such as Wise, Payoneer, and Airwallex may operate in Canada only by registering as Money Services Businesses (MSBs) under the PCMLTFA and by complying with the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA), which entered into force in phases in 2024 and requires payment-service providers to register with the Bank of Canada by September 2025. Legal constraints: (i) an offshore EMI cannot open a Canadian settlement account in the EMI's own name for card-network clearing; (ii) Shopify Payments, Stripe, and PayPal Business each require settlement into a Canadian-chartered bank account or their own branded balance; (iii) incoming CAD settlement from an EMI may be treated as a third-party transfer by the Canadian bank, triggering additional source-of-funds review; (iv) USD clearing is typically executed through Canadian correspondent banks, adding 1–2 business days' settlement lag. Recommended architecture: hold the primary operational and capital accounts at a Schedule I bank and use one registered EMI solely for marketplace receivables collection and high-frequency cross-border vendor payments under separate client money segregation.
