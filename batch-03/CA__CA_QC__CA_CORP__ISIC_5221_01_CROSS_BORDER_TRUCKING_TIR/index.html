Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current-account and capital-account convertibility of the Canadian dollar in both directions since the repeal of the Foreign Exchange Control Act in 1952. No Bank of Canada authorization is required to inject capital, borrow offshore, settle trade liabilities, open a foreign-currency account, or remit dividends and profits from a Quebec business corporation. Profit-repatriation frictions are purely fiscal and supervisory: Part XIII withholding tax at 25 percent default (treaty-reduced, e.g., under the Canada-United States Tax Convention to 5 percent on qualifying dividends for 10-percent corporate shareholders and 0 percent on certain arm's-length interest); corporate solvency tests prescribed by the Canada Business Corporations Act and the Quebec Business Corporations Act; and asset-freeze obligations imposed by the Special Economic Measures Act and the United Nations Act for designated sanctioned persons or entities. Non-resident investment in a Quebec corporation is generally open without exchange-control approval, although the Investment Canada Act may trigger a notification or national-security review, especially in transportation-related critical infrastructure.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory export-proceeds surrender, repatriation deadline, or mandatory conversion ratio exists in Canada. A Quebec motor carrier may retain United States dollar freight receipts indefinitely in a Foreign Currency Account at any Schedule I bank, or elect Canadian-dollar functional-currency tax reporting under the Income Tax Regulations if its business is conducted principally in USD. The relevant obligations are ordinary record-keeping: six-year retention of books and records under the Income Tax Act; the Quebec Registraire des entreprises du Québec reporting requirements applicable to the corporation; CBSA eManifest and Advance Commercial Information (ACI) transmission for highway exports; and FINTRAC reporting by the financial intermediary for electronic funds transfers of C$10,000 or more in a single transaction. These reporting rules monitor but never restrict the currency, destination, or conversion of export proceeds.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) is the central monetary authority; no standalone foreign-exchange administration or exchange-licensing agency exists in Canada. Exchange-control policy powers reside with the Department of Finance Canada under the Currency Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. C-52); prudential banking supervision is delegated to the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing enforcement is vested in FINTRAC; and national payment-system oversight is shared between Payments Canada and the Bank of Canada under the Canadian Payments Act and the Retail Payment Activities Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility: MEDIUM as a secondary disbursement rail only. Canada has no standalone electronic-money-institution licence category; a digital cross-border provider (Wise Business, Airwallex, Payoneer, or a Canadian fintech) must register as a money services business with FINTRAC, comply with the Retail Payment Activities Act registration and safeguarding obligations supervised by the Bank of Canada, and is prohibited from taking insured deposits or offering bank-grade commercial credit. Legal constraints: an EMI-equivalent cannot open a true Canadian bank account, cannot issue commercial letters of credit, and must conduct independent PCMLTFA customer due diligence on the Quebec corporation. Practical consequence: fintech rails are suitable for small-dollar driver disbursements and fuel-card top-ups, but the primary liquidity clearing, USD settlement infrastructure, and TIR-customs bonding suite must remain at a regulated Canadian Schedule I bank or its licensed US correspondent.

Cash-intensive or unvouchered fuel and maintenance purchases perceived as a money-laundering sink

Root cause: Cash-intensive or unvouchered fuel and maintenance purchases perceived as a money-laundering sink

26.5% combined headline rate (federal 15% under Income Tax Act s. 123(1) + Quebec 11.5% under Taxation Act s. 1028; no small business deduction available to non-resident-controlled corporation; trucking services do not qualify for the Manufacturing & Processing profits reduction)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% statutory Canadian withholding tax on dividends paid to non-residents pursuant to Income Tax Act s. 212(2). Treaty relief under Canada-US Convention Article X: 5% where the US corporate shareholder owns at least 10% of the voting stock of the Canadian payer throughout the 365-day period ending on the dividend declaration date; 15% for portfolio investors. A US LLC treated as transparent is not entitled to treaty relief unless it obtains a Limitation on Benefits (LOB) determination under Article XXIX A.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

Both T2 and CO-17 are due no later than the last day of the sixth month following the corporation's fiscal year-end. Example: December 31 year-end is due June 30 of the following year. Fiscal periods may not exceed 53 weeks without CRA approval.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canadian TP documentation obligations apply to all non-arm's length cross-border transactions with no de minimis threshold for record-keeping. Formal contemporaneous documentation must be prepared where the aggregate value of cross-border related-party transaction exceeds CAD 1,000,000 (goods, services, intangibles, or loans); CRA can demand documentation for any quantum. Mandatory reporting: File T106 - Information Return of Non-Arm's Length Transactions With Non-Residents for every fiscal year containing reportable related-party transactions, irrespective of value; penalties apply for each failure to file T106. Master File and Local File requirements align with OECD TP Guidelines through CRA Information Circular IC 87-2R, IC 94-4R, and the Canada Revenue Agency's APA program. For a Quebec trucking subsidiary of a US carrier, the typical related-party transaction is payment of management fees, dispatch fees, fuel-card clearing charges, and terminal lease payments to the US parent.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no Economic Substance Regulations akin to UAE; nevertheless, the economic substance of a Quebec trucking carrier is tested through the general anti-avoidance rule (ITA s. 245), the sham doctrine, and the statutory preamble of the Income Tax Act. CRA may disregard transactions lacking a bona fide business purpose where the sole or predominant purpose is tax avoidance; profits will be re-characterized on a substance-over-form basis. Further, Quebec applies the doctrine of 'business purpose' through provincial audit practice; the absence of real physical operations in Quebec (no terminal, no drivers, no dispatch) combined with routing arrangements through the US parent will trigger deemed agency under common law, PE exposure under Article V of the Canada-US treaty, and the potential application of the 'designated person' anti-avoidance provisions. The carrier must demonstrate substantive presence through Quebec registered rolling stock, Quebec-licensed drivers, and Quebec-based fuel and insurance contracts.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion / wilful failure to report income

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under s. 239(1) of the Income Tax Act: fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, plus imprisonment for up to five years. Civil reassessment at the full statutory rate with gross negligence penalties of 50% of the understated tax under s. 163(2) and interest compounded daily.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who participated in or acquiesced to the evasion are jointly and severally liable for the corporation's unpaid tax under s. 227.1; upon criminal conviction, directors face personal imprisonment up to five years and can be enjoined from serving as a director of any Canadian corporation for up to five years. Restitution orders under the Criminal Code may attach personal assets, and CBSA may seize the trucking assets as proceeds of crime.
Failure to remit payroll withholding and non-resident withholding

Statutory sanction: Immediate assessment of all un-remitted withholdings plus arrears interest at prescribed rates (currently 9% corporate). Failure to file NR4 and T4 information returns triggers penalties of CAD 100 per form per day, minimum CAD 100 and maximum CAD 7,500 per form; failure to remit is subject to a 10% penalty on amounts unpaid after 3 days for large assessments.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable under s. 227.1 for the full amount of unremitted source deductions regardless of corporate insolvency, without necessity of proving intent. CRA may obtain a civil judgment against personal assets; in systemic cases, directors are charged under s. 242 of the Income Tax Act with fines up to 100% of the unremitted amount plus imprisonment of up to two years. Travel bans may be issued under the Extradition Act in cross-border cases where a director flees Canada.
Transfer pricing documentation failure and concealment of related-party transactions

Statutory sanction: Under s. 162(9) and s. 162(10) of the ITA: penalty for each failure to file T106 of CAD 100 per day up to CAD 25,000 plus an additional CAD 1,000 penalty for every 30-day block of delinquency. Upon audit, CRA will recharacterize the profits and adjust taxable income; re-assessed tax carries 9% interest and gross negligence penalties at 50% under s. 163(2) where records are inadequate or false. The Quebec tax authority (Revenu Quebec) applies parallel sanctions under the Taxation Act.

Director personal liability: Directors who personally participate in preparing or approving false T106 declarations are subject to penalties under s. 239(2) of the ITA (fine of 25% to 100% of the tax sought to be evaded) and criminal prosecution. In flagrant cases, CRA issues a director's liability assessment which becomes a secured claim against personal real estate. The Canada-US mutual assistance treaty enables the IRS and CRA to cross-collect; personal bank accounts in both countries can be garnished.
Customs, CBSA and TIR Carnet fraud

Statutory sanction: Under the Customs Act s. 127 and s. 160: forfeiture of the conveyance and goods, monetary penalty equal to the value of the goods or the amount of duty evaded, plus a penalty not exceeding CAD 25,000. Offences under the Criminal Code s. 380 (fraud) may impose imprisonment up to 14 years. An adverse CBSA finding triggers automatic suspension or cancellation of the carrier's TIR Carnet guarantee and the revocation of the Customs Bonded Carrier licence; the carrier is expelled from the TIR Chain of Guarantee system operated by the Canadian Chamber of Commerce.

Director personal liability: Directors who control the customs compliance program are personally liable for CBSA penalties when they knowingly participate in false declarations. The Canada Border Services Agency may issue a personal penalty assessment under s. 109.1 of the Customs Act; directors can be arrested under the Criminal Code, detained, and prohibited from leaving Canada until the penalty is paid. Seizure of the company's tractor units and trailers is routinely executed at the border crossing point.
Anti-money laundering / FinTRAC compliance failure

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act: administrative monetary penalties (AMPs) of up to CAD 100,000 for a first violation and CAD 500,000 for subsequent violations per contravention; registration as a money services business is voided. Criminal prosecution for laundering proceeds of crime carries imprisonment up to 10 years under Criminal Code s. 462.31. FinTRAC referrals to CBSA and CRA will trigger a coordinated tax, customs, and currency audit.

Director personal liability: The compliance officer and the board must personally attest to the FinTRAC compliance regime; failure to do so subjects directors to AMPs issued against them in their individual capacity under s. 73.2. Knowingly issuing false compliance statements attracts fines of up to CAD 500,000 and imprisonment of up to five years. Personal assets are attachable under the proceeds of crime forfeiture regime, and directors may be barred from leaving Canada pending the criminal hearing.
