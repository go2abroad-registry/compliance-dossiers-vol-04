Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no check-the-box regime; a business corporation is a separate resident taxpayer on worldwide income. Available elections: (1) associated-corporation elections and Small Business Deduction allocation agreements for Canadian-controlled private corporations under ITA Part I and the Quebec Taxation Act; (2) election to waive the Small Business Deduction; (3) hybrid mismatch notional-account elections and BEPS Action 2 adjustments as codified in the Income Tax Act; (4) functional currency election (ITA s. 261); (5) foreign affiliate exempt-surplus and designated-surplus elections under ITA s. 5907 and Part LIX regulations; (6) non-resident-owned investment corporation status is not applicable given active game-publishing business.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 for GST (small-supplier threshold on total worldwide taxable supplies, measured per calendar quarter or four consecutive quarters); CAD 30,000 for QST (identical small-supplier threshold). Mandatory registration once either threshold is exceeded; voluntary registration permitted below threshold. Non-resident digital-supplier simplified GST/QST registration applies to foreign platforms supplying Canadian consumers, but is not required for the resident game publisher itself.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Excise Tax Act s. 23 and Schedule VI Part V, supplies of services (including advertising and ad-network mediation) and intangible personal property (game software licenses, in-app digital content) made to non-resident recipients are zero-rated where the recipient is not receiving the supply in Canada. The QST zero-rating mirrors the GST under the Quebec Taxation Act. For hyper-casual ad-monetized games, zero-rating applies where: (1) advertising inventory is supplied to non-resident ad networks or advertisers such as AdMob and AppLovin; (2) in-app purchases are made by non-resident users substantiated by at least two non-conflicting indicators of non-residency (foreign payment method, foreign IP address, foreign app store territory, foreign address); and (3) the corporation does not supply or promote the service for consumption in Canada. In-app revenues sourced from Canada or Quebec consumers remain taxable at the combined 14.975% rate (GST 5% plus QST 9.975%).

No real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction controls, government pre-approval, or digital stamping mandate exists in Canada or Quebec. Invoices must state the supplier's BN, GST/QST registration numbers, invoice date, vendor/receiver identity, description, tax base, and tax amounts. Records must be retained in readable electronic form for six years under the Excise Tax Act and Quebec Tax Administration Act. Mandatory electronic filing applies to corporate income tax and GST/HST/QST returns for registrants exceeding revenue thresholds; Peppol-based B2B e-invoicing is optional under the Pan-Canadian B2B e-Invoicing framework.

CRA and Revenu Quebec may reassess GST/QST within 4 years of filing (extended indefinitely for misrepresentation, fraud, or waiver); income tax reassessment generally applies within 3-4 years by entity type. Improper export zero-rating triggers retrospective clawback of uncollected net tax plus compound daily interest at the prescribed rate. Civil penalties include: ITA s. 163(2) penalty of 50% of income tax avoided for false statements or omissions; Excise Tax Act s. 280 failure-to-file penalty of 5% of net tax plus 5% per complete month (maximum 12 months); Excise Tax Act s. 285 gross-negligence penalty of 25% of net tax (minimum CAD 100) for knowingly false statements; and mirror penalties under the Quebec Taxation Act and Tax Administration Act. Missing remittance or platform payout evidence within the 180-day repatriation benchmark raises audit risk and may result in full clawback of zero-rated input tax credits and net tax. Note: Canada does not impose a statutory 180-day FX repatriation deadline; the benchmark reflects CRA administrative practice, and accrued receivables are reportable on the accrual basis regardless of settlement timing.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a floating exchange rate regime with full current account convertibility under the Bank of Canada Act. Capital account transactions, including equity investments and profit remittances, generally permit free repatriation subject to prior notification for non-resident investments above CAD 100 million and mandatory FATCA/CRS reporting. Profit remittance hurdles include 15-25% dividend withholding tax under the Income Tax Act and beneficial ownership verification.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No central bank surrender obligation applies to most digital service or ad revenue exports. Repatriation occurs at market rates upon bank receipt of commercial invoices, proof of delivery, and AML documentation. Export permits under the Export and Import Permit Act are required solely for restricted goods; otherwise, banks process conversions freely with FINTRAC compliance.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada

Federal general corporate income tax rate: 15%. Quebec general corporate income tax rate: 11.5%. Combined general rate: 26.5%. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access a federal rate of 9% and a Quebec rate of 3.2% on eligible active business income within the applicable business limit, producing a combined rate of approximately 12.2%, subject to eligibility, business-limit allocation, associated-corporation rules, and income-type restrictions.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Canadian Part XIII withholding tax on taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident, subject to reduction under an applicable income tax treaty. Common treaty rates are 15% for qualifying portfolio holdings and 5% for qualifying corporate holdings, but the applicable treaty, ownership percentage, holding period, limitation-on-benefits provisions, beneficial-ownership test, and anti-avoidance rules must be verified.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

Both the T2 and CO-17 are generally due within six months after the corporation's fiscal year-end. Any balance is generally payable within two months after year-end. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally have three months to pay its balance if statutory conditions are satisfied, although the return remains due within six months.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not use a simple statutory Local File/Master File threshold equivalent to all OECD jurisdictions. Under Income Tax Act section 247, contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is required to support arm's-length pricing for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions. Form T106 reporting generally applies where the aggregate reportable transactions with non-residents exceed CAD 1,000,000 or other statutory reporting conditions are met. CRA may request documentation, which generally must be provided within three months. Canadian entities subject to the OECD country-by-country reporting regime may have additional CbC reporting obligations, generally based on consolidated group revenue of EUR 750 million or more.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to some offshore jurisdictions. Substance is assessed through the Income Tax Act transfer-pricing rules, the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty residence and beneficial-ownership principles, purpose and abuse analysis, and judicial doctrines concerning sham transactions and legal relationships. The corporation should maintain Canadian decision-making, employees or qualified contractors, control over game development and IP risks, board governance, commercial contracts, bank accounts, accounting records, and evidence that intercompany services and IP arrangements reflect actual functions, assets, and risks.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Return

Statutory sanction: Civil reassessment of tax, interest, gross-negligence penalties generally equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit, and possible prosecution under the Income Tax Act. Tax evasion can result in fines ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment of up to five years, subject to the charged offence and court determination.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face prosecution for personal participation, authorization, acquiescence, or wilful blindness. Corporate tax debt does not ordinarily become director debt solely because of office, but personal criminal liability, civil penalties, restitution, and prosecution remain possible. Travel restrictions are not an automatic tax sanction but may arise from bail, court orders, immigration consequences, or enforcement of a criminal sentence.
Failure to Remit Payroll, GST/HST, QST, or Part XIII Withholding

Statutory sanction: Assessment of the unremitted amount, interest, penalties, director-liability exposure, and potential criminal prosecution. Part XIII withholding assessments can include the required tax plus interest and penalties. GST/HST and QST amounts collected and not remitted may be treated as trust amounts.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable under federal and Quebec statutory provisions for unremitted source deductions and certain trust amounts if statutory due-diligence defences are unavailable. Personal assets may be pursued after corporate recovery steps. Imprisonment requires criminal prosecution and conviction; a travel ban is not automatic.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation and Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: Transfer-pricing adjustment under Income Tax Act section 247, denial of deductions, income inclusion, interest, penalties generally equal to 10% of the adjustment in qualifying circumstances, and possible secondary withholding-tax consequences. Repeated or abusive arrangements may trigger GAAR or treaty anti-abuse provisions.

Director personal liability: Directors are not ordinarily personally liable merely because a pricing adjustment is made. Personal exposure may arise for knowing participation in fraud, false statements, obstruction, or deliberate tax evasion. CRA may assess penalties against the corporation and pursue responsible individuals criminally.
Shell Company, Sham, or Artificial IP Structure

Statutory sanction: Denial of deductions, recharacterization of transactions, loss of treaty benefits, GAAR assessment, withholding-tax reassessment, transfer-pricing adjustments, interest, and civil penalties. Beneficial-ownership and residence claims may be rejected.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure where they knowingly participated in sham documentation, fraud, or evasion. Corporate separateness is generally respected for legitimate operations, but it does not protect individuals from criminal conduct, statutory director liability, or fraudulent-transfer consequences.
AML, Proceeds of Crime, and Sanctions

Statutory sanction: Potential FINTRAC compliance measures, administrative monetary penalties, transaction restrictions, criminal prosecution under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act or Criminal Code, forfeiture, and sanctions-related penalties. Corporate and individual consequences depend on the conduct and applicable regime.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be personally prosecuted for knowing participation, counselling, aiding, abetting, or directing unlawful transactions. Conviction can result in fines, forfeiture, imprisonment, and court-imposed travel restrictions. A travel ban is not automatic solely from an AML finding.
Improper SR&ED or Quebec Digital-Media Credit Claim

Statutory sanction: Denial or clawback of credits, reassessment of tax, interest, gross-negligence penalties, preparer penalties where applicable, and potential prosecution for knowingly false claims.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for deliberate false statements, fraud, or criminal participation. Ordinary corporate overclaiming without personal misconduct generally does not pierce the corporate veil.
Corporate Records, Beneficial Ownership, and Filing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Administrative penalties, filing penalties, denial of deductions or treaty relief, audit reassessments, compliance orders, and possible offences under federal or Quebec corporate, tax, and anti-money-laundering legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors may face statutory fines or prosecution for authorizing non-compliance and may lose the ability to rely on a due-diligence defence. Personal imprisonment or travel restrictions require applicable criminal proceedings or court orders and are not automatic consequences of a recordkeeping deficiency.
