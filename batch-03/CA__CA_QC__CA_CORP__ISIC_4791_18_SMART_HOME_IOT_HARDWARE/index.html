Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada — federal GST/HST and federal corporate income tax (Portal: CRA My Business Account, https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html); Revenu Québec — Quebec Sales Tax (QST/TVQ) and Quebec corporate income tax (Portal: Clé Revenu, https://www.revenuquebec.ca); Registraire des entreprises du Québec — corporate registry and Quebec Enterprise Number (NEQ), https://www.registreentreprises.gouv.qc.ca.

E-government filing portal

STEP 1: Incorporate under the Canada Business Corporations Act (federal) or the Quebec Business Corporations Act, or register an extra-provincial corporation with Corporations Canada; obtain the 9-digit federal Business Number (BN) from CRA via the online 'Register for a business number' service or Form RC1 (no fee, 2-5 business days). STEP 2: Register the corporation with the Registraire des entreprises du Québec to obtain the NEQ (mandatory for any corporation carrying on business in Quebec; electronic registration via the one-stop 'Guichet unique' service). STEP 3: Register the BN program accounts with CRA for GST/HST (RT program account) and, if hiring staff, payroll (RP); a foreign-owned Canadian corporation registers in its own name and the non-resident parent is not required to hold a BN unless separately carrying on business in Canada. STEP 4: Register with Revenu Québec for the QST account and Quebec corporate income tax account using the NEQ (Form LM-1-V for QST), electing to file the annual municipal/tax declaration; under the administrative one-stop service, federal BN/GST registration data is shared with Revenu Québec. STEP 5: File the initial T2 corporate income tax return within 6 months of year-end (typical first year-end can be extended up to 53 weeks from incorporation); QST returns are filed on the same frequency as GST returns. Foreign entities must also confirm whether a permanent establishment in Quebec is created, which would trigger full registration regardless of the $30,000 small-supplier threshold.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: A Business Corporation (Inc./Corp.) under federal or Quebec law is a separate legal taxpayer under the Income Tax Act (Canada) and the Tax Act (Quebec); no pass-through treatment applies (no equivalent of a US 'check-the-box' election; a corporation is taxed at the entity level on net income, federal general corporate rate 15% plus Quebec general rate 11.5%, with a combined effective rate of approximately 26.5-26.9% (general) and approximately 12.2% (CCPC active business income up to CA$500,000, subject to Quebec business limit rules)). Territorial Scope: A Canadian-incorporated corporation whose central management and control is in Canada is resident in Canada and taxed on worldwide income; foreign-source active business income may be sheltered by applicable tax treaties and the foreign affiliate rules (s. 113 ITA). Tax_Exempt_Zone: None — Canada and Quebec do not maintain special economic zones or tax holidays relevant to IoT hardware retail; the standard GST/QST regime applies in full.

Available election forms

Canadian tax law does not provide a check-the-box election. Available statutory elections include: (1) CCPC status and the associated-corporation rules (ITA s. 256) — an election to allocate the $500,000 business limit among associated corporations may be needed to preserve the small business deduction; (2) GST/HST s. 156 election for nil consideration on taxable supplies between closely related corporations, with the QST counterpart election for Quebec; (3) joint GST/HST 'closely related corporation' election to treat inter-corporate supplies as made for nil consideration; (4) ITA s. 85 rollover election for tax-deferred transfer of property into the corporation on incorporation; (5) ITA s. 216 election for non-residents to file on a net basis for rental/royalty income; (6) Quebec-specific election to allocate taxable income between the Quebec and other provincial permanent establishments; (7) for hybrid mismatch purposes, Canada's MLI and domestic hybrid rules (ss. 18.4-18.6 ITA) deny deductions in cross-border hybrid arrangements — no elective regime exists to override entity classification; foreign hybrid entities such as US LLCs are treated as corporations by the CRA unless the CRA's administrative position confirms pass-through status for treaty benefits.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CA$30,000 — the small-supplier threshold under ETA s. 240(1) and the QST Act: a person, including a non-resident carrying on business in Canada, is not required to register for GST/HST and QST until the total worldwide taxable supplies made in Canada exceed CA$30,000 in the current or preceding four calendar quarters; registration becomes mandatory upon exceeding the threshold. A non-resident supplier of goods delivered into Canada (e.g., cross-border e-commerce) remains subject to the same threshold; separately, the simplified non-resident GST/HST registration regime for digital services has a CA$30,000 threshold for digital supplies, but physical IoT hardware retail with a Quebec permanent establishment is governed by the standard threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods: Zero-rating applies under Part V of Schedule VI, s. 1(a) ETA to a supply of tangible personal property made by way of sale where the goods are exported 'as a result of the sale' and are delivered to a location outside Canada by the supplier, a common carrier, or a consignee acting on the supplier's behalf, or are exported by the purchaser within 30 days of delivery to the purchaser (ETA s. 1(b)). The IoT hardware retailer must ensure title and risk pass to the foreign buyer outside Canada and that goods physically leave the country. Services: Zero-rated commercial services (e.g., installation support, IoT hub configuration, interoperability software) are eligible where the recipient is a non-resident and not registered for GST/QST, the service is not in connection with real property or tangible personal property situated in Canada, and the service is not rendered wholly or substantially to a person present in Canada. The supplier may claim full input tax credits (ITCs) on expenses attributable to zero-rated exports; the same substantiation rules apply to QST.

  • Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) electronic export declaration, transmitted via a CBSA-approved service provider (mandatory for exports valued above CA$2,000; for goods below CA$2,000, an exemption may apply but the exporter must retain detailed shipment records)
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill / truck manifest (or other carrier-issued transport document showing the foreign destination port or address, carrier name, and movement data)
  • Commercial Invoice with full HS classification (NAICS 443142 / HTS 8517.79 or 8537.10 equivalents), Incoterms 2020 terms, unit prices, currencies, and statement of export
  • Destination-country import or customs release documentation (e.g., US CBP entry summary or broker release) where available, as secondary proof of arrival
  • Proof of foreign-currency settlement — bank transfer/remittance records, SWIFT MT103 messages, or credit card settlement records from the non-resident buyer, functionally equivalent to an inward remittance certificate

Canada and Quebec do not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction controls, government invoicing portals, or digital stamping as of the 2024-2025 compliance cycle. The federal government is developing a voluntary Pan-Canadian e-invoicing framework based on the PEPPOL 4-corner model (Budget 2023 and 2024 announcements), but adoption remains opt-in. Mandatory invoice content rules apply: every GST/HST registrant must issue a tax invoice under ETA s. 223 containing supplier name and BN, recipient name, date of invoice, description of goods/services, and tax amounts (simplified invoices permitted for supplies of CA$30 or less); Revenu Québec requires the supplier's QST registration number and, if known, the customer's QST number (QST Act s. 27). Electronic invoices are acceptable without pre-clearance, but all invoice, export, and accounting records must be retained for six years in machine-readable format (ETA s. 286; Tax Administration Act s. 36; CRA Information Circular IC-04-04) and produced on audit.

CRA and Revenu Québec actively audit export zero-rating under the 'evidence of export' test, frequently reviewing cross-border e-commerce records. If substantiation is deficient, zero-rating is reversed: the supply is reassessed as taxable at 14.975% combined GST+QST (or 5% GST if outside Quebec) and all associated ITCs previously claimed on inputs are clawed back without offset. Reassessment may extend back four years from the original notice of assessment (unlimited in the case of misrepresentation). Monetary penalties include: (i) 25% of the tax understated for false statements or omissions (ETA s. 280; QST Act equivalent through the Tax Administration Act); (ii) an additional 50% penalty for knowingly or under circumstances amounting to gross negligence making a false statement (ETA s. 285.1); and (iii) interest at the current prescribed rate (compound daily, approximately 8% for both CRA and Revenu Québec) running from the return due date. Organized export-fraud schemes (straw buyers, fake bill-of-lading, in-transit dummy goods) are referred to the Canada Border Services Agency and the Public Prosecution Service of Canada for fraud charges under the Criminal Code s. 380(1), punishable by imprisonment of up to 14 years, and civil penalties may be imposed under the Customs Act in addition to tax reassessments.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a fully free-floating currency with de jure and de facto convertibility on both current and capital account. There are no surrender requirements, no authorization requirements for profit remittances, no dividend flow restrictions, and no central-bank approval for inbound foreign direct investment or outbound capital repatriation. A Québec non-resident-owned corporation can freely receive share-subscription capital in CAD or foreign currency, convert via licensed financial institutions, and remit dividends subject only to Part XIII withholding tax (generally 25 percent, often reduced under the Canada-US or Canada other-jurisdiction income tax treaty) and CRS/FATCA reporting. Practical convertibility constraints are limited to correspondent-bank compliance screening, sanctions lists administered by Global Affairs Canada, and OSFI's large-value transaction reporting thresholds under the PCMLTFA; no exchange-control permit or Bank of Canada approval is required for the target ISIC 4791 IoT hardware retail operation.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender timeline or mandatory conversion ratio applies to export receipts in Canada. Exporters of smart-home IoT hardware retain full discretion over the currency denomination, holding location, and conversion timing of their proceeds. Nevertheless, customs declarations through the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) must accurately report the value of exported goods for balance-of-payments statistics, and financial institutions report cash and wire transactions of CAD 10,000 or more to FINTRAC pursuant to PCMLTFA. The exporter may maintain foreign-currency operating accounts in Canada without deemed-disposition tax consequences; the Income Tax Act Section 128.1(4) and Section 27 rules govern functional-currency elections for foreign exchange gains or losses, while Section 95 attribution rules do not apply absent a controlled foreign affiliate structure.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as monetary and retail-payment supervisor, acting in coordination with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for deposit-taking licensing, and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for anti-money-laundering supervision under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: MEDIUM with material compliance preconditions. A non-resident electronic-money institution (EMI) or cross-border payment fintech cannot operate as a primary treasury rail without (i) FINTRAC registration as a Money Service Business (MSB) or (ii) a partnership with an OSFI-licensed Schedule I bank acting as the funded sub-account custodian. The Bank of Canada's Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) regime, once the registration window is fully sunset, may capture payments-service providers operating in Canada, requiring registration with the Bank of Canada and compliance with safeguarding and operational-risk rules. Viable commercial arrangement: utilize Wise Platform or Stripe Treasury-linked virtual accounts that terminate into a Canadian Schedule I bank account, ensuring all regulatory obligations (PCMLTFA reporting, RPAA registration, and PCI-DSS compliance) are contractually allocated to the licensed Canadian partner. Pure offshore EMIs that do not chain to an OSFI-regulated deposit-taking institution are effectively non-viable for the settlement of consumer sales taxes (GST/QST) and supplier payments.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 (total value of transactions with non-residents in a fiscal year triggers mandatory contemporaneous documentation)

Economic substance scope: Canada applies the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) requiring economic substance for transactions; no separate ESR but substance is critical for treaty benefits and to avoid recharacterization. For IoT retail, substance includes physical presence, employees, and real business activities.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Penalty of 50% of tax evaded (gross negligence penalty) plus interest; criminal prosecution under Income Tax Act s.239 – fines up to 200% of tax evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unpaid corporate taxes if they failed to withhold/remit; civil restitution, travel bans, and imprisonment for willful evasion
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: GAAR recharacterization of transactions; denial of treaty benefits; penalties up to 10% of transaction value; potential reassessment of income

Director personal liability: Directors may be held personally liable for taxes reassessed due to sham arrangements; travel bans and asset freezes under tax enforcement
AML Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1,000,000 for corporations; criminal penalties up to CAD 500,000 and imprisonment for officers

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally fined up to CAD 500,000 and imprisoned for up to 5 years for willful non-compliance; civil liability for damages
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Penalty of 10% of the net adjustment (if adjustment exceeds CAD 1 million); interest on reassessed tax; potential for double taxation

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for penalties if they knowingly participated in or authorized non-compliant TP arrangements; civil restitution and potential criminal charges
