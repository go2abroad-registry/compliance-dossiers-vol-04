Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Federal: Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – GST/HST Program, Business Registration Online (BRO), My Business Account portal. Provincial (Quebec): Revenu Québec – Quebec Sales Tax (QST), payroll deductions and corporate income tax administration via Clic Revenu and the Registre des entreprises du Québec (REQ).

E-government filing portal

A foreign-incorporated corporation must obtain a federal Business Number (BN) and GST/HST account from the CRA using Form RC148 (Non-Resident Registration) or Business Registration Online (BRO) before commencing commercial activity in Canada. In Quebec, the entity must also register with Revenu Québec under the System of Registration and the REQ, resulting in a Quebec Enterprise Number (NEQ) and a QST account number. Non-resident filers must appoint a Canadian representative with a completed Form RC391 (Power of Attorney) and maintain books and records in Canada or make them available electronically to the CRA and Revenu Québec.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: A Canadian business corporation is a separate legal taxpayer under the Income Tax Act (Canada) and the Taxation Act (Quebec). A resident Canadian corporation is subject to full Canadian tax on worldwide income, while a non-resident corporation is taxable only on Canadian-source business income attributable to a permanent establishment in Canada/Quebec. The provincial allocation follows Quebec's territorial sourcing and permanent establishment rules.

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a US-style 'check-the-box' entity classification election; a business corporation is per se a taxable Canadian corporation. Available statutory elections relevant to cross-border manufacturing/export entities include: (i) voluntarily registered GST/HST and QST elections to recover input tax credits on pre-operating and export-related expenses; (ii) the election under section 156 of the Excise Tax Act for nil consideration on supplies between closely related resident corporations (GST/HST neutralization); (iii) section 216 election (rental/royalty income) and section 217 election (Canadian pension benefits) for non-residents; and (iv) hybrid mismatch rules under sections 18.4-18.8 of the Income Tax Act (and Part 6.1) which neutralize double non-taxation arising from foreign entity classification mismatches.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000: A supplier is a 'small supplier' and is not required to register for GST/HST or QST if its total worldwide/taxable supplies made in Canada in the current calendar quarter and the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters do not exceed CAD 30,000. Once the threshold is exceeded, registration with the CRA (GST/HST) and Revenu Québec (QST) is mandatory. Non-residents carrying on business in Canada are eligible for the same threshold; voluntary registration is available to recover input tax credits. Online marketplace and payment facilitator rules apply where sales are mediated through digital platforms.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of tangible goods exported from Canada qualify for the 0% GST/HST rate under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act (and the mirror QST zero-rating provisions) when the supplier exports the goods before the supply, or delivers them to a common carrier for export, or the non-resident recipient exports the goods within the prescribed regulatory time limits. For the assembly of bionic robotic pet hardware (ISIC 3240 / NAICS 339930), the zero rating applies where possession is transferred in Canada and shipment is made to a destination outside Canada. Services performed directly on exported goods, and services supplied to non-residents not relating to Canadian real estate, are also zero-rated. The exporter must retain contemporaneous evidence of export, transport, payment and foreign destination; QST zero-rating follows the same documentary disciplines and the supplier must be a registered QST filer.

  • Customs Export Declaration: B13A Canada Customs Coding Form / Canadian Automated Export Declaration (CAED) or G19 Non-Declaration Report filed with the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) where shipment value exceeds CAD 2,000.
  • Transport and Commercial Documentation: Bill of Lading (ocean), Air Waybill (air), CIM/CMR consignment note (road/rail), commercial invoice and packing list showing the foreign consignee and exit port/terminal from Canada.
  • Inward Remittance / FX Repatriation Evidence: Bank SWIFT MT103, payment gateway settlement records, or foreign exchange conversion certificates demonstrating receipt of export proceeds from outside Canada within the commercial terms (benchmark period: 180 days).

Canada and Quebec do not currently impose a mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, electronic invoicing, digital stamping, or tax-authority API validation regime for B2B/B2C supplies. The CRA participates in the voluntary Pan-Canadian Trust Framework / Peppol e-invoice standard, and Revenu Québec accepts structured electronic invoices, provided the prescribed invoice content is complete and legible. Corporations must e-file GST/HST and QST returns through the CRA My Business Account and Revenu Québec portals; invoice data transmission to tax authorities is not required. Export invoices must show the supplier's BN/QST number, the zero-rated nature of the supply, and the foreign destination of the goods.

The CRA and Revenu Québec may reassess export zero-rating within the normal four-year limitation period, or beyond where misrepresentation, false statements, or fraud are established; unsubstantiated claims result in clawback of the GST/HST and QST input tax credits previously recovered in connection with exported goods, together with prescribed-rate interest. Civil penalties may range from 5% to 10% of the net tax misstatement for false or grossly negligent statements, plus a 50% gross negligence penalty under the Income Tax Act where income tax consequences are also adjusted. Failure to register penalties, suspension of QST registration, director's joint and several liability, and criminal prosecution for knowingly false documentation (including fines and imprisonment) may all be pursued. The 180-day FX repatriation benchmark serves primarily as an anti-avoidance and documentary substantiation threshold; commercial payment terms should be designed to repatriate export proceeds within that period.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is unconditionally convertible on both current and capital account under the IMF Article VIII obligations; there are no surrender requirements and no differential treatment between residents and non-residents for deposit, borrowing, or remittance purposes. The only statutory gateways are the Investment Canada Act (net benefit review for foreign direct investment in Canada) and Part XIII withholding tax under the Income Tax Act (generally 25% on dividend remittances unless reduced by treaty, e.g., 5% under the Canada-USA Treaty for qualified corporate shareholders). Profit remittance requires a valid board-drawn dividend resolution, Quebec corporate-law solvency certification, and a correctly executed Form NR601 if a waiver of the withholding obligation for treaty-reduced dividends is claimed. No prior Bank of Canada or Revenu Québec approval is necessary for the outward transfer of funds.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There are no statutory export-proceeds surrender rules in Canada. Exporters conducting the ISIC 3240 / NAICS 339930 business (assembly of bionic companion robotic animals) are free to retain foreign currency receivables in domestic foreign-currency bank accounts or in offshore accounts without mandatory conversion into CAD. The only compliance obligations are: (i) accurate B13A declaration of exported goods to the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) for goods over CAD 2,000, with the reporting requirement met at the time of export; (ii) preserving underlying commercial invoices, bills of lading, and proof of receipt of payment for customs audit purposes; and (iii) the corporate requirement under the Quebec Business Corporations Act (QBCA) to render financial accounts in CAD while presenting foreign-currency translation in accordance with IFRS. No timeline, ratio, or conversion mandate applies to export receipts.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as the monetary and clearing settlement authority; Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for prudential bank supervision; Department of Finance Canada as the statutory administrator of the Bank of Canada Act and the Payment Clearing and Settlement Act; Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) as the AML/KYC supervisor. Canada maintains no dedicated foreign-exchange control administration, reflecting the fully liberalized status of the Canadian dollar.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Medium: Licenced offshore financial-tech EMIs (Wise Platform, Airwallex, Wex, and credas) may legally support cross-border disbursements into and out of Canada only as registered Payment Service Providers or Money Service Businesses under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) with registration with Bank of Canada, and where applicable FINTRAC MSB licensing. They cannot be treated as a primary bank for maintaining statutory share capital or dividend remittance because they do not hold deposit-taking licences; they are feasible for payment collection on the Stripe/card rails and for bilateral FX conversion of trade receivables. Legal constraints include the obligation to keep all settlement funds in a segregated trust account with a Canadian Schedule I bank, the impossibility of opening a FI (financial institution) deposit account in the fintech's own name, and the requirement to maintain beneficial-ownership transparency with FINTRAC under PCMLTFA Part 1. The recommended architecture is a Tier-1 domestic bank account as the legal treasury anchor, with the fintech EMT used only for merchant-led fund routing and low-value B2B supplier payments.

Accept only wire transfers in CAD or convert at a Tier-1 bank's treasury desk; mandated USD/CNY transfers must be routed via clearing SWIFT MT103 to the Toronto or Montreal correspondent and then converted at or near the Bank of Canada daily reference rate with a fixed FX confirmation order. The wire message must state a purpose code of 'G20' (equity contribution) or 'G55' (intercompany direct investment) as recognized by the Bank of Canada's balance-of-payments account; the remitter must align the SWIFT tag 50F with the non-resident shareholder's legal name exactly as registered in the REQ registry. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium: Licenced offshore financial-tech EMIs (Wise Platform, Airwallex, Wex, and credas) may legally support cross-border disbursements into and out of Canada only as registered Payment Service Providers or Money Service Businesses under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) with registration with Bank of Canada, and where applicable FINTRAC MSB licensing. They cannot be treated as a primary bank for maintaining statutory share capital or dividend remittance because they do not hold deposit-taking licences; they are feasible for payment collection on the Stripe/card rails and for bilateral FX conversion of trade receivables. Legal constraints include the obligation to keep all settlement funds in a segregated trust account with a Canadian Schedule I bank, the impossibility of opening a FI (financial institution) deposit account in the fintech's own name, and the requirement to maintain beneficial-ownership transparency with FINTRAC under PCMLTFA Part 1. The recommended architecture is a Tier-1 domestic bank account as the legal treasury anchor, with the fintech EMT used only for merchant-led fund routing and low-value B2B supplier payments.
