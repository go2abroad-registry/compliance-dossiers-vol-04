Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), including Business Registration Online (BRO), My Business Account, and Represent a Client; Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA), including the Canada Export Reporting System (CERS) and the Canada Assessment and Revenue Management (CARM) portal, administers export and customs reporting. Ontario partnership registration is administered by the Ontario Ministry of Public and Business Service Delivery and Procurement and is separate from federal tax registration.

E-government filing portal

1. Register the Ontario limited partnership under the Ontario Limited Partnerships Act and obtain the applicable Ontario registry confirmation and business-name registration, where required. 2. Determine whether the partnership is carrying on business in Canada, has a permanent establishment, is making taxable supplies in Canada, imports commercial goods, or has Canadian-resident partners. 3. Apply to the CRA through Business Registration Online, or through the CRA non-resident registration process where BRO eligibility is unavailable, for a nine-digit Business Number (BN). The application generally requires the legal name, Ontario registration details, business address, mailing address, formation date, accounting period, responsible persons, partners, business activities, and representative authorization. 4. Open the appropriate CRA program accounts: RT for GST/HST, RM for import-export, RP for payroll, and other accounts only where applicable. An Ontario limited partnership generally does not obtain an RC corporate income-tax account merely because it exists, because a partnership is generally fiscally transparent for Canadian income-tax purposes. 5. Register as a non-resident GST/HST registrant if the statutory conditions are met, including the obligation to register despite non-resident status where required. A non-resident may need a Canadian security deposit unless the CRA waives or modifies the requirement under applicable administrative rules. 6. Obtain an RM account and complete CARM onboarding where the partnership imports commercial goods into Canada or otherwise has customs-account obligations. 7. Non-resident partners must separately assess Canadian income-tax filing, withholding, treaty, information-reporting, and section 216 or section 219 filing obligations. A BN is not a universal personal or corporate taxpayer identification number and does not replace a partner's foreign tax identification number.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada has no general United States-style check-the-box election for an Ontario limited partnership. A validly constituted partnership is generally treated as a flow-through arrangement for Canadian income-tax purposes: the partnership computes income under the Income Tax Act, and each partner reports its allocated share. The partnership generally files a T5013 Partnership Information Return where the statutory filing conditions are met. Classification may differ under foreign law or an applicable tax treaty, and Canadian residency and permanent-establishment status depend on legal facts rather than an elective classification. Relevant indirect-tax elections can include GST/HST elections for closely related corporations under section 156 of the Excise Tax Act, joint-venture elections under section 273 where statutory conditions are satisfied, fiscal-period and accounting-method elections, and prescribed elections affecting partnership allocations or corporate partners. These are not entity-classification elections and must not be used to override anti-avoidance, hybrid-mismatch, or treaty rules.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, including zero-rated supplies, measured under the Excise Tax Act small-supplier rules, generally over a single calendar quarter or over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters. The threshold does not apply in every circumstance: for example, certain taxi and ride-sharing operators, non-resident performers, and persons making specific taxable supplies may have special registration rules. A non-resident that is required to register must generally register even if the ordinary small-supplier threshold is not exceeded. Taxable supplies of related persons and associated entities may require aggregation under applicable rules.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods may generally be zero-rated where they are supplied for export and the statutory export conditions are satisfied, including delivery or transfer outside Canada, shipment by the supplier or by an authorized carrier to a destination outside Canada, or another prescribed export circumstance. The supplier must retain credible evidence linking the supply to export, such as customs records, carrier documentation, purchase orders, contracts, delivery confirmations, and proof of the destination outside Canada. Services supplied to a non-resident may be zero-rated only where the specific place-of-supply and export-service rules are satisfied. A service is not automatically zero-rated merely because the customer is non-resident or pays from an offshore bank account. Many services relating to real property situated in Canada, tangible personal property situated in Canada, litigation or regulatory matters in Canada, or services performed in Canada are subject to exclusions or special rules. Financial services, personal services performed in Canada, and services connected with Canadian activities require separate classification. GST/HST registrants claiming input tax credits or refunds must maintain records sufficient to substantiate both the zero-rated output treatment and the underlying inputs.

  • Canada Export Declaration through CERS, where an export declaration is required for the goods, including applicable controlled, restricted, or regulated goods and prescribed commercial exports
  • Commercial invoice or equivalent accounting document identifying the supplier, purchaser, goods, value, currency, destination, and terms of sale
  • Bill of lading, airway bill, courier record, or other carrier evidence showing shipment from Canada to a destination outside Canada
  • Packing list and transport or delivery records, where relevant to identifying the exported goods
  • Customs export proof, CBSA transaction or confirmation records, and import-country customs evidence where available
  • Written contract, purchase order, customer correspondence, and proof of delivery supporting the export condition
  • Proof of payment or settlement, where relevant to commercial substantiation; a Canadian inward-remittance certificate is not generally a statutory Canadian requirement
  • For offshore services: service agreement, customer residency evidence, scope and location-of-performance records, invoices, work-product delivery evidence, and records demonstrating that no statutory exclusion from zero-rating applies
  • Foreign exchange remittance evidence where required by a bank, lender, foreign jurisdiction, or internal control policy; Canada does not generally require an inward-remittance certificate as a condition for GST/HST zero-rating

Canada does not generally impose a nationwide real-time GST/HST invoice-clearance, digital-stamping, or transaction-level e-invoicing mandate for ordinary private-sector supplies. GST/HST registrants must issue invoices, receipts, agreements, or other supporting documentation containing prescribed information when required by the customer or for input-tax-credit substantiation, including the supplier's identity, GST/HST registration number where applicable, date, consideration, tax rate, tax amount or prescribed indication, and sufficient description of the supplies. Electronic records are permitted if they remain reliable, accessible, readable, and capable of audit. CRA electronic filing and payment requirements apply to specified registrants and circumstances, but they are not a real-time clearance system. Public-sector procurement may impose separate electronic-invoicing standards. Customs export reporting is submitted electronically through CERS when required, and customs accounting is administered through CARM; these systems do not constitute general domestic GST/HST invoice clearance.

Canada generally imposes no statutory requirement to repatriate export proceeds to Canada within a fixed number of days, so no mandatory Canadian FX-repatriation deadline applies. CRA may reassess GST/HST where export conditions are not met, evidence is inadequate, supplies were misclassified, or input tax credits or refunds were overstated. The registrant may be required to pay the reassessed net tax, applicable interest, and penalties. Administrative penalties can include failure-to-file, failure-to-remit, false-statement, gross-negligence, and repeated-failure penalties; a gross-negligence penalty can generally be 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit or refund, subject to the applicable statutory computation. Incorrectly claimed input tax credits or refunds may be denied or clawed back, with reassessment generally subject to the applicable limitation period and extended periods for misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, wilful blindness, or fraud. Tax evasion, fraudulent claims, obstruction, or knowingly false records may result in prosecution, fines, imprisonment, and director, partner, or third-party liability under applicable legislation. CBSA can separately impose customs penalties, demand duties and taxes, seize goods, or pursue enforcement for inaccurate export declarations or prohibited conduct.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible for both current and capital account transactions. There are no general exchange control restrictions under the Currency Act or the Bank of Canada Act. Profit remittance for foreign-owned limited partnerships is permitted freely, subject to withholding tax obligations under the Income Tax Act (Canada) and applicable tax treaty provisions. A Canadian limited partnership with non-resident partners may distribute profits without Bank of Canada approval; however, the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) may apply anti-avoidance rules, including the foreign accrual property income (FAPI) regime and thin capitalization limitations, if the transaction lacks substantial economic substance. For regulated financial sectors, the Bank Act requires OSFI approval for foreign bank representative offices or branches, but that does not apply to a registered agent/offshore secretarial enterprise. No material hurdle exists to convert CAD to foreign currency for commercial payments or dividends.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory requirement for the surrender of export proceeds to the Bank of Canada or any government authority. Exporters and service providers may retain foreign currency earnings in offshore or domestic accounts without mandatory conversion or repatriation timelines. The only applicable obligations are tax reporting: amounts received must be reported in CAD using the Bank of Canada daily exchange rate for income tax purposes, and transactions over CAD 10,000 or that otherwise trigger the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) must be reported to FINTRAC. For cross-border service receipts relating to registered agent and offshore secretarial activities, a reasonable transfer pricing arrangement must be documented under section 247 of the Income Tax Act.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) in coordination with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC); foreign exchange administration is decentralized with no standalone FX control authority, while the Minister of Finance retains residual authority under the Currency Act and the Special Economic Measures Act (SEMA).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium feasibility with legal constraints. Non-regulated offshore electronic money institutions (EMIs) and digital banks not chartered under the Bank Act cannot accept deposits in Canada or provide Canadian domestic payment accounts without registration as a foreign money services business (MSB) with FINTRAC. An offshore EMI may be used only as a supplementary collection rail for non-Canadian clients, provided: (i) the EMI is licensed in its home jurisdiction and does not hold itself out as a Canadian bank; (ii) the Canadian LP does not maintain its primary operating account there; (iii) client funds are not commingled; and (iv) all transactions are reported to FINTRAC if they exceed the prescribed thresholds. Practical viability is rated as Medium-High for receiving payments in USD, EUR or GBP from offshore jurisdictions, but Low where the account is used to pay Canadian suppliers or employees because of the absence of provincial regulatory protection and on-demand real-time settlement through the Canadian payment system.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital injections from parent entities or foreign investors into the Canadian LP must be effected via SWIFT MT103 (or equivalent TELEX/CHIPS) from a bank in the investor’s jurisdiction to the LP’s designated Canadian bank account. The wire message must explicitly state the Ontario Limited Partnership’s legal name, the registered office address, and the LP number. For capital contributions, the purpose code should use the ISO 20022 Business Rule for 'Investment' or the Canadian bank’s internal purpose code equivalent to 'Foreign direct investment / capital contribution'. In practice, banks require a completed 'Purpose of Remittance' declaration keyed to one of the CRA international transaction codes: Use code 'A0400 – Capital contributions to Canadian residents' (or bank-equivalent 'Equity Injection') where the receipt creates a permanent capital interest. For operating expense reimbursements or shareholder loans, use code 'B0400 – Loans from non-residents' or 'B0500 – Parent company finance'. The LP’s onboarding package must include the partnership agreement, certificate issued under the Limited Partnerships Act (Ontario) or a current registry profile, if applicable. All capital injections must be recorded in CAD at the Bank of Canada daily exchange rate and properly classified as contributed capital on the partnership’s books. FINTRAC reporting applies if any single transaction or series is CAD 10,000 or greater (or equivalent in foreign currency) or if the transaction triggers an STR.For trade and operational disbursements, the LP may use the following clearing rails: (1) SWIFT MT103 for direct cross-border supplier payments, professional fees, and statutory government filing fees; the SWIFT tag 71A must be 'OUR' only if the LP absorbs the bank charges, and tag 70 must include an unmasked invoice or contract reference to avoid AML referrals; (2) Commercial Letters of Credit (LC) for substantial procurement or subadvisory contracts; the issuing bank will request the Ontario LP’s certificate of good standing, the underlying commercial contract, and detailed description of the goods/services; (3) SWIFT MT202/MT202 COV for interbank transfers between the LP’s CAD account and its foreign currency clearing account, provided that the originator, intermediary, and beneficiary are properly disclosed; (4) Fedwire/ACH if the LP opens a US-dollar account with a Canadian bank having US clearing affiliate (e.g., RBC, BMO, TD), which allows same-day USD settlement with FTIN (Frequently Targeted International Non-Bank) screening; (5) CAD domestic rails (Lynx) for payments to Ontario government ministries (e.g., Ontario Business Registry filings), though EFT is preferable; and (6) International ACH or SWIFT gpi with the ‘gpi’ tag for traceability. For payments qualified as registered agent fees to an offshore secretarial affiliate, the transfer should be annotated as 'Contractual fee for statutory corporate services' with supporting invoice retained to satisfy transfer-pricing documentation. The LP must ensure the beneficiary bank account is in the affiliate’s name and not a private trust account unless explicitly documented.Foreign exchange forwards (CAD vs USD, CAD vs EUR) transacted through a Canadian Schedule I bank or registered Canadian securities dealer for periods up to 12 months, with no speculative purpose, used solely to hedge forecasted fee receipts or capital repayment obligations.Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) in exotic offshore vehicles (if the LP holds minor currencies such as CNY or RUB) executed with a Canadian bank through its structured products desk, only where a deliverable forward is not legally feasible; NDFs may be used solely for economic hedging of an invoiced receivable or payable, with clean-up clauses consistent with Canadian Bankers Acceptance rules and CSA derivatives licensing.Cross-currency swaps (CAD/USD or CAD/GBP) used for a matched asset-liability hedge, typically limited to a notional equal to the principal external foreign-currency debt or a long-term service contract; swap documentation must comply with ISDA Master Agreement and the Canadian Derivatives Regulations under the Derivatives Act (Ontario) where applicable.FX options (European-style vanilla calls/puts) via a CIRO-regulated dealer, purchased for protection of large receivables and not for volatility speculation; the premium cost is deductible only if the hedge relates to income-earning cross-border activities.Foreign currency bank accounts as natural hedge: holding operating funds in USD, EUR or GBP in a Canadian chartered bank with no conversion lock; this is not a derivative but is expressly permissible and effectively reduces FX revaluation exposure.Swap or forward contracts embedded in a commercial LC or multi-currency term loan with the same Canadian bank, structured as a back-to-back funding hedge tied to the same underlying disbursement.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium feasibility with legal constraints. Non-regulated offshore electronic money institutions (EMIs) and digital banks not chartered under the Bank Act cannot accept deposits in Canada or provide Canadian domestic payment accounts without registration as a foreign money services business (MSB) with FINTRAC. An offshore EMI may be used only as a supplementary collection rail for non-Canadian clients, provided: (i) the EMI is licensed in its home jurisdiction and does not hold itself out as a Canadian bank; (ii) the Canadian LP does not maintain its primary operating account there; (iii) client funds are not commingled; and (iv) all transactions are reported to FINTRAC if they exceed the prescribed thresholds. Practical viability is rated as Medium-High for receiving payments in USD, EUR or GBP from offshore jurisdictions, but Low where the account is used to pay Canadian suppliers or employees because of the absence of provincial regulatory protection and on-demand real-time settlement through the Canadian payment system.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: No statutory local-file monetary threshold is codified in the Income Tax Act; however, CRA requires contemporaneous documentation whenever a non-resident transaction occurs. The administrative trigger for filing CRA Form T9016 (Schedule 40) is where the aggregate gross revenue of the Canadian partnership exceeds CAD 5 million and covered transactions with non-residents exceed CAD 1 million for tangible goods, CAD 250,000 for intangible property and services, or CAD 250,000 for financial transactions.

Economic substance scope: Canada lacks a separate Economic Substance Regulations framework; however, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA s. 245) and the 'economic substance' doctrine recharacterize transactions lacking genuine commercial substance. The Canada Revenue Agency may disregard a limited partnership that functions exclusively as a secretarial mailbox with no real operational office in Ontario, attribute income to the general partner, or deny treaty benefits under the OECD Multilateral Instrument (MLI).

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Wilful Blindness

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act, s. 239: fine of an amount between 50% and 200% of the evaded tax, or between CAD 50,000 and CAD 200,000, and imprisonment for up to five years. Civil penalties under s. 163(2) add 50% of the understated tax; failure to file penalties may exceed 17% of unpaid taxes.

Director personal liability: CRA may assess directors or general partners personally under s. 227.1 for unremitted source deductions or Part XIII amounts. Convicted individuals may be subject to travel bans, extradition, and civil restitution orders under the Mutual Legal Assistance in Criminal Matters Act.
Shell Company / Sham Partnership

Statutory sanction: GAAR reassessment disallowing all tax benefits; penalties under s. 163(2) of the Income Tax Act plus interest; anti-money laundering administrative penalties under PCMLTFA can reach CAD 1 million for individuals and CAD 2 million for entities. Criminal fraud under the Criminal Code carries up to 10 years imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Courts may pierce the corporate veil where the partnership is a sham, making general partners personally liable for all debts and torts. Directors may be prohibited from acting as directors under s. 118 of the Canada Business Corporations Act.
AML/KYC Compliance Failure (Registered Agent)

Statutory sanction: Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1.5 million for non-first-time violations; criminal money laundering (Criminal Code s. 462.31) is punishable by imprisonment up to 10 years. The winning party may also face regulatory license revocation under Ontario's Regulated Business Licensing framework for registered agents.

Director personal liability: General partners and directors named as 'persons who directed or participated' in the violation face personal fines up to CAD 500,000; regulators may issue compliance orders and asset freezes. Repeat offenders may be barred from carrying on business in Ontario.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s. 247: the same-level adjustment and recharacterization; a transfer pricing penalty of 10% of the net adjustment (without reduction for taxes paid in a treaty partner state) applies where the gross transaction amount exceeds CAD 1 million and the taxpayer fails to sustain reasonable efforts. Interest is compounded daily on reassessed tax.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners may be jointly liable for the transfer pricing penalty if they knowingly participated in the false statement (s. 247(3) and s. 227.1). In egregious cases, the court can order restitution of the tax avoided plus a civil penalty equal to 10% of the total adjustment.
