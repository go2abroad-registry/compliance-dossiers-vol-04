Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), including the Business Registration Online service and My Business Account portal, for federal Business Numbers and GST/HST accounts; Revenu Quebec (Revenue Quebec), including its online registration and tax services portals, for Quebec Sales Tax (QST) accounts.

E-government filing portal

A foreign corporation carrying on business in Canada must obtain a nine-digit CRA Business Number (BN) and register the relevant program accounts. The corporation should file CRA Form RC1, Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts, or use the CRA Business Registration Online service where eligible. A non-resident corporation may need to contact the CRA directly if online registration is unavailable. A GST/HST account is opened under the BN using program identifier RT. The corporation must separately register for QST with Revenu Quebec where it makes taxable supplies in Quebec and meets the applicable registration requirements; the QST account is generally administered under a Quebec identification number. The corporation must maintain Canadian business, banking, accounting, invoicing, customs, and representative records and identify a Canadian service address or authorized representative where required. Importers and exporters may also require a CBSA import-export program account and a customs client identifier through the Canada Border Services Agency's CARM system. Income tax corporation identification is generally tied to the CRA BN and the corporation's T2 filing obligations; a separate universally applicable federal TIN certificate is not generally issued in the same manner as a United States EIN.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no general United States-style check-the-box election. A Canadian corporation is normally taxed as a separate legal entity, and a foreign corporation carrying on business in Canada is subject to Canadian corporate income tax on taxable Canadian income, subject to treaty relief. Specific elections may apply to particular transactions or classifications, including section 85 rollovers, section 86 reorganizations, foreign affiliate elections, capital cost allowance elections, GST/HST election forms, and Quebec tax elections. Canadian hybrid mismatch rules under the Income Tax Act address deduction/non-inclusion and duplicate deduction arrangements; they do not provide a general election to convert a corporation into a pass-through entity. The corporation should obtain separate GST/HST and QST registrations rather than relying on its corporate incorporation certificate.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, including zero-rated supplies, during a single calendar quarter or over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters is the principal small-supplier threshold for GST/HST registration. Quebec generally uses a CAD 30,000 threshold for QST registration based on taxable supplies made in Quebec. The threshold analysis is entity-specific and may require aggregation of associated persons or related business activities. A non-resident may be required to register earlier where it is carrying on business in Canada or makes taxable supplies in Canada and does not qualify as a small supplier.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Canada are generally zero-rated for GST/HST where the supplier establishes that the goods were exported and the statutory export conditions are satisfied. The supplier must retain evidence such as customs export records, carrier documentation, bills of lading or airway bills, commercial invoices, customer and destination details, and proof that the goods were delivered or made available outside Canada. Goods sold to a non-resident but delivered in Canada are not automatically zero-rated. Services supplied to a non-resident may be zero-rated only where a specific statutory rule applies, such as qualifying advisory, professional, financial, or other prescribed services; services physically performed in Canada, services connected with Canadian real property, and many services relating to tangible personal property in Canada may remain taxable. QST treatment must be analyzed separately under Quebec rules. Exporters may claim input tax credits for eligible GST/HST incurred in commercial activities and may claim eligible QST input tax refunds or credits, subject to registration, documentation, and allocation requirements.

  • Commercial invoice identifying exporter, purchaser, goods, value, currency, destination, and terms of sale
  • Bill of lading, airway bill, courier manifest, or other carrier evidence of shipment outside Canada
  • Packing list and product description, including quantity and harmonized tariff classification where applicable
  • Proof of delivery or destination outside Canada and customer correspondence supporting the export transaction
  • Import clearance or equivalent destination-country evidence where reasonably available
  • Accounting records reconciling the zero-rated invoice, export record, payment, and inventory movement

Canada and Quebec do not generally impose a universal real-time electronic invoicing, government clearance, API transmission, or digital tax-stamping mandate for ordinary domestic B2B or B2C transactions. Tax invoices must contain the prescribed GST/HST and QST information, including supplier identity, registration numbers where applicable, date, consideration, tax amount or rate, and sufficient transaction description. Electronic invoices are generally acceptable if integrity, authenticity, accessibility, and retention requirements are satisfied. Certain public-sector procurement arrangements, payment platforms, customs systems, or sector-specific programs may impose separate electronic data requirements. GST/HST and QST returns are filed through CRA and Revenu Quebec systems, respectively; filing frequency depends on the assigned reporting period. Records must generally be retained for at least six years after the end of the relevant year, subject to longer periods for unresolved matters or specific records.

Canada generally imposes no universal statutory requirement that export proceeds be repatriated within 180 days and no general mandatory foreign-exchange repatriation certificate for GST/HST zero-rating. CRA and Revenu Quebec may deny zero-rating, assess uncollected GST/HST or QST, deny input tax credits or refunds, and assess interest where the exporter cannot prove the statutory export conditions or where the transaction was mischaracterized. Incorrect GST/HST returns may attract penalties, including penalties for false statements or omissions, repeated failure to file, failure to remit, and gross negligence. Fraudulent or knowingly false conduct may result in civil penalties and prosecution under the Excise Tax Act, the Income Tax Act, or applicable Quebec tax legislation. CRA may generally reassess GST/HST within the applicable normal period, commonly four years for a registrant, with extended or unlimited periods in cases involving misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, wilful blindness, fraud, or conduct intended to evade tax. Revenu Quebec may apply comparable reassessment, penalty, interest, and prosecution rules under Quebec tax legislation. Amounts previously refunded or credited may be clawed back together with arrears interest and penalties.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full currency convertibility under IMF Article VIII for both current and capital account transactions. Profit remittance by a non-resident shareholder is not subject to exchange-control barriers, but Canadian withholding tax under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act applies: 25% on dividends absent treaty relief, typically reduced to 5% or 15% under applicable tax treaties. Quebec imposes a parallel provincial withholding tax, and a non-resident corporation may need to file a Canadian and Quebec tax return to recover treaty overpayments. There is no prior approval requirement for repatriation of invested capital, share subscription proceeds, or trade-related payments; however, large cross-border transactions may be captured by FINTRAC reporting thresholds and the Canada Payments Act screening requirements.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no statutory export-proceeds surrender or mandatory conversion regime. Exporters may lawfully retain all foreign currency export receipts and are under no obligation to convert them back to Canadian dollars. Nevertheless, exporters must meet record-keeping and reporting duties: exports exceeding CAD 2,000 per shipment must be reported through the Canadian Exporters' Database / E-Export declaration, and any cash or monetary instrument cross-border transfer of CAD 10,000 or more must be reported to FINTRAC. No forced conversion or central-bank deposit requirements exist for wooden-puzzle or general goods exporters.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as the central bank; no dedicated foreign-exchange-control body exists. Foreign exchange and anti-money-laundering reporting are administered by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), with trade data reporting to Statistics Canada and the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium. Foreign digital EMIs (e.g., Wise, Airwallex, Payoneer) may lawfully manage cross-border transactions and provide currency conversion, but they are not chartered banks under the Bank Act and may not take Canadian deposits as a primary business. To provide money remittance to Canadian businesses, the EMI must be registered with FINTRAC as a money services business (MSB), and the Canadian company must hold a separate account at a Tier 1 Canadian bank for payroll, GST/QST, and regulatory escrow. Legal constraints include inability to access Lynx (the Canadian high-value payment system) and limited eligibility for commercial letters of credit; therefore, an EMI is prudent only as a secondary settlement rail for small-value international supplier payments and Amazon/Stripe proceeds sweep.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound shareholder or parent-company capital must be wired via SWIFT MT103 using the ISO20022 or interbank purpose code for capital contribution ('CAPT', 'A20', or '4400' as applicable), with the corporate name and account number of the beneficiary clearly stated. The receiving Canadian bank will conduct OFAC, FINTRAC, and sanctions screening. Funds settle through Lynx (Canada's high-value payment system) or the Real-Time Rail (RTR) for CAD, and in USD through the internal bank clearing or SWIFT connection. To avoid KYC blockages, the remitter must provide a board resolution, a subscription agreement, evidence of the ultimate beneficial owner's identity, and a legal opinion confirming no exchange-control authorization is needed. Banks generally verify that the capital is recorded as share capital or contributed surplus for corporate-law and tax-accounting consistency.Outbound trade settlements can be executed through SWIFT MT103 wire transfers, documentary collections (DP/DA), commercial letters of credit, and supply-chain finance instruments. For the wooden-puzzle business, typical disbursements include raw-material imports (laser-cut wood panels, hinges, packaging) and machinery spare parts. SWIFT MT103 wires must include the beneficiary bank BIC, SWIFT/IBAN-equivalent account, purpose code ('GFIL' for intercompany funding, 'TRAD' for trade-related services, or 'MDIS' for merchandise trade), and the ISIC/NAICS reference (ISIC_3240 / NAICS 339930) in narration. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Combined 21% for a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC): federal 9% after general rate reduction and small business deduction; Quebec 12% on active business income up to the provincial business limit of CAD 500,000 per annum. For a taxable corporation over the limit, the combined rate is 30% (federal 15% plus Quebec 26.5% are separately computed but the effective combined rate for Canadian sources approximates 30%).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% statutory withholding tax under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (Canada) on deemed dividends paid by a Canadian corporation to a non-resident shareholder. No provincial withholding tax is levied by Quebec; the federal Part XIII rate is exclusive.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 (total annual gross revenue or threshold of transaction aggregates). CRA requires contemporaneous documentation (Local File and Master File) when the aggregate value of intercompany transactions exceeds CAD 1,000,000 per transaction class and the corporation has common control with the foreign counterpart. Master File is required when Canadian group consolidated revenue exceeds CAD 20,000,000.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not enforce a standalone Economic Substance Regulation (ESR); however, under GAAR, CRA may disregard arrangements lacking genuine business substance where the primary purpose is to obtain a tax benefit (s.245 ITA). CRA also requires management and control to reside in Canada for residency. For Quebec, Revenu Québec applies substance-over-form jurisprudence and anti-avoidance rules (s.1079.1.2 of the Taxation Act). The wooden-puzzle manufacturer must hold real premises, productive laser equipment, and evidence of executive decision-making within Quebec.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Under section 239 of the Canadian Income Tax Act, a conviction for tax evasion carries a fine from 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment of up to five years. Revenu Québec statutory counterpart (s. 1039 of the Taxation Act) imposes at minimum 100% of the amount evaded plus a civil restitution order.

Director personal liability: The directors and officers may be jointly and severally liable for the unpaid corporate tax under s. 227(5) ITA and Quebec equivalent; CRA can also obtain a Mareva-type asset freeze and obtain a departure order restricting travel by the offending director until the court enters an approval order.
Shell Company Misuse and Economic Substance Failure

Statutory sanction: CRA may apply General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s.245) to recharacterize the arrangement, eliminating deductions and imposing a penalty equal to 25% of the tax benefit denied. Revenu Québec imposes a similar GAAR penalty under s.1079.1.2.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false statutory declarations are exposed to strict liability penalties under s.163.2 ITA (culpable omission) of CAD 100 plus the tax avoided; willfully continuing the shell arrangement may trigger the s.239 evasion offence. Use of an offshore legal wrapper to obscure the real owner allows the court to lift the corporate veil and hold the ultimate beneficiary personally responsible for the entire Quebec tax base.
Failure to Withhold and Remit Non-Resident Tax

Statutory sanction: The payer corporation is liable for the full Part XIII unpaid amount plus daily interest and penalties; under section 227(9) ITA the failure to remit withheld sums attracts a penalty equal to 10% of an original failure amount plus additional indirect taxes.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for unremitted non-resident withholding taxes under s.227(5) ITA without having to pierce the veil; Revenu Québec executes any Quebec taxes into the director's personal property, including seizing residential real estate owned by the director. Court-imposed travel bans are common during the proceedings to enforce personal liability.
Façade Contract Scheme in Transfer Pricing

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess to attribute the Canadian and foreign profit shares under arm's-length rule s.247(2), impose the 25% GAAR penalty on the avoided benefit, and add transfer-pricing penalty under s.247(3) (10% of the gross adjustment amount when the taxpayer did not make reasonable efforts to apply arm's-length price).

Director personal liability: Because s.247(3) penalties are direct corporate penalties, directors can be held civilly liable for the resulting deficiency if they approved the pricing and are found not to have acted in good faith; serious abuse generates criminal culpability for fraud and breach of fiduciary duty. Quebec authorities can invoke the Oppression Remedy under the Business Corporations Act (QC) to impose personal liability for repayment to the corporation of the avoided taxes.
