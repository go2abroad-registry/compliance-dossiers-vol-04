Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — federal tax administration and GST/HST registrar; digital portal: My Business Account and Business Registration Online (BRO) at canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html. The Ontario Ministry of Finance (OMF) does not operate a separate front-line registration for the corporation's income tax or HST: Ontario corporate income tax is administered by the CRA under the Tax Administration Agreement (TAA), and the Ontario HST component is administered by the CRA under the Comprehensive Integrated Tax Coordination Agreement (CITCA). The single registration window is therefore the CRA.

E-government filing portal

A foreign-owned Ontario Business Corporation obtains its statutory identifiers exclusively through the CRA's single Business Number (BN) framework. Procedure: (1) Apply for a BN by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Related Accounts) or using Business Registration Online (BRO), designating a Canadian contact or registered agent where the corporation has no Canadian operational presence; note the Ontario Business Corporations Act (OBCA) requires at least 25% of directors to be resident Canadians (at least one where the board has fewer than four directors), and the Canada Business Corporations Act (CBCA) imposes a comparable resident-Canadian rule under the federal regime. (2) Register the corporate income tax program account (RC) under the BN; the T2 Corporation Income Tax Return will be filed with CRA on behalf of both Canada and Ontario. (3) Register the GST/HST program account (RT) under the same BN where the corporation is not a 'small supplier' or where voluntary registration is elected to claim input tax credits; the Ontario HST is reported and remitted on the same RT account, with CRA acting as the collecting agent for the province. (4) Register the payroll (RP) and source-deduction accounts where Canadian employees are engaged, and the import/export account (RM) only if tangible goods cross the border. No separate Ontario TIN or VAT ID exists.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a check-the-box or fiscally-transparent election regime; a Business Corporation is statutorily a non-transparent, entity-level taxpayer, and partnership-style pass-through treatment is not electable. Elective mechanisms relevant to the Ontario AdTech corporation include: (i) ITA subsection 85(1)/(2) rollover elections for tax-deferred transfers of property into the corporation on incorporation or reorganization; (ii) ITA section 216 election for non-resident persons with Canadian rental or timber-royalty income to compute net-basis Canadian tax; (iii) ITA section 219 (Part XIV) branch-tax compliance framework for non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada; (iv) GST/HST elections, including the Quick Method election (Form GST74), reporting-period elections, and voluntary-registration or de-registration elections; and (v) Canada's hybrid-mismatch rules (BEPS Action 2 implementing provisions, generally effective for periods after 30 June 2022), which deny deductions or recharacterize payments where arrangements exploit fiscally transparent entities (e.g., US check-the-box disregarded LLCs) or hybrid financial instruments; these hybrid rules are mandatory and carry no elective opt-out, although taxpayers may structure to avoid triggering them. The general anti-avoidance rule (ITA s.245) and transfer-pricing rules (ITA s.247) supplement these mechanisms.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (the 'small supplier' threshold) — a person whose worldwide gross revenue from taxable supplies, including zero-rated supplies, in the immediately preceding four calendar quarters does not exceed CAD 30,000 is not required to register for GST/HST; registration becomes compulsory upon exceeding that threshold. A parallel CAD 30,000 threshold applies to non-resident digital-services and marketplace suppliers under the simplified foreign-supplier registration regime effective 1 July 2021. The Ontario AdTech corporation, once its taxable advertising, ad-exchange and SSP revenues exceed the threshold, must register for the RT account and charge 13% HST on supplies made to Canadian-resident recipients; voluntary registration is available to any person carrying on a commercial activity in Canada so as to recover input tax credits.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under the Excise Tax Act (ETA), Schedule VI, Part V, section 7, a supply of a service made to a non-resident person is zero-rated (0% GST/HST) unless the service: (i) is supplied to an individual non-resident physically present in Canada at the time the service is rendered, with limited exceptions; (ii) is, or is incidental to, a supply of real property situated in Canada; (iii) is, or is incidental to, a supply of tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time the service is rendered; or (iv) is rendered to a non-resident person registered for GST/HST under Subdivision d of Division V of Part IX of the ETA at the time the service is rendered. Programmatic ad-exchange, DSP and SSP services (ISIC 7310) exported to non-resident advertisers, publishers, agencies, DSPs or SSPs qualify for zero-rating where the recipient is a non-resident corporation or an individual non-resident and the service is not consumed at a Canadian situs. The registrant must obtain and maintain documentary evidence of the recipient's non-resident status (jurisdiction of incorporation, registered address, contractual representations, W-8-style certifications where applicable), commercial invoices, supply contracts, and payment evidence demonstrating receipt of consideration. Canada imposes no statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for service export proceeds; the 180-day benchmark retained in this dossier is the model's due-diligence convention for documenting receipt of export consideration.

  • Canada Export Declaration (CED) transmitted via the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) — required for exports of goods; not required for pure digital services but applicable where the SSP platform bundles tangible hardware, media or connected devices for export
  • Commercial invoice and executed service or supply agreement identifying the non-resident recipient, nature of the service, and export character of the supply
  • Bill of Lading or House Air Waybill (where tangible media or connected devices are exported; not required for pure digital services)
  • Proof of non-resident status of the recipient — certificate of incorporation or foreign registry extract, registered address, and beneficial-ownership attestation
  • Foreign-currency inward-payment evidence — SWIFT MT103, bank remittance advice, or ACH Direct advice confirming receipt of the export consideration
  • Recipient's GST/HST registration number, where relevant, and export exemption certificates or attestations retained in the RT account records

Canada has no real-time fiscal clearance, no digital stamping regime, and no mandatory CRA e-invoice pre-clearance API. The CRA accepts paper and electronic invoices; GST/HST registrants must include the BN/GST-HST registration number, invoice date, vendor and purchaser identification, description of supply, tax rate, and tax amount (or state 'GST/HST included'), and must retain supporting records in an accurate, complete, machine-readable format for at least six years. The Pan-Canadian E-Invoice Standard (Peppol BIS 3.0 / Universal Business Language) is a voluntary business-to-government and business-to-business interoperability framework being progressively adopted by federal and provincial procurement bodies; Ontario has no equivalent mandatory provincial clearance mandate. There is no fiscal interface or clearance gateway linked to SWIFT wire or ACH Direct payment rails.

CRA audits zero-rated export claims strictly and the registrant bears the onus of proving the non-resident status of the recipient and the export character of the supply. Where zero-rating is ineligible or inadequately substantiated, the supply is reassessed as taxable HST at 13% (Ontario) from the original supply date, with statutory arrears interest compounding daily. The ETA reassessment limitation period is four years from the filing date, but is unlimited where a return was filed with any misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, or willful default, or where fraud is established (ETA s.296(2)). Civil penalties include: the gross-negligence penalty of the greater of CAD 250 and 40% of the tax sought to be evaded (ETA s.285); false-statement penalties (ETA s.280.1); and, on the income-tax side, the ITA s.163(2) gross-negligence penalty equal to 50% of the understated tax attributable to false statements. Criminal prosecution may be initiated under ETA s.327/329 and ITA s.239, carrying fines up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for up to five years (two years less a day on summary conviction). A fraudulent export zero-rating claim exposes the taxpayer to retrospective clawback of both the federal 5% GST and the Ontario 8% provincial component, together with disallowance of related input tax credits previously claimed in connection with the export supply.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a free-floating exchange rate under the Bank of Canada Act. There are no exchange control statutes distinguishing current-account from capital-account convertibility. Dividends, interest, royalties, management fees, repatriated capital and liquidation proceeds are freely remitted in any convertible currency. The only practical hurdles are Part XIII withholding tax obligations under the Income Tax Act (Canada), documentation required for tax treaty relief, reporting obligations under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), the Canadian Payments Act, and Statistics Canada balance-of-payments reporting. Full current-account and capital-account convertibility exists for a Canadian Business Corporation.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory surrender requirement, conversion ratio, or prescribed timeline for export proceeds. A Canadian exporter may retain and deploy export receipts in foreign currency bank accounts, whether maintained in Canada or abroad. Conversion to Canadian dollars is voluntary and market-determined. FINTRAC requires reporting of international electronic fund transfers of CAD $10,000 or more and all large cash transactions, but this is an AML reporting duty only and does not operate as a surrender or conversion mandate.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), acting as fiscal agent for the Minister of Finance in the management of the Exchange Fund Account; the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) supervises federally regulated financial institutions.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low standalone feasibility rating. Canada does not recognise the European Electronic Money Institution (EMI) licence. A non-resident EMI seeking to source or settle Canadian-dollar payments must either register as a Money Services Business (MSB) with FINTRAC under the PCMLTFA, and comply with the Retail Payment Activities Act and upcoming Payment Service Providers Act, or operate through a Canadian chartered bank as its settlement and clearing agent. For an Ontario Business Corporation engaged in programmatic AdTech, using an offshore EMI as a direct treasury rail raises heightened correspondent-bank due diligence expectations and materially increases AML risk. The recommended architecture is a Canadian Schedule I bank account as the principal settlement account, supported by an authorised foreign branch or FINTRAC-registered MSB only for jurisdictions where local payment rails require it.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

For a capital injection by a non-resident shareholder into the Ontario Business Corporation, the remitting bank should send a SWIFT MT103 payment using the beneficiary Canadian bank's BIC, the Ontario company's legal name, account number, and full street address. Field 50K must contain the ordering customer's legal name and registered address; field 57A the beneficiary bank; field 70 must clearly state the economic purpose, for example: 'Capital Contribution — Issuance of Common Shares — ISIC 7310 Programmatic Advertising Platform'. ISO 20022 purpose code should be set to 'CAPT' (Capital Payment) or 'COUN' (Payment to a Company), with 'EQUI' used where the remitter records the transfer as equity investment. The verification package to be pre-delivered to the Canadian receiving bank consists of a certified board resolution approving the share issuance, the amended articles or share register, proof of identity and source of funds of the remitting shareholder, a copy of the wire instruction, and the company's latest financial statements. Transactions equal to or exceeding CAD $10,000 trigger FINTRAC international EFT reporting by the receiving financial institution.For cross-border settlement of programmatic advertising inventory purchases and publisher payouts, the primary clearing rail is SWIFT MT103 (serial or cover payment) routed through the Canadian bank's international treasury group. Domestic Canadian and United States disbursements should be effected via Payments Canada and ACH Direct, respectively, where settlement timing and account routing permit. Commercial Letters of Credit (MT700 / UCP600) are generally unsuitable for continuous microtransaction ad-auction settlements, but may be used for pre-negotiated inventory minimum-guarantee contracts and high-value publisher financing arrangements. Bulk payments should be aggregated into bank batch files, with each beneficiary identified by legal name, full account number, BIC/IBAN/ABA, and a precise remittance reference, such as 'Q3 Publisher Payout — Campaign ID #ADV-2025-11937 — Invoice #AR-2025-046'. Outbound disbursements above CAD $10,000 are subject to FINTRAC international EFT reporting.Deliverable FX forward contracts executed with a Schedule I Canadian bank for fixed-price cancellation of known USD revenue or payroll commitmentsNon-deliverable forwards (NDFs) for currencies without active deliverable markets, permitted only where the underlying programmatic ad transaction is documented and the NDF is ineligible for physical settlementCross-currency swaps and FX swaps for balance-sheet hedging of foreign-currency subscription receivables or intercompany loansVanilla FX options purchased for downside protection on forecasted USD / CAD conversion volumesFlexible forward contracts offered by Canadian bank treasury desks to net recurring DSP/SSP settlement flows on a rolling monthly cycle

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Low standalone feasibility rating. Canada does not recognise the European Electronic Money Institution (EMI) licence. A non-resident EMI seeking to source or settle Canadian-dollar payments must either register as a Money Services Business (MSB) with FINTRAC under the PCMLTFA, and comply with the Retail Payment Activities Act and upcoming Payment Service Providers Act, or operate through a Canadian chartered bank as its settlement and clearing agent. For an Ontario Business Corporation engaged in programmatic AdTech, using an offshore EMI as a direct treasury rail raises heightened correspondent-bank due diligence expectations and materially increases AML risk. The recommended architecture is a Canadian Schedule I bank account as the principal settlement account, supported by an authorised foreign branch or FINTRAC-registered MSB only for jurisdictions where local payment rails require it.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000; a reporting entity must file Form T106 (Information Return of Non-Arm’s Length Transactions with Non-Residents) when aggregate consideration for reportable non-arm’s-length transactions exceeds this threshold, and contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation must be prepared and held ready to support arm’s-length outcomes.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no separate Economic Substance Regulations comparable to Caribbean/offshore regimes, but the CRA applies the income tax general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR, subsection 245(2) of the Income Tax Act) and treaty-beneficial-ownership doctrines to disregard artificial structures lacking commercial purpose. A Canadian-resident AdTech company must maintain substantive employees, board decision-making, contracts, and operational risk in Canada to preserve treaty entitlement and avoid reassessment as a contractual shell.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Corporate Filing

Statutory sanction: Criminal tax evasion conviction: on indictment, a fine of between 100% and 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years; civil reconsideration may also trigger a gross negligence penalty of 50% of the understated tax and assessed interest on the unpaid balance.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorize, participate in, or knowingly acquiesce to false filings face joint and several criminal liability; CRA may obtain a Federal Court compliance or travel-restriction order where a director is identified as connected to an active tax evasion investigation.
Transfer Pricing Under-Documentation or Mis-Pricing

Statutory sanction: CRA may re-characterize or adjust the consideration under subsection 247(2) of the Income Tax Act; the penalty under subsection 247(3) is 10% of the net adjustment amount where documents have not been prepared and reasonable efforts were not made; additional penalties apply for late or missing Form T106 filings.

Director personal liability: A director who signs a false T106 or relies on a knowingly fabricated transfer pricing study may be assessed under gross negligence provisions; knowing participation in a sham margin arrangement can pierce the corporate veil and subject the director to civil damages for the resulting corporate assessment.
Unreported Part XIII Withholding on Profit Repatriation

Statutory sanction: The CRA can collect 25% of the gross amount as assessed withholding tax, plus statutory interest and penalties; failure to withhold may also result in an additional civil penalty and the denial of the payer’s deduction for the underlying payment in certain circumstances.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for the corporation’s unremitted Part XIII withholdings under subsection 227.1(1) of the Income Tax Act unless the director exercised due diligence; the CRA can issue a certificate of default and pursue realizable assets, garnishment wages, or a Federal Court travel embargo against the director.
Shell Company / Substance and Treaty Shopping Abuses

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty benefits under contemporary anti-treaty-shopping jurisprudence, GAAR reassessment under subsection 245(2), income reattribution to the deemed beneficial owner, and penalties for misrepresentation; the federal business registry may also dissolve the corporation for failure to maintain registered agent or active annual filing.

Director personal liability: A director who signs statutory declarations falsely representing Ontario residency or business substance can be subject to securities and corporate fraud sanctions; where the corporation is used as a façade for principal transactions, courts may pierce the corporate veil and hold directors personally liable for tax restitution, costs, and aggravated damages.
