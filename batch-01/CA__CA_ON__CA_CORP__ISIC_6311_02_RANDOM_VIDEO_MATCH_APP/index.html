Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — Business Registration Online (BRO) portal and My Business Account (GST/HST NETFILE); Ontario Ministry of Finance for Ontario corporate tax, administered by the CRA through the T2 Corporate Income Tax Return (Ontario Tax on Corporations).

E-government filing portal

Obtain a Business Number (BN) from the CRA via Business Registration Online (BRO), Form RC1, or by telephone. A Federal Business Corporation must first be incorporated under the Canada Business Corporations Act (CBCA) through Corporations Canada, then file an Extra-Provincial Registration in Ontario if the head office/operations are outside that province. Register for GST/HST once carrying on business in Canada or, for non-resident suppliers of digital services to Canadian consumers, once the CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold is exceeded. Non-residents must appoint a Canadian resident representative/canadian agent and provide a Canadian address for service. The GST/HST registration number is the BN followed by the 'RT' program identifier.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation (Canadian-resident corporations are taxed at the corporate level on worldwide income under Part I of the Income Tax Act; non-resident corporations are subject to Part I tax on Canadian-source business income and Part XIV branch tax on Canadian branch profits. Canada does not permit a check-the-box pass-through election; no LLC-style disregarded entity treatment is available for federal tax purposes).

Available election forms

No check-the-box election exists in Canada. Material statutory elections affecting tax identity include: (i) Income Tax Act section 216 election for non-residents to file on a net basis for Canadian rental income; (ii) section 219 treaty-based election to waive or reduce Part XIV branch tax (limitation on benefits provisions apply); (iii) Excise Tax Act section 156 election for closely related corporations to treat inter-corporate supplies as made for nil consideration for GST/HST; (iv) GST/HST Quick Method election; (v) simplified GST/HST registration regime for non-resident digital services suppliers under Excise Tax Act sections 211.1–211.3; (vi) hybrid mismatch election/ordering rules under Income Tax Act sections 18.2 and 18.21 (in force for losses arising in 2024 and subsequent years, with grandfathering rules).

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (the 'small supplier' threshold under Excise Tax Act section 148; a non-resident supplier of digital services to Canadian consumers must register once its worldwide taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in the preceding four calendar quarters or in a single calendar quarter; no separate Ontario/PST registration is required for HST-registered persons).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Excise Tax Act Schedule VI, a supply of a service to a non-resident is zero-rated (0% GST/HST) if the recipient is a non-resident who is not registered for GST/HST and the service is not performed in connection with real property situated in Canada or tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time of performance. For the video-match/social telephony app: (i) B2C supplies of intangible personal property (digital access, virtual credits) to foreign users are zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI where the place of supply rules locate the recipient outside Canada; (ii) B2B supplies to non-resident platform operators also qualify where the 'use in Canada' test is not triggered. The exporter must be a GST/HST registrant to claim input tax credits on zero-rated exports; the zero-rating claim requires demonstrable evidence of the non-resident status of the recipient and of delivery outside Canada.

  • Customs Export Declaration – Canada Customs Invoice / B13A Export Declaration (required only for tangible goods; for digital services, contractual and delivery evidence substitutes for customs clearance)
  • Commercial invoice / service agreement identifying the digital service and excluding Canadian real property or tangible property involvement
  • Electronic delivery logs (IP address country data, geolocation, app-store transaction records, server usage records) evidencing consumption outside Canada
  • Inward remittance certificate / bank wire or payment-gateway settlement statements (SWIFT, Apple IAP, Paymentwall reports) evidencing actual receipt of funds from the non-resident
  • Recipient non-resident status evidence (customer registration data, tax identification number or residence declaration, payment method country data)

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, government digital stamping, or API-based pre-approval e-invoicing requirement exists in Canada. E-invoicing is voluntary and follows the Pan-Canadian PEPPOL standard. Registrants above the small-supplier threshold must file GST/HST returns electronically via GST/HST NETFILE (including API/web services) or GST/HST TELEFILE; monthly filers must file within one month of the reporting period end. The CRA relies on post-filing transaction-level audit, information returns, and payment-rail data rather than pre-clearance of invoices.

Canada imposes no statutory 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for service or digital exports; the figure reflects a customary documentary benchmark, and revenue must be recognized on the accrual basis regardless of remittance timing. Zero-rating claims and related input tax credit refunds are subject to CRA audit and reassessment within the normal four-year limitation period (unlimited where misrepresentation or fraud exists). False or unsupported zero-rating claims attract: (i) a penalty under Excise Tax Act section 285.1 of up to CAD 25,000 for a false statement, increased to 50% of any tax payable for repeated gross negligence; (ii) failure-to-file penalties under section 280 plus interest under section 280.1; (iii) clawback of input tax credits and refunds, with compounded daily interest; (iv) criminal prosecution for tax evasion under section 327; and (v) application of the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) to recharacterize artificial export arrangements, together with a five-year extended limitation period for wilful evasion or fraud under section 298.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current-account and capital-account convertibility for the Canadian dollar. There is no general foreign-exchange control law, no central bank approval requirement for inward or outward capital movements, and no restriction on profit, dividend, or royalty remittance by a non-resident shareholder of an Ontario business corporation. The practical hurdles are anti-money-laundering (FINTRAC) reporting thresholds, OSFI prudential expectations for correspondent banking, OFAC and United Nations sanctions screening, and enhanced due diligence for payments involving high-risk or non-cooperative jurisdictions. Profit repatriation is subject to Canadian Part XIII withholding tax unless reduced by an applicable income tax treaty (e.g., Canada-US Treaty) and must be supported by tax residency certificates and a valid withholding tax remittance with Canada Revenue Agency (CRA).

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory requirement to surrender export proceeds to the Bank of Canada or any other government authority, and there is no mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts. Exporters may lawfully retain proceeds in foreign currency bank accounts held outside or inside Canada. The only compliance obligations affecting such proceeds are FINTRAC reporting for cross-border electronic funds transfers and physical currency movements of CAD 10,000 or more, Canadian income tax accrual rules for foreign exchange gains and losses, and Canadian bookkeeping/tax-reporting obligations for foreign-denominated receivables.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as the monetary authority and issuer of the Canadian dollar; OSFI as the prudential regulator of federally regulated financial institutions; FINTRAC as the financial intelligence unit administering the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Low-to-Medium as a primary treasury rail and Medium as an auxiliary payout rail. Offshore digital EMI accounts (non-Canadian) cannot serve as the regulated settlement account for an Ontario operating entity without raising FINTRAC money-services-business (MSB) registration, regulatory nexus, and bank de-risking concerns. A foreign EMI may be used for low-value disbursements only if the EMI is duly registered where required, holds a Canadian MSB registration when soliciting Canadian clients, and maintains clear audit trails to the Canadian bank account. Apple IAP and Paymentwall settlement should be channelled to the Canadian bank account rather than an offshore EMI to preserve clean audit trail and tax mapping.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined federal and Ontario general corporate income tax rate, comprised of the 15% federal Part I rate under Income Tax Act (Canada) subsection 123(1) and the 11.5% Ontario general corporate income tax rate under the Ontario Corporations Tax Act. The rate applies to taxable income before credits, loss carryovers, and available refundable tax offsets.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act. Any dividend, including deemed dividends, paid by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident individual or corporation is subject to 25% withholding tax unless reduced by an applicable income tax treaty. For a Canada-U.S. resident beneficial owner, the Canada-U.S. Tax Treaty generally reduces the rate to 15%, and to 5% where the beneficial owner is a corporation that owns 10% or more of the voting power of the payer.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 must be filed no later than six months after the end of the corporation's taxation year under subsection 150(1) of the Income Tax Act. For a taxation year ending December 31, the filing deadline is June 30 of the following calendar year. The balance of tax payable is generally due two months after the taxation year-end, or three months for eligible CCPCs.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose a bright-line monetary threshold for preparing a Local File or Master File. Instead, section 247 of the Income Tax Act requires every non-arm's-length cross-border transaction with a resident of a treaty country to be contemporaneously documented. In practice, CRA Information Circular 87-2R and the OECD transfer pricing guidelines require Local File and Master File level documentation where material related-party transactions exist. The transfer-pricing penalty under subsection 247(3) applies when the net transfer-pricing adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 and 10% of the taxpayer's gross revenue. Separately, an multinational enterprise group with consolidated group revenue of CAD 750,000,000 or more must file CRA Form RC5100, Country-by-Country Report, in Canada for the reporting fiscal year.

Economic substance scope: Canada has not enacted United Arab Emirates-style Economic Substance Regulations. However, the economic substance doctrine is operational through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) under section 245 of the Income Tax Act, the common law sham doctrine, and the transfer-pricing anti-avoidance rule in section 247. A video-matching social application company that books high-value IP royalty payments to a zero-tax foreign affiliate while the actual strategic, operational, and technical functions remain in Ontario will fail an economic substance review. The CRA will examine the people, premises, assets, contracts, and risks actually borne in Canada. Ontario also enforces business presence rules, and the absence of local substance can result in the CRA reallocating profits to the Ontario permanent establishment and imposing the transfer-pricing penalty.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Willful Failure to File

Statutory sanction: Under subsection 239(1) of the Income Tax Act, tax evasion on conviction is punishable by a fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, and imprisonment for up to five years on indictment or two years less a day on summary conviction. Subsection 239(2) imposes penalties for false statements or deceptive transactions, and section 238 creates a penalty for failing to file with fines and imprisonment. The CRA may also assess interest, gross-negligence penalties under subsection 163(2), and civil liability for unpaid tax.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who direct or knowingly participate in the concealment of income can be charged as parties to the offence under section 21 of the Criminal Code. Criminal restitution orders can attach to personal assets, and the corporate veil can be pierced when the corporation is a mere façade or an instrument of fraud. A director charged with serious tax evasion is also subject to bail conditions that may include travel bans and surrender of passports, followed by imprisonment upon conviction.
Shell Company and General Anti-Avoidance

Statutory sanction: The CRA may apply GAAR under section 245(2) to deny the tax benefit and reassess the taxpayer at the full 26.5% combined rate plus interest. The sham doctrine permits the CRA to recharacterize transactions as actually intended. Transfer pricing adjustments under subsection 247(2) may be applied as if the cross-border transaction had occurred at arm's length, and a gross negligence penalty under subsection 163(2) can be imposed. Ontario can also strike the corporation's extra-provincial or provincial registration for carrying on business without authority.

Director personal liability: Directors of a shell who execute false resolutions, sign incomplete or misleading T2 schedules, or personally facilitate the foreign IP migration can be held personally liable as makers of false statements under section 239. In the fraudulent conversion context, Ontario courts may pierce the corporate veil and order directors to repay diverted assets and profits. Serious cases under the Criminal Code, including fraud affecting public revenue, carry imprisonment and ancillary travel restrictions.
Transfer Pricing Documentation Failure and Non-Arm's-Length Pricing

Statutory sanction: Under subsection 247(3) of the Income Tax Act, the CRA can impose a transfer-pricing penalty equal to 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment where the adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 and 10% of the taxpayer's gross revenue for the year. The corporation loses the reasonable-efforts defense if contemporaneous records were not prepared or were materially inaccurate. The CRA may also adjust all related transactions and impose referral penalties, interest, and treaty under-withholding assessments.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly approve false intercompany invoices or instruct tax preparers to exclude transfers may be jointly liable with the corporate taxpayer for the unremitted Part XIII withholding and related director liability under subsection 227.1 of the Income Tax Act for withheld amounts, including payroll source deductions. Criminal exposure arises for aiding and abetting tax evasion if the director's conduct is fraudulent, and a court may issue personal restitution orders and restrict travel as part of bail conditions.
AML and Proceeds of Crime Compliance Failure

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC may issue administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1,000,000 per violation for corporations and significant lesser amounts for individuals. Criminal prosecution under section 462.31 of the Criminal Code for laundering proceeds of crime carries imprisonment up to 10 years. The PCMLTFA also creates separate offences for failing to report large cash transactions, failing to verify identity, and continuing business with a shell entity, with additional fines for every day the offence continues.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who authorize high-risk user funding, integrate third-party payment rails without AML controls, or permit the platform to be used for money laundering can be personally charged under the Criminal Code as principals or accessories after the fact. Convictions trigger mandatory criminal restitution, significant individual fines, and imprisonment. While on criminal process, directors are subject to travel bans and surrender of travel documents, eliminating executive mobility while charges are pending.
