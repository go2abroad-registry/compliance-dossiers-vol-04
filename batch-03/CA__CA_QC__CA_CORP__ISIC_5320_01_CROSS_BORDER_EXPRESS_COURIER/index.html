Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital; shares may be issued for such consideration as the board determines (QBCA s. 33). No escrow or deposits are required at formation. Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) for federal GST/HST and Part I corporate income tax (portal: CRA My Business Account). Revenu Québec for Quebec Sales Tax (QST), provincial corporate income tax, and payroll (portal: Clic Revenu). Registraire des entreprises du Québec is the single-point business registry accepting one-stop BN/GST/HST/QST registration.

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity carrying on business in Quebec must (1) obtain a Business Number (BN) through Revenu Québec's one-stop online portal (Clic Revenu) or CRA Form RC1, serving as the federal TIN; (2) complete immatriculation extra-provinciale with the Registraire des entreprises if the foreign corporation operates a branch in Quebec; (3) open a GST/HST account and QST account in the same application, required when taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 per annum or when the entity is deemed a large carrier; (4) supply foreign incorporation certificate, registered office address, director/UBO identification, and proof of permanent establishment or Canadian agent designation; (5) receive the 9-digit BN plus 2-letter program account (RT for GST/HST, RQ for QST), typically issued within 1-5 business days online or 4-8 weeks by mail.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation — A foreign entity carrying on business in Quebec must (1) obtain a Business Number (BN) through Revenu Québec's one-stop online portal (Clic Revenu) or CRA Form RC1, serving as the federal TIN; (2) complete immatriculation extra-provinciale with the Registraire des entreprises if the foreign corporation operates a branch in Quebec; (3) open a GST/HST account and QST account in the same application, required when taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 per annum or when the entity is deemed a large carrier; (4) supply foreign incorporation certificate, registered office address, director/UBO identification, and proof of permanent establishment or Canadian agent designation; (5) receive the 9-digit BN plus 2-letter program account (RT for GST/HST, RQ for QST), typically issued within 1-5 business days online or 4-8 weeks by mail.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box regime exists; a business corporation is mandatorily taxed under Income Tax Act Part I. Hybrid mismatch rules (ITA ss. 18.2/18.3, effective July 1, 2022) restrict deductions in cross-border hybrid arrangements and a publicly filed election to exclude double-dip interest is available. GST/HST and QST both allow an election among closely related corporations (ETA s. 156 and QST RLRQ c. I-3 equivalent) to deem intercompany supplies non-taxable; joint venture election (ETA s. 273) also available for courier co-ownership arrangements.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

0.14975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 worldwide annual taxable supplies for both GST/HST and QST small-supplier thresholds; courier services where goods are transported into or out of Canada count toward the threshold; registration is compulsory upon exceedance, but cross-border carriers typically register immediately for input tax credit recovery on Canadian fuel, warehousing, and labour costs.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

GST/HST zero-rating applies to (a) freight transportation services and courier services supplied from a place in Canada to a destination outside Canada (ETA Sch. VI, Pt. V, ss. 7 and 20) and (b) supplies made to non-resident exporters where the goods are exported as part of the courier shipment; QST zero-rates the identical supply chain under the Quebec Taxation Act (RLRQ c. I-3, sch. I, Pt. IV, s. 7 mirror). To claim 0%, the courier must be registered, the shipment must physically depart Canada within a reasonable period, the transport document must show a foreign consignee, and the service consideration must be charged to a Canadian exporter (or non-resident) with a valid BN for GST/QST purposes; low-value export shipments under CBSA's Courier Low Value Shipment Program may require no B13A, but the zero-rating claim must still be substantiated.

  • Customs Export Declaration (B13A or Automated Export Declaration via CBSA CAED/AES) plus CBSA eManifest confirmation (ACI) for the outbound movement
  • Carrier waybill or Master Air Waybill (MAWB) / Bill of Lading issued by the express courier indicating place of origin in Canada and foreign destination
  • Commercial invoice with exporter, foreign consignee, HS classification, and declared value of the exported goods; Inward Remittance Certificate / SWIFT or bank credit advice evidencing foreign-currency settlement of the courier fee

No statutory real-time fiscal clearance, no digital stamping, and no ERP/API interface mandate. The Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Framework uses the PEPPOL BIS 3.0 standard on a voluntary basis; CRA requires electronic records to be complete, legible, and accessible in Canada for audit. Couriers clearing goods at the border must, however, file an eManifest through CBSA's ACI/eManifest portal (API/EDEX gateway) — this is a customs pre-arrival clearance mandate, not a VAT e-invoicing mandate. Revenu Québec accepts recognized standards (e.g., EDI, FACTUR-X) for QST invoices; analogue paper invoices are equally valid if GST/QST registration numbers appear.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current account and capital account convertibility under the IMF Article VIII status. There is no foreign exchange control law, no central bank permission requirement, and no 'real demand' test for profit remittance. The Canadian dollar is fully convertible; non-resident corporations may freely inject, hold, convert, and repatriate capital and dividends through authorized banks. The only substantive cross-border hurdles are fiscal: the federal Part XIII withholding tax on dividends, interest, rents and royalties paid to non-residents (statutory 25%, reduced under applicable tax treaties to levels commonly between 5% and 15% for corporate shareholders), and the provincial Quebec withholding rules which are collected in coordination with the Canada Revenue Agency. No economic benefit review, exchange allocation, or convertibility licensing requirement applies.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender, repatriation timeline, or mandatory conversion ratio for export proceeds. A Quebec-based cross-border express courier operator is free to hold export receivables in Canadian dollars, US dollars, or any freely traded foreign currency in a Canadian or offshore bank account. The only relevant mandatory obligations are financial reporting thresholds: physical currency or monetary instruments equal to or exceeding C$10,000 imported or exported must be declared to CBSA; electronic transfers equal to or exceeding C$10,000 are subject to FINTRAC reporting by the financial institution; and the corporation must include realized foreign exchange gains or losses in its Canadian income tax computations under the Income Tax Act.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); banking regulation is administered by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); anti-money-laundering and cross-border electronic funds transfer oversight is conducted by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC); physical cross-border currency movements are declared to the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA). Canada does not operate a separate foreign exchange control administration.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

PayPal Fit: High

Medium viability with strict legal limits. A foreign-owned digital electronic money institution cannot be chartered as a bank under the Bank Act and cannot accept Canadian deposits as a bank, but it may operate in Canada as a money services business (MSB) registered with FINTRAC. Payment-focused platforms such as Wise Canada, PayPal Canada, Payoneer, Airwallex, and Mural are viable for low-value courier receivables, merchant card acquiring, ACH settlement, and cross-border employee or partner disbursements; however, they cannot lawfully provide a full corporate bank account, cheque writing, bank credit, or a cash-backed capital escrow facility under Quebec corporate law. A Schedule I Canadian bank account remains mandatory as the liquidity anchor; any foreign EMI must be integrated as an auxiliary payment rail only.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inward equity capital must be wired from the non-resident shareholder using SWIFT MT103, with the message clearly annotated in Field 70 as 'INJECTION-EQUITY-CA-CORP' or an equivalent narrative purpose code. Canada does not prescribe statutory purpose codes for inward remittances; however, the receiving Canadian bank will require underlying documentation comprising the board resolution approving the share subscription, the capital contribution agreement, the Quebec Registraire des entreprises registration, and a source-of-funds certificate from the remitting shareholder. Funds may arrive in CAD or USD; if USD, the bank will convert or credit a USD clearing account through Payments Canada or correspondent Nostro rails. For any single electronic transfer equal to or above C$10,000, the receiving bank must file a Large Virtual Currency Transaction / Large Cash Transaction Report to FINTRAC; the corporation should be ready to supply the business rationale and the capital call schedule. No authorization from Bank of Canada or CBSA is needed.Outbound settlement to overseas agent couriers, customs brokers, air carriers, and partner logistics companies should be executed using Bank of Canada dollar clearing via Payments Canada Lynx for domestic CAD obligations, and via SWIFT MT103 or MT202 for cross-border payments. For USD settlement to US-based counterparties, the preferred rail is Fedwire or CHIPS through a Canadian bank's US correspondent. For documentary trade with international airlines and customs customs-bond partners, commercial letters of credit should be issued as SWIFT MT700 from a Montreal commercial banking desk. Each outbound transfer must contain a UETR and compliant Field 70 narrative such as 'SETT-STAGE4-COURIER-INV', and supporting invoices should be retained for six years to satisfy FINTRAC record-retention and Revenu Québec audit requirements. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Combined general corporate income tax rate: 26.5% in Quebec, comprising 15% federal tax and 11.5% Quebec tax. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access a combined rate of approximately 12.2% on eligible active business income within the applicable business limit, comprising 9% federal tax and 3.2% Quebec tax.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% on taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident, subject to treaty reduction. Common treaty outcomes include 5% where the beneficial owner is a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting the minimum ownership threshold and 15% in other qualifying cases. The Canada-United States treaty commonly provides 5% for qualifying corporate ownership and 15% otherwise.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate value of non-arm's-length cross-border transactions with a particular non-resident or relevant transaction category is the principal threshold for Form T106 reporting. Canada does not generally impose an OECD-style mandatory Local File and Master File filing requirement for every taxpayer. Contemporaneous documentation is required under the Income Tax Act where non-arm's-length cross-border transactions occur, regardless of whether a CAD 1,000,000 T106 threshold is exceeded. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to an in-scope multinational group with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million, subject to Canadian rules and filing conditions.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone general Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to some offshore jurisdictions. Economic substance is evaluated through the arm's-length principle, the general anti-avoidance rule, specific anti-avoidance provisions, beneficial-ownership principles, debt-versus-equity analysis, thin-capitalization rules, principal-purpose or treaty-abuse rules where applicable, and common-law sham and agency doctrines. The corporation must maintain real decision-making, personnel, assets, risk control, contractual capacity, and commercial rationale in Canada consistent with the income reported.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Corporate Income Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Conviction under the Income Tax Act may result in a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment for up to 2 years, assessment of the tax, interest, civil penalties, and prosecution costs.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face criminal prosecution where they participated in, authorized, acquiesced in, or directed the conduct. The corporation remains liable for tax, interest, and civil penalties. Personal exposure can include restitution, seizure, and asset-preservation orders; travel restrictions may arise from judicial orders or criminal process, but are not automatic.
False Statements, Gross Negligence, and Fraudulent Tax Reporting

Statutory sanction: Federal penalties may include 10% of an understated amount or overstated credit, a minimum fixed penalty in applicable cases, and a gross-negligence penalty generally equal to 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit. Criminal fraud or tax-evasion charges may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors may be prosecuted where personally involved and may be liable for statutory remittances. The corporate veil is not automatically pierced for ordinary tax debt, but fraud, oppression, sham arrangements, or deliberate statutory breaches may support personal remedies.
Failure to Remit Payroll, GST/HST, or QST Amounts

Statutory sanction: Assessment of the unremitted amount, prescribed interest, civil penalties, director-liability assessments, collection enforcement, and potential criminal prosecution for intentional conduct.

Director personal liability: Directors can be jointly and severally liable for specified unremitted source deductions and certain indirect taxes if statutory due-diligence defenses are unavailable. Personal collection actions may include garnishment, liens, seizure, and asset recovery.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess under section 247, deny or adjust deductions, impute income, impose a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment in qualifying circumstances, charge interest, and apply withholding-tax consequences.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal consequences for knowing participation in false reporting or tax evasion. Ordinary transfer-pricing reassessment generally remains a corporate liability, but fraudulent or sham arrangements may support personal prosecution and recovery.
Treaty Abuse and Beneficial-Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty relief, withholding at 25%, arrears interest, penalties, reassessment under treaty-anti-abuse principles or the general anti-avoidance rule, and possible denial of deductions.

Director personal liability: Directors may be exposed where they knowingly certified false treaty documentation or directed abusive arrangements. Personal criminal exposure requires proof of the relevant statutory offence or participation.
Shell Company, Sham, or Artificial Permanent-Establishment Structure

Statutory sanction: Corporate residence or permanent-establishment reassessment, Canadian income taxation, branch tax, withholding, penalties, interest, GAAR application, and possible criminal charges for fraud or evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face prosecution, asset seizure, restitution, and disqualification consequences where they participated in the scheme. Travel bans or imprisonment require applicable judicial or criminal orders and are not automatic civil consequences.
Customs and Import-Export Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Assessment of duties and taxes, administrative monetary penalties, seizure or forfeiture of goods, customs enforcement action, license or program suspension, and criminal prosecution for intentional false declarations.

Director personal liability: Directors and responsible officers may be prosecuted for knowing participation. Personal civil liability depends on the statutory basis and conduct; corporate liability does not automatically become director liability.
AML, Sanctions, and Proceeds-of-Crime Risk

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, publication of violations, criminal prosecution for money laundering or sanctions offences, significant fines, and imprisonment under applicable federal legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and compliance personnel may be personally prosecuted for knowing participation, consent, or wilful blindness. Criminal penalties can include fines, imprisonment, forfeiture, and court-imposed travel restrictions.
Unlicensed or Non-Compliant Courier Operations

Statutory sanction: Administrative penalties, permit suspension or revocation, seizure, prohibition orders, contractual disqualification, and prosecution under the applicable federal or Quebec statute.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable where legislation imposes officer liability or where they authorized the offence. Ordinary licensing breaches do not automatically pierce the corporate veil.
Record Retention and Data Integrity Failure

Statutory sanction: Failure-to-produce penalties, adverse audit inferences, denied deductions or credits, extended reassessment periods, obstruction penalties, and prosecution where destruction was deliberate.

Director personal liability: Responsible officers may face personal prosecution for obstruction or destruction of records. Directors should implement documented retention, access-control, backup, and legal-hold procedures.
Insolvency, Improper Distributions, and Creditor Evasion

Statutory sanction: Transaction reversal, creditor claims, tax collection proceedings, director-liability claims, oppression remedies, bankruptcy recovery, and potential fraud or preference offences.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil restitution, contribution claims, disqualification, asset recovery, and criminal prosecution where fraudulent intent is established. Imprisonment and travel restrictions require applicable criminal proceedings or court orders.
