Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Swiss Federal Tax Administration (FTA; Eidgenössische Steuerverwaltung ESTV / Administration fédérale des contributions AFC), Brückfeldstrasse 230, 3008 Bern, Switzerland; e-services portal: https://www.estv.admin.ch

E-government filing portal

Foreign entities must apply to the FTA for a UID (Unternehmens-Identifikationsnummer) and a VAT number (MWST-Nummer). Registration is effected in writing or online using Form 'Anmeldung zur Mehrwertsteuerpflicht' per Art. 66 MWSTV; the foreign entity must provide its foreign registration number, the Swiss representative or place of summary registration, estimated turnover, and expected supply categories. Upon successful registration, the FTA issues a UID with MWST indicator, which becomes the statutory tax identifier for all Swiss tax filings, invoices, and customs transactions.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation (resident Aktiengesellschaft is an independent legal person subject to unlimited Swiss corporate income tax on worldwide income; for VAT, territorial supply rules in Switzerland apply independently of corporate tax residence) — Foreign entities must apply to the FTA for a UID (Unternehmens-Identifikationsnummer) and a VAT number (MWST-Nummer). Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation (resident Aktiengesellschaft is an independent legal person subject to unlimited Swiss corporate income tax on worldwide income; for VAT, territorial supply rules in Switzerland apply independently of corporate tax residence)

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box regime exists in Switzerland. An AG may elect VAT grouping under Art. 21 MWSTG to combine domestic taxable persons into one taxable entity. Hybrid mismatch elections are not available; Swiss rules implement BEPS Action 2 with mandatory recharacterisation and denial of deductions in cross-border hybrid mismatches.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

8.1%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CHF 100,000 annual worldwide turnover from taxable supplies (Art. 10 para 2 lit a MWSTG); non-resident suppliers making taxable supplies in Switzerland are generally required to register without turnover threshold (Art. 10 para 2 lit b MWSTG)

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods qualify for zero-rated VAT if they are physically exported from Switzerland to a foreign destination and the exporter holds documented proof of export under Art. 23 MWSTG; the supplier must evidence that the goods left Swiss customs territory and that the recipient is located abroad. Export-related and offshore services may be zero-rated when the recipient is domiciled abroad and the benefit is realised outside Switzerland, consistent with Art. 23 and Art. 24 MWSTG.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Ausfuhrzollanmeldung) including export reference number and customs confirmation of physical exit
  • Commercial invoice and transport documents — Bill of Lading, CMR, or Air Waybill evidencing dispatch to destination country
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / bank transfer confirmation evidencing receipt of foreign currency export proceeds (supporting evidence for zero-rating and customs valuation)
  • Customs proof of exit (Ausfuhrbescheinigung) or equivalent electronic export notification where applicable

Switzerland does not currently impose mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, API tax clearance, or digital stamping. Electronic invoicing is permissible, including QR-bill format under SIX Swiss Payment Standards and cross-border formats such as PEPPOL; all invoices must satisfy content requirements under Art. 26 MWSTG, including supplier identification, UID, date, type of supply, consideration, and applicable VAT rate.

Fraudulent or unsupported export zero-rating is treated as Swiss VAT tax evasion under Art. 96 MWSTG, exposingthe taxpayer to fines of up to three times the evaded VAT, default interest, and mandatory restitution of wrongfully claimed input tax credits; the FTA may retrospectively adjust assessments and claw back credits for up to 10 years in cases of intentional concealment (Art. 42 VStrR), and cross-border customs fraud may additionally activate customs and Swiss Criminal Code penalties.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Swiss Franc (CHF) is fully convertible on both current and capital accounts. Switzerland operates no general exchange-control legislation, no convertibility restrictions, and no withholding or blocking of cross-border transfers. Capital account transactions (equity injections, shareholder loans, M&A payments) and current account transactions (trade payments, license fees, dividends) are freely permitted. The only frictional element is the anticipatory withholding tax of 35% on dividends and similar distributions, which is a tax event, not a convertibility restriction, and may be reclaimed under a double-tax treaty or fully avoided through a Swiss qualifying participation holding structure. Profit remittance is surgically unimpeded; banks merely record the IMF/BPM6 balance-of-payments statistical codes for SNB reporting purposes. The SNB exercises monetary policy through target ranges, yet does not impose balancing, licensing, or conversion quotas on corporate treasury operations.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Switzerland imposes no statutory timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no surrender requirement for export proceeds. Export receivables in foreign currency may be retained offshore, converted at free-market rates, or used directly to settle foreign-currency supplier invoices without SNB notification. Corporate borrowers are subject only to SNB statistical reporting obligations (monthly balance-of-payments survey) when aggregate foreign assets or liabilities exceed CHF 10 million, and to FINMA anti-money-laundering documentation rules. The CHF is a fully deliverable currency, so the absence of surrender rules aligns with the absence of any administered exchange-rate band.

Central bank supervision: Swiss National Bank (SNB), the independent central bank established under the Swiss Federal Constitution (Art. 99) and governed by the National Bank Act of 2003.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Swiss transfer pricing documentation obligations are triggered when the multinational group's consolidated turnover (including the Swiss entity) exceeds CHF 50 million. The mandatory Local File must be maintained when annual related-party transactions exceed CHF 100,000 for a single category of goods, services, royalties or financial transactions. A Master File is required for groups with consolidated revenue above CHF 50 million. Even below these thresholds, the Swiss tax administration requires contemporaneous TP documentation when the taxpayer's transactions involve so-called 'safe heavens' or contractual risk allocation.

Economic substance scope: Switzerland does not impose a formal Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) code akin to UAE/offshore regimes, but the substance over form doctrine applies rigorously in tax treaty practice and Swiss anti-abuse case law. A Swiss AG is considered to lack economic substance if it has no local management decisions, no physical offices, no employees, or no substantive integration into the value chain. Such a company can be challenged as a letter-box entity, denied DTA benefits, exposed to retrospective taxation, and potentially subjected to forced liquidation under Swiss law.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Certification

Statutory sanction: Under Article 186 of the Swiss Federal Act on Direct Taxes (DBG), a tax evasion fine of up to 250% of the tax evaded, in addition to tax arrears and interest, and a criminal fine of up to CHF 250,000 in serious cases. In egregious fraud involving foreign tax administration, imprisonment of up to 5 years under the Swiss Criminal Code (Art. 146) can be imposed.

Director personal liability: Directors who approve or sign fraudulent returns are jointly and severally liable for the resulting tax deficiency and fines. Swiss courts may order veil-piercing against the director's personal assets, and foreign criminal investigations may lead to travel bans, Interpol red notices and extradition requests.
Shell Company / Artificial Arrangement

Statutory sanction: The tax authority disregards the corporate form, assesses taxation on the economic owner or effective management, denies treaty benefits, and may retroactively re-open several years. Additionally, a shell company can be struck off the commercial register and liquidated.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil is pierced; directors may be personally liable to pay the Swiss corporate tax, plus interest and procedural penalties. Directors may be banned from serving on Swiss boards for up to two years.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing / Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: The Swiss tax authority substitutes an arm's length profit, levying additional tax and late-payment interest at 4.5% annually, and imposes a procedural fine of up to 30% of the adjusted deficiency. If the mispricing was intentional, it escalates to tax evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors of the Swiss entity may be held personally liable for the tax differential and penalties when they knowingly executed the mispricing or directed the financial record falsification. Travel restrictions and seizure of assets for amounts exceeding CHF 1 million are possible.
Withholding Tax Evasion / Constructive Dividend

Statutory sanction: The 35% withholding tax remains due from the distributing company, together with default interest at 5% per annum and penalties. In confirmed cases, the company can be criminally sanctioned and the foreign recipient is treated as subject to Swiss tax law for the disguised amount.

Director personal liability: Directors who are aware of an unlawful distribution are personally liable to the State for the unpaid withholding tax. Swiss criminal courts may impose custodial sentences up to 3 years, as well as confiscation of director's salary or assets used to commit the offence.
AML / Money Laundering through Corporate Conduit

Statutory sanction: Violations of the Swiss Anti-Money Laundering Act (AMLA) attract criminal prosecution for money laundering, with imprisonment up to 5 years and fines up to CHF 1 million. Unlicensed financial activity can lead to forfeiture of the account and withdrawal of banking privileges.

Director personal liability: Directors who act as resource persons for money laundering are personally sued by the financial regulators; Swiss authorities can issue ten-year entry bans, seize personal bank accounts in Switzerland, and enforce criminal fines on director's private assets.
