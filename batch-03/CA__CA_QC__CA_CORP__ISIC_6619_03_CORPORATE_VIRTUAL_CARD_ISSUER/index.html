Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) for federal GST/HST and income tax, with the 'My Business Account' portal at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html; Revenu Québec for the Quebec Sales Tax (QST) and provincial income tax, with the 'Cliquez Affaires' portal at https://www.revenuquebec.ca/en/businesses/

E-government filing portal

Foreign non-resident corporations must first obtain a Business Number (BN) from the CRA by filing Form RC1, 'Request for a Business Number and Certain Accounts', or by using Business Registration Online. After obtaining the BN, the corporation must add a GST/HST program account (RT) for the supply of taxable goods or services in Canada; if the corporation has no permanent establishment and all material decisions are made abroad, a non-resident agent may be required for service of documents. Separate registration is then required with Revenu Québec to obtain a QST identification number by filing the Quebec registration form through the Cliquez Affaires portal, providing the BN, legal name, address, business structure, and director or officer information. The same BN can be used for income tax, payroll, and GST/HST accounts, but the QST number remains a distinct Revenu Québec identifier. No minimum capital is required for the Business Corporation, and non-resident ownership is permitted subject to filing disclosure requirements for foreign ownership and any applicable sectoral licences.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a US-style 'check-the-box' election; entity classification is determined under the Income Tax Act and established Canadian common-law principles. A Canadian Business Corporation is automatically treated as an entity-level taxpayer subject to income tax on its worldwide revenue. Available elections relevant to cross-border fintech operations include the section 85(1) rollover election, the section 216 non-resident net-income election, the section 249.1 election to use a non-calendar fiscal period for tax purposes, and, in the context of hybrid mismatch rules, sections 18.2 to 18.4 apply automatically without any elective safe harbour. For GST/QST purposes, a registrant may elect to use the Quick Method or annual filing where legislation provides, though financial institutions do not qualify; no election changes the fundamental entity-level taxation of the corporation.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies (exclusive of exempt financial services and supplies made through a permanent establishment) in the current calendar quarter together with the preceding three consecutive calendar quarters triggers mandatory GST/HST registration. For QST purposes, Revenu Québec applies an analogous CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold, calculated on the supplier's worldwide taxable supplies, at which mandatory QST registration becomes effective in Quebec.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available under the Excise Tax Act for a supply of financial services made to a non-resident person who is not registered under the GST/HST and does not have a significant presence or carrying-on-business nexus in Canada. A corporate virtual-card issuer must demonstrate that the card issuing, spend-management, and processing services are acquired by the non-resident client for use predominantly outside Canada, and that the recipient is the true legal counter-party. For tangible goods exported from Canada, zero-rating applies if the goods are delivered by the supplier to a common carrier or by another qualifying export mechanism via the Canada Border Services Agency export declaration and the supplier has timely evidence of export. Quebec QST zero-rating generally mirrors the federal GST financial-services export rules for supplies made to non-residents and supplies shipped or delivered outside Canada.

  • Completed Canada Customs Export Declaration (B13A or the electronic CAED version) filed with the Canada Border Services Agency
  • Transport document evidencing physical export, such as a Bill of Lading, Airway Bill, road manifest, or bare-carrier certificate
  • Commercial invoice and supply contract identifying the non-resident buyer, full goods/services description, and contract value
  • Bank outward/export payment evidence, including an Inward Remittance Certificate, SWIFT/telegraphic transfer confirmation, or a Canadian bank wire credit advice showing receipt of payment from the non-resident counter-party
  • Carrier or freight-forwarder certificate of export, where applicable, to supplement the customs declaration and transport documents

No national or Quebec real-time fiscal clearance, government API stamping, or continuous transaction control mandate applies as of the current filing year. Electronic invoicing is accepted and encouraged by the CRA and Revenu Québec, but there is no statutory requirement for preclearance or digital stamping of B2B invoices. All registered corporate-card issuers must retain complete invoice images, transaction-level interchange and settlement records, bank confirmation files, and underlying contract documentation to substantiate GST/QST treatment upon review. A transition toward mandatory e-invoicing is under active policy discussion in Quebec, but no mandatory clearance mandate is currently in force.

CRA and Revenu Québec may reassess zero-rated supplies within four years of the original return, and an unlimited review period applies where misrepresentation, neglect, or fraud is established. If export documentation is missing or inadequate, the entire zero-rating claim is clawed back, converting the supplies to taxable at the combined 14.975% rate plus interest. Negligent or deliberate overstating of zero-rated revenue triggers the Excise Tax Act gross-negligence penalty of 25% of the understated net tax, further section 285 penalties for false statements, potential criminal prosecution under section 327, and equivalent QST penalties under the QST Act. The taxpayer bears the burden of proving physical export and correct recipient status; internal card operations, interchange records, settlement audit trails, and FX repatriation evidence are therefore critical to preserve.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full current-account and capital-account convertibility of the Canadian dollar (CAD). No exchange controls are in force; the Currency Act and the Bank of Canada Act authorize a free-floating exchange rate. Profit remittances, dividends, interest, and liquidation proceeds may be transferred cross-border without ministerial authorization or conversion mandate. Statutory frictions are limited to (i) Part XIII non-resident withholding tax under the Income Tax Act (default 25% on dividends, reduced by treaty), (ii) FINTRAC and Customs Act reporting of currency or monetary instruments equal to or exceeding CAD 10,000 crossing the border, (iii) FATCA/CRS self-certification, and (iv) Investment Canada Act notification/review if the inbound capital injection constitutes a reviewable non-Canadian acquisition of a Quebec business.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory export-proceeds surrender rules exist in Canada. The former Exchange Control Act was repealed in 1951; there is no mandated timeline, minimum conversion ratio, or central bank deposit requirement for foreign currency receipts. Export settlements may be held in any convertible currency, subject only to standard accounting (IFRS), tax reporting, and OFAC/United Nations/Canadian sanctions screening obligations.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BoC) as monetary authority; Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) as AML/CFT regulator; Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for federally regulated bank counterparties; Autorité des marchés financiers (AMF) of Quebec as provincial securities and money-services-business regulator; Global Affairs Canada for sanctions administration under the Special Economic Measures Act (SEMA) and the United Nations Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low direct equivalence. Canada does not recognize a European Economic Area EMI passport. A non-resident fintech/EMI must establish a Canadian presence: (i) register as a Money Services Business (MSB) with FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA); (ii) register as a money-services business with the AMF under Quebec's Act respecting money-services businesses (CQLR c S-41.1) when operating in Quebec; (iii) register as a payment service provider with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) where RPAA thresholds are met; and (iv) secure a principal-member sponsorship from an OSFI-regulated Canadian bank for Visa/Mastercard program access, as direct network membership is not available to non-deposit-taking fintechs.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal general corporate income tax rate of 15% plus Quebec general corporate income tax rate of 11.5%, producing a combined statutory headline rate of 26.5%; reduced rates apply to the first CAD 500,000 of qualifying active business income for a Canadian-Controlled Private Corporation (CCPC).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% federal Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder; Quebec imposes no additional provincial dividend withholding tax.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

T2 return must be filed within 6 months after fiscal year-end; balance-due tax is payable within 2 months after fiscal year-end, or 3 months for a CCPC eligible for the Small Business Deduction in the tax year.

Deadline: · Frequency:

CO-17 must be filed within 6 months of fiscal year-end; final Quebec tax payment is due within 2 months after fiscal year-end, or 3 months for a CCPC entitled to the reduced Quebec small-business rate.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate non-resident non-arm's-length transaction value triggers T106 filing and mandatory contemporaneous Local File/TP documentation; CAD 750,000,000 consolidated MNE group revenue triggers Country-by-Country (CbC) report and corresponding Master File support.

Economic substance scope: No standalone Economic Substance Regulations exist in Canada; the doctrine is effectuated through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA section 245), Quebec Taxation Act section 1078.2, and the 'economic substance' jurisprudence requiring a genuine commercial purpose. A virtual-card issuer or holding company lacking real operations, employees, or substantive business purpose is a high-priority audit target.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Wilful Failure to File

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act section 239: fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment up to 5 years, or both; plus civil interest and gross-negligence penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly authorised evasion may be personally assessed for unremitted source deductions under section 227.1; the corporate veil is pierced for fraud; travel restrictions and passport surrender may be imposed pending criminal prosecution.
Gross Negligence / False Statements

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act section 163(2): 50% gross-negligence penalty on the understated tax; Quebec Taxation Act imposes an analogous 50% penalty, plus interest and attributable costs.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for corporate GST/QST and payroll remittances if they knowingly participated in failure to remit; civil restitution may extend to monies diverted through shell companies.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing / Inadequate Documentation

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act section 247(2) permits CRA to recharacterise and adjust cross-border transactions to the arm's-length price; section 247(3) imposes a penalty equal to 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment where documentation is deficient; no cap applies.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for TP adjustments but become personally liable for associated withheld amounts if they knowingly authorised the mispricing or hid documentation from CRA.
Unlicensed Money-Services Business / AML Violation

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1,000,000 per violation; criminal conviction may lead to fines up to CAD 2,000,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years; Quebec Autorité des marchés financiers may impose additional sanctions.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers can be personally named in FINTRAC AMPs and criminal proceedings; travel bans, asset freezes, and imprisonment apply upon indictment; corporate veil protection is unavailable where the corporation was a deliberate sham.
Shell Company / No Economic Substance / Abusive Avoidance

Statutory sanction: CRA may apply GAAR to deny the tax benefit and recharacterise the conduit arrangement; false or sham documentation triggers section 239 evasion penalties and gross-negligence penalties under section 163; interest compounds from the original filing date.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally assessed for the corporation's unremitted withholdings and GST/QST; fraudulent conveyance doctrine permits piercing the veil; criminal conviction may impose imprisonment and travel bans.
