Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

Permitted with caveats. Quebec law (Act respecting the legal publicity of enterprises, arts. 20 and 23) requires the head office (siège social) of a business corporation to be a genuine address in Quebec where documents may be served and the corporate register may be consulted. Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) - federal GST/HST registration, Business Number (BN), and federal corporate income tax administration (portal: CRA My Business Account at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services.html); Revenu Québec - QST registration, Quebec corporate income tax and provincial enterprise registration interfaces (portal: Revenu Québec Clic ReQ at https://www.revenuquebec.ca/en/online-services/).

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity obtains its federal Business Number by filing Form RC1A (Request for a Business Number from a Non-Resident) with CRA, either through online Business Registration Online or paper submission, and simultaneously registers for GST/HST. For Quebec, the entity must file Form LM-1-V with Revenu Québec or use the Clic ReQ online registration service to obtain a QST registration number and the Quebec Enterprise Number (NEQ). The entity must also designate a Quebec address for service and, if incorporated outside Quebec, complete extra-provincial registration with the Registraire des entreprises. No minimum capital is required and non-resident ownership is allowed.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation — A foreign entity obtains its federal Business Number by filing Form RC1A (Request for a Business Number from a Non-Resident) with CRA, either through online Business Registration Online or paper submission, and simultaneously registers for GST/HST. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box election exists; a business corporation is statutorily taxed as a separate person. Elective mechanisms that affect tax identity or character include the Income Tax Act s. 216(1) election for non-residents to file net rental income on Canadian real property (including data centre space leases) instead of gross Part XIII withholding; s. 85 / 85.1 rollover elections for incorporation or reorganization; the GST/HST s. 156 joint election between closely related corporations; and various Quebec QST elections concerning deemed supplies. Canada's hybrid mismatch rules (ITA ss. 18.2/18.3) apply automatically without any elective reclassification mechanism.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 - mandatory GST/HST and QST registration is required for a foreign supplier once the total consideration for taxable (non-exempt) supplies made in Canada/Quebec exceeds CAD 30,000 in the preceding four calendar quarters; the small-supplier threshold is not available once this amount is exceeded. Because data centre rack-space leasing is an ongoing taxable supply of real property situated in Quebec, registration will generally be required at commencement or as soon as the threshold is met.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply is zero-rated under Schedule IX Part V of the Excise Tax Act and corresponding QST rules if the recipient is a non-resident not registered under GST/HST/QST and the service is not in respect of real property situated in Canada, not in respect of tangible personal property situated in Canada, and not supplied to a recipient carrying on business in Canada. Data centre rack-space leasing is generally a supply of a right to use real property situated in Quebec and therefore remains taxable at 14.975% even for non-resident customers. Bare-metal server hosting may qualify for zero-rating only if contractually structured as a data-processing service not in respect of Canadian tangible or real property; technical legal analysis is required. Any zero-rated claim must be supported by contemporaneous evidence of the non-resident status and non-registration of the recipient.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CAED electronic filing or AES/ACI export reporting) for any tangible goods exported with the service
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical export from Canada (if applicable)
  • Inward remittance certificate, bank SWIFT or ACH record showing payment from the non-resident recipient
  • Executed service agreement containing place-of-supply and non-resident representations
  • Dated invoices with the zero-rating statement and GST/HST/QST registration numbers

No real-time fiscal clearance, API validation, mandatory e-invoicing approval, or digital stamping regime applies under Canadian federal or Quebec provincial law. Electronic invoices (EDI, XML, PDF) are accepted for GST/HST and QST purposes; the invoice must contain the supplier's BN and QST registration number, the recipient's name, the date, a description and quantity, the tax rate and tax amount, and applicable disclosure phrases. Records must be retained for six years and made available to CRA and Revenu Québec on request.

CRA and Revenu Québec may reassess zero-rating claims within the ordinary limitation period (generally three years, extended to four years for certain non-residents or misrepresentation) and retrospectively deny the 0% rate, thereby assessing uncollected GST/QST, interest, and penalties. Penalties can reach 25% of the tax amount under Excise Tax Act s. 280 and equivalent QST penalty provisions; fraudulent or grossly negligent claims expose the taxpayer to penalties up to 50% of any refund or rebate and to criminal prosecution for tax evasion. Although Canadian law imposes no statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline, CRA treats remittance within 180 days as strong corroborative evidence of an export transaction; longer payment terms require documented commercial justification.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full de jure and de facto convertibility on both capital and current account transactions. There are no exchange-control statutes, no repatriation restrictions, and no central-bank approval requirement for inbound equity, intercompany debt, or profit remittances. Dividend and royalty outflows are freely permitted, subject only to Part XIII non-resident withholding tax under the Income Tax Act (statutory 25%, typically reduced to 5–15% under applicable income tax treaties) and to standard FINTRAC, FATCA, and CRS reporting. The Canadian dollar floats freely; no conversion mandate is imposed on residents.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender rule and no mandatory conversion timeline for export receipts. Proceeds from colocation and hosting services may be retained in any foreign currency without restriction. The only compliance obligations are FINTRAC large-transaction reporting for cross-border currency or EFT transfers of CAD 10,000 or more, CBSA/Statistics Canada reporting for goods, and StatCan surveys for service exports. No surrender ratio or conversion deadline exists.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); prudential regulation by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); AML/ATF supervision by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Low–Medium. Offshore digital EMIs (e.g., Revolut Business, Wise Platform, Airwallex) cannot lawfully accept deposits in Canada without Bank Act authorization or a foreign-bank branch licence. They may operate only as money services businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC, and Quebec activities may further require an AMF registration or agent status. Recommended role: supplementary disbursement corridor and low-cost FX conversion for operating payments; not a substitute for a Schedule I bank operating account, escrow account, or LC facility.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined (Federal 15% general rate + Quebec 11.5% general rate); CCPC small-business deduction reduces federal rate to 9% and Quebec rate via provincial small-business deduction on the first C$500,000 of active business income.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident beneficial owner (Part XIII of the Income Tax Act, section 212); Quebec imposes no separate dividend withholding tax. Treaty reduction to 5% typically applies when the beneficial owner is a resident of a treaty country and owns at least 10% of the voting shares of the Canadian payer.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 in aggregate non-arm's-length cross-border transactions with any one non-resident in a fiscal year (triggers mandatory Form T106 filing and the obligation to produce contemporaneous Local File/Master File documentation within 3 months of a CRA request); Country-by-Country Report filings are mandatory where consolidated group revenue equals or exceeds C$750,000,000.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not impose UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations (ESR). Substance is instead enforced indirectly through: (i) the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA section 245, amended 2024 to include an explicit 25% penalty on tax benefits and an expanded avoidance-transaction test); (ii) the central-management-and-control residence test under Canadian common law and the deemed-residence rules in section 250; (iii) permanent establishment attribution under section 253 and OECD Article 7; and (iv) Quebec corporate law requiring a registered office in Quebec and a minimum of 25% resident-Canadian directors for CBCA/Quebec Business Corporations Act corporations. A Quebec data-centre colocation corporation must keep board meetings, employees, operational decision-making, and the physical servers, racks, UPS, and cooling infrastructure under Canadian control; a 'letterbox' conduit with no real substance will be disregarded for treaty and transfer-pricing purposes.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Willful Misstatement

Statutory sanction: ITA section 239(1): on summary conviction, a fine of 50%-100% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 2 years; on indictment, a fine of 100%-200% and imprisonment up to 5 years. Civil gross-negligence penalty under section 163(2): 50% of the understated tax, plus daily compound interest; Quebec equivalent penalty under the Tax Administration Act.

Director personal liability: Directors who participate in or authorize the misstatement are jointly indictable; CRA may issue a directors' liability assessment for unremitted withholdings under section 227.1, and in evasion prosecutions courts may impose travel bans, passport revocation, and restitution orders as bail conditions.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: ITA section 247(3) penalty of 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment where the adjustment exceeds the lesser of C$5,000,000 and 10% of gross revenue and no reasonable effort to apply the arm's-length principle is documented; recharacterization of disguised fees as Part XIII royalties subject to 25% withholding; interest accrues from the original due date.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false T106 declarations can be assessed for unremitted Part XIII tax on recharacterized management fees; the 'reasonable effort' due-diligence defense is unavailable where no TP policy, benchmarking, or contemporaneous records exist.
Shell Company / Treaty Shopping and GAAR

Statutory sanction: GAAR (section 245) denies the treaty benefit and re-characterizes the arrangement; the dividend becomes subject to the full 25% Part XIII withholding, plus interest and a 25% GAAR penalty (applicable to avoidance transactions entered into after the 2024 GAAR amendments); CRA may also reassess the corporation for a permanent establishment in Canada.

Director personal liability: Directors of the conduit may be personally assessed for unremitted withholding taxes if the corporation is insolvent; courts can issue departure-prevention orders and asset freezes where the structure is found to be a sham for tax evasion.
Unpaid Source Deductions and Part XIII Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: The full Part XIII amount becomes immediately payable, plus daily compound interest and late-payment penalties (5% plus 1% per month up to 12 months); criminal prosecution is available for wilful non-remittance.

Director personal liability: Under section 227.1 of the ITA, directors are jointly and severally liable for the entire unremitted withholding amount regardless of corporate solvency; the due-diligence defense requires proof that the director exercised reasonable care and relied on adequate internal controls.
Anti-Money Laundering / FINTRAC Compliance

Statutory sanction: PCMLTFA administrative monetary penalties of up to C$500,000 per violation for corporations, compliance orders, and criminal money-laundering charges under Criminal Code section 462.31 carrying imprisonment up to 10 years.

Director personal liability: Officers and directors who knowingly facilitate unreported cash flows can be criminally charged; FINTRAC may freeze assets, and border alert mechanisms can prevent the departure of individuals under active investigation.
