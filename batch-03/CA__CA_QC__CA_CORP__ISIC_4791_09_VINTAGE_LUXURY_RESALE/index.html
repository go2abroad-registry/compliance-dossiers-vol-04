Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — GST/HST Program, Business Registration On-Line service, and My Business Account portal for federal tax accounts; Revenu Québec — clicSÉQUR Affaires portal and Service québécois d'imposition for Quebec Sales Tax (QST) and provincial corporate tax administration

E-government filing portal

A non-resident entity carrying on business in Canada must obtain a federal GST/HST account by filing Form RC1 (Request for GST/HST Registration) with the CRA, filed directly through My Business Account or the Business Registration On-Line service. Revenu Québec requires separate QST registration via clicSÉQUR Affaires (Form LM-1), conditioned on the non-resident maintaining a physical establishment in Québec or appointing a mandatary/representative resident in Québec for service of process. The federal Business Number (BN) serves as the primary TIN; the QST account is a distinct 14-digit identifier. For online consignment and secondhand luxury resale, foreign consignors using a Canadian fulfilment centre are deemed to make taxable supplies in Canada and must register once the CAD 30,000 threshold is exceeded, even absent local incorporation.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — a Business Corporation incorporated federally under the Canada Business Corporations Act or provincially under the Québec Business Corporations Act is a taxpayer separate from its shareholders, subject to Part I federal corporate income tax and the Québec Taxation Act corporate tax regime; no flow-through transparency for corporations; Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPCs) qualify for the reduced federal and Québec small business deduction rates on active business income, while the online resale platform revenue is characterized as active business income if no foreign-controlled specified services exception applies.

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a US-style 'check-the-box' classification regime. Available statutory elections include: (i) GST/HST closely related corporation election under section 156 of the Excise Tax Act, and its QST mirror under the Québec Taxation Act, treating otherwise taxable intra-group supplies as made for nil consideration; (ii) section 216 Income Tax Act election for non-residents disposing of Canadian real property to file on a net basis; (iii) sections 73 and 85 rollover elections permitting tax-deferred transfer of vintage luxury inventory and platform goodwill into the Canadian corporation; (iv) hybrid mismatch election mechanics under Part VI.1 of the Income Tax Act (sections 18.2 and 212.3) requiring prescribed return disclosures to coordinate Canadian recharacterization of hybrid instruments or disregarded branches in cross-border resale structures; and (v) the section 156.1 GST/HST joint venture election for co-venturers operating an online marketplace.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — the CRA small-supplier threshold for GST/HST is CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies in a single calendar quarter or cumulatively over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters; Revenu Québec applies the identical CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold for QST registration; non-resident online sellers exceeding this threshold through Quebec-destined sales must register and collect both taxes.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of secondhand luxury goods exported by the vendor qualify for GST/HST zero-rating under section 1 of Part V, Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act (supply of property exported in compliance with prescribed conditions) and for QST zero-rating under section 1 of Schedule III to the Regulation respecting the Québec Sales Tax, where the vendor causes the goods to be exported promptly after the sale in accordance with ordinary commercial practice, transfers continuous possession and ownership to a non-resident purchaser outside Canada, and retains documentary proof of export, including where export occurs via a third-party freight forwarder or fulfilment-centre bridge warehouse. Online consignment intermediation services supplied to non-resident consignors may also qualify for zero-rating as services to a non-resident under section 7 of Part V, Schedule VI, provided the services are not received in Canada. Proceeds from export sales are expected to be repatriated to Canada within the administrative documentary benchmark of 180 days from the date of shipment to affirm bona fide export intent.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A or electronic export reporting through the Canadian Export Reporting System / CBSA A8.1 requiring an approved carrier or freight forwarder to report the export entry)
  • Commercial invoice and fully executed sales or consignment contract evidencing transfer of ownership and risk to the non-resident purchaser or consignee
  • Transport documentation — Bill of Lading, airway bill, courier shipment manifest, or freight forwarder certificate showing physical departure of the goods from Canada and delivery outside Canada
  • Proof of foreign currency payment settled into a Canadian financial institution, comprising SWIFT MT103, Inward Remittance Certificate, or payment-rail settlement report from Stripe or Escrow-Pay custodial accounts
  • Foreign exchange contract, payment retention records, and ledger extracts demonstrating that export proceeds are received within the contractual and documentary deadlines and are fully declared for Canadian income tax and GST/HST/QST purposes

Canada does not currently impose mandatory real-time fiscal electronic clearance, digital stamping, or continuous transaction controls for B2B/B2C invoicing. The CRA is progressively adopting the Pan-Canadian Peppol-based e-invoicing interoperability framework on a voluntary basis, and Revenu Québec is evaluating a provincial e-reporting mandate. Registrants must nevertheless issue invoices containing prescribed information (federal BN and QST registration number, tax amounts, vendor/purchaser identification), maintain electronic books and records accessible in Canada in ERP/API-ready format, and be prepared for CRA and Revenu Québec e-audit data extraction under electronic records audit protocols.

CRA and Revenu Québec subject export zero-rating claims to enhanced audit scrutiny: a registrant must produce the complete export documentation bundle on demand within a 30-day statutory books-and-records request window, and any failure to substantiate zero-rating triggers reassessment of the supply at the combined 14.975% GST/QST rate with reversal of related input tax credits, statutory interest computed from the original filing due date, and penalties under section 285 of the Excise Tax Act — the greater of CAD 100 and 25% of the net tax attributed to the misstatement, increasing to 50% where the misstatement was made knowingly or through gross negligence. Knowingly creating false export documentation or fraudulently claiming export zero-rating can additionally attract criminal prosecution under section 327 of the Excise Tax Act with fines up to CAD 50,000 and imprisonment, as well as full retrospective clawback of input tax credits previously claimed on consigned vintage luxury inventory and denial of future zero-rating status.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility on both current and capital account under IMF Article VIII. Canada maintains no exchange-control legislation, no surrender requirements, and no pre-approval gateways for foreign-currency purchases or cross-border remittances. The Canadian dollar floats freely. Profit remittance hurdles are exclusively tax-driven: non-resident shareholders are subject to Part XIII withholding tax at the 25% statutory default, generally treaty-reduced to 5%-15% on dividends, interest, and royalties; Form NR4 filing and bank-level sanctions/AML screening apply. A functional-currency election under section 261 of the Income Tax Act is available to keep the corporate books in USD, minimizing recurring CAD-conversion friction.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Not applicable. Canada imposes no statutory timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no obligation to surrender export receipts. Vintage-luxury resale revenues may be retained in USD, EUR, or CAD in any domestic or foreign account. FX gains and losses on export proceeds are ordinary income or ordinary expense for Canadian tax purposes, recorded at the Bank of Canada daily exchange rate and reported on the T2 corporation return.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); foreign exchange and monetary policy administered jointly with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for prudential regulation; Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for AML/CTF enforcement; Autorite des marches financiers (AMF) as the Quebec provincial securities and financial-services conduct regulator.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: MEDIUM with strict legal constraints. FINTRAC-registered money service businesses such as Wise Business, Payoneer, and Stripe Treasury are permissible for transaction settlement, multi-currency receiving, and low-value disbursement, but they are not Schedule I chartered banks and cannot lawfully maintain a regulated capital escrow account for a Canadian corporation. Offshore EMIs lacking Canadian MSB registration cannot hold settlement accounts for a Quebec entity and expose the company to uninsured, non-compliant treasury risk. EscrowPay is viable as an escrow technology rail only when structured as a trust/escrow arrangement with a chartered bank as custodian; funds held solely with the fintech are not CDIC-insured. Recommended architecture: Stripe as card-acquiring rail, EscrowPay as escrow settlement engine, and a Schedule I bank as the sole liquidity and final-disbursement treasury account.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant protocol: (1) Originate a SWIFT MT103 from the shareholder or intercompany account to the Quebec corporation's Schedule I bank account; (2) Populate field 70 with a transparent reference: CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION [ENTITY NAME] [AGREEMENT DATE] or DIRECTOR LOAN [ENTITY NAME] for debt injections; (3) Execute and archive the share-subscription agreement or loan agreement, board resolution, and updated corporate register; (4) Cash-equivalent deposits above CAD 10,000 require the bank's large-cash or large-EFT reporting form and FINTRAC reporting as applicable; (5) Any single credit above CAD 100,000 automatically triggers source-of-funds verification — respond with the full transaction dossier within three business days to prevent account hold. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: MEDIUM with strict legal constraints. FINTRAC-registered money service businesses such as Wise Business, Payoneer, and Stripe Treasury are permissible for transaction settlement, multi-currency receiving, and low-value disbursement, but they are not Schedule I chartered banks and cannot lawfully maintain a regulated capital escrow account for a Canadian corporation. Offshore EMIs lacking Canadian MSB registration cannot hold settlement accounts for a Quebec entity and expose the company to uninsured, non-compliant treasury risk. EscrowPay is viable as an escrow technology rail only when structured as a trust/escrow arrangement with a chartered bank as custodian; funds held solely with the fintech are not CDIC-insured. Recommended architecture: Stripe as card-acquiring rail, EscrowPay as escrow settlement engine, and a Schedule I bank as the sole liquidity and final-disbursement treasury account.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal general corporate income tax rate: 15%. Quebec general corporate income tax rate: 11.5%. Combined general rate: 26.5%. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access a federal small-business rate of 9% and a Quebec rate of approximately 3.2%, producing a combined rate of approximately 12.2% on eligible active business income within the applicable business limit, generally CAD 500,000 subject to federal and Quebec rules.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The domestic Canadian Part XIII withholding rate on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is 25%. A treaty commonly reduces the rate to 15% for portfolio dividends and to 5% where the treaty ownership and corporate-recipient conditions are satisfied; the precise rate depends on the applicable treaty, ownership percentage, voting rights, beneficial ownership and anti-abuse provisions.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The federal T2 and Quebec CO-17 are generally due within six months after the corporation's fiscal year-end. Any balance is generally due two months after year-end; a qualifying CCPC meeting the statutory conditions generally receives a three-month balance-payment period. Instalments may be required monthly or quarterly. A corporation carrying on business in Quebec generally must file both federal and Quebec corporate returns.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Generally due by the corporation's T2 filing deadline for the relevant fiscal period.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose a single universal bright-line threshold for maintaining contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation. Form T106 reporting generally applies under the applicable CAD 1 million aggregate transaction threshold rules. A Canadian taxpayer must maintain contemporaneous documentation supporting arm's-length pricing and provide it to CRA on request. Canadian entities in an MNE group with consolidated revenue of at least CAD 1 billion may have Country-by-Country reporting obligations and may be required to provide a Master File or equivalent information under the applicable international reporting framework. The CAD 1 million and CAD 1 billion thresholds must not be treated as a general exemption from local transfer-pricing documentation.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no general standalone Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to the UAE or certain offshore jurisdictions. Nevertheless, CRA may challenge arrangements under the Income Tax Act transfer-pricing rules, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule, treaty anti-abuse provisions, beneficial-ownership principles, residence and central-management-and-control concepts, and the requirement that expenses be incurred to earn income. A Canadian corporation should maintain Canadian decision-making evidence, commercial rationale, personnel or outsourced operational capacity, inventory and fulfillment records, banking controls, customer and supplier contracts, and substance proportionate to its resale activities.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Corporate income-tax evasion and false reporting

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction under the Income Tax Act may result in a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to five years. Civil reassessment, arrears interest, gross-negligence penalties and unreported-income penalties may apply separately.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal criminal exposure for knowing participation, authorization or acquiescence. Directors may also be assessed personally for certain corporate remittance liabilities. Travel restrictions are not an automatic tax sanction but may arise through ordinary criminal-process mechanisms, including bail or judicial orders.
GST/HST and QST suppression

Statutory sanction: Assessment of unremitted GST/HST or QST, interest, civil penalties and potentially criminal prosecution. False statements and tax-evasion provisions can produce substantial fines and imprisonment under federal and Quebec legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for certain unremitted GST/HST and Quebec source or consumption taxes where statutory due-diligence defenses are unavailable. Personal liability is generally imposed by statute rather than automatic piercing of the corporate veil.
Payroll and source-deduction failure

Statutory sanction: Assessment of unremitted deductions, employer contributions, interest and penalties; serious cases may attract criminal sanctions.

Director personal liability: Directors may be assessed personally under the Income Tax Act for corporate source deductions if statutory conditions are met and due diligence is not established.
Transfer-pricing non-compliance

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust income under the arm's-length principle, impose transfer-pricing penalties generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory conditions are satisfied, assess interest and apply reassessment periods or information penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors generally face corporate civil exposure rather than automatic personal transfer-pricing liability, but personal criminal exposure can arise from intentional false statements, obstruction or tax evasion.
Counterfeit, stolen or illicit luxury goods

Statutory sanction: Seizure, forfeiture, customs penalties, civil trademark or copyright damages, injunctions and potential Criminal Code prosecution for possession of property obtained by crime, fraud, trafficking or proceeds-of-crime offences. Penalties depend on the conduct and evidence.

Director personal liability: Individuals who direct, authorize, knowingly facilitate or personally participate may be prosecuted and may face restitution, forfeiture, fines or imprisonment. Corporate limited liability does not protect participants in criminal conduct.
Anti-money-laundering and FINTRAC non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties may be imposed by FINTRAC, with serious violations potentially reaching CAD 100,000 for an individual and CAD 500,000 for an entity per violation under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act framework. Criminal money-laundering offences can result in significant fines and imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Compliance officers, directors and responsible persons may face personal administrative or criminal exposure where they participated in, directed, consented to or deliberately ignored the violation. A shell company is not unlawful per se, but concealment of beneficial ownership or use for laundering or evasion is a critical red flag.
Beneficial-ownership and corporate-record failure

Statutory sanction: Corporate-law penalties, tax reassessment, denial of treaty benefits, enhanced compliance review and potential criminal or proceeds-of-crime charges may apply depending on the conduct.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face statutory penalties and criminal liability for knowingly false corporate filings or participation in concealment. Corporate veil protection does not shield fraud or deliberate statutory breaches.
Profit repatriation and withholding-tax failure

Statutory sanction: The Canadian payer may be liable for the unwithheld amount, interest and penalties, generally without being able to recover the full liability from the foreign recipient. Treaty benefits may be denied where beneficial ownership or anti-abuse requirements fail.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal liability for statutory remittance debts in appropriate cases, particularly where they authorized payments or failed to exercise due diligence. Criminal exposure requires additional facts showing knowing or fraudulent conduct.
Fraudulent insolvency or creditor prejudice

Statutory sanction: Transactions may be set aside, assets recovered, directors disqualified or sued, and criminal fraud or bankruptcy offences may apply. Tax debts, penalties and interest remain enforceable against the corporation and potentially responsible persons.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil restitution, contribution claims, statutory director-liability assessments and criminal prosecution. Imprisonment is possible for fraudulent conduct; travel bans are not automatic and require a legal basis such as bail, arrest or court order.
