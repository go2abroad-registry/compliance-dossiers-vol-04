Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; official online portal: My Business Account via canada.ca (cra-arc.gc.ca); CRA also administers Ontario corporate income tax and the Ontario component of HST on behalf of the Ontario Ministry of Finance.

E-government filing portal

For a newly incorporated federal/Ontario Business Corporation with non-resident shareholders: incorporate under the Canada Business Corporations Act or Ontario Business Corporations Act, then obtain the 9-digit federal Business Number (BN) and, if registration is required or elected, a GST/HST program account through the CRA's Business Registration Online (BRO) service or by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts). Required evidence: certified copy of articles of incorporation, certificate of corporate status, registered office address within Ontario, board of directors particulars, central securities register or shareholder list demonstrating non-resident ownership, and a resident 'person in Canada' for service of documents and records. CRA issues the BN, which serves as the statutory TIN; a separate GST/HST account number is issued where taxable supplies exceed the small supplier threshold or where voluntary registration is elected. For non-resident corporations without Canadian incorporation, the foreign entity may itself register under Part I of the Income Tax Act using Form RC1 and must provide a Canadian contact agent and address.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation (Canadian federal/Ontario corporations are separate taxable persons under Part I of the Income Tax Act; no pass-through eligibility; the corporation pays federal corporate income tax and Ontario provincial corporate tax on taxable income, with Ontario tax allocated by permanent establishment factors; non-resident shareholders are subject to Part XIII withholding tax on dividends and royalties) — Territorial_Scope: residents taxed on worldwide income; non-resident corporations taxed only on Canadian-source business income and dispositions of taxable Canadian property. — For a newly incorporated federal/Ontario Business Corporation with non-resident shareholders: incorporate under the Canada Business Corporations Act or Ontario Business Corporations Act, then obtain the 9-digit federal Business Number (BN) and, if registration is required or elected, a GST/HST program account through the CRA's Business Registration Online (BRO) service or by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts). Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation (Canadian federal/Ontario corporations are separate taxable persons under Part I of the Income Tax Act; no pass-through eligibility; the corporation pays federal corporate income tax and Ontario provincial corporate tax on taxable income, with Ontario tax allocated by permanent establishment factors; non-resident shareholders are subject to Part XIII withholding tax on dividends and royalties) — Territorial_Scope: residents taxed on worldwide income; non-resident corporations taxed only on Canadian-source business income and dispositions of taxable Canadian property.

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box election in Canada; tax classification is statutory. Available elections: (i) GST/HST election under section 156 of the Excise Tax Act to treat supplies between closely related corporations as having no consideration; (ii) section 216 Income Tax Act election for a non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada only through a permanent establishment; (iii) CCPC-status-related elections (generally unavailable where control rests with non-residents); (iv) provincial income tax allocation elections filed with CRA; (v) hybrid mismatch rules under Part VI.1 of the Income Tax Act apply automatically with no elective check-the-box, though certain anti-deferral elections exist for foreign affiliate surplus extracted through hybrid instruments.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD $30,000 small supplier threshold under section 148 of the Excise Tax Act, measured on the total taxable supplies made in Canada over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters; once exceeded, mandatory GST/HST registration applies; non-resident digital-economy suppliers of software, online games, and gaming services to consumers in Canada are subject to the same threshold and must register and collect HST where the threshold is met; voluntary registration may be elected to recover input tax credits. For Ontario, there is no separate provincial registration threshold: the Ontario 8% provincial component is collected as part of the federal-administered HST.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies by a registered Canadian corporation of software, digital content, and gaming services to a non-resident person are zero-rated under section 7 of Part V, Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act, provided that: (i) the recipient is not resident in Canada at the time of the agreement or payment; (ii) the supply is not a service rendered in connection with real property situated in Canada or tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time the service is performed; and (iii) the recipient is not an individual present in Canada when the service is rendered. For MMORPG digital downloads and in-app purchases sold through Apple App Store and Google Play to offshore end users, the Canadian developer may zero-rate the supply where the billing address, GPS data, or app-store geo-fencing confirms the end user is outside Canada. Under merchant-of-record arrangements, the platform may be the seller of record for sales to Canadian consumers, but the developer's cross-border revenues from non-resident subscribers and overseas platform settlements are zero-rated. Input tax credits for GST/HST incurred on related Canadian expenses remain available, and excess ITCs are refundable quarterly by filing Form GST66.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canadian Automated Export Declaration or Form B13A for tangible goods; for pure digital exports, a technical export record and end-user geolocation evidence is substituted)
  • Bill of Lading or air waybill (for tangible software carriers only)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (or bank transfer confirmation evidencing foreign-currency inflow from offshore subscribers or platform settlements)
  • Apple App Store financial report and Google Play payout statement (sales report, settlement report, and tax report identifying non-resident transaction locations)
  • Digital service agreement or terms of service executed with non-resident counterparties, together with IP geolocation and session logs
  • GST/HST zero-rated supply summary by non-resident jurisdiction and CRA Form GST66 for quarterly ITC refund claims

No statutory real-time fiscal clearance, no digital stamping, and no government-side e-invoice clearance mandate for GST/HST in Canada. The Pan-Canadian B2B/B2G e-invoicing framework (Peppol-based, administered by the Digital Governance Council) is voluntary and not mandated for private commerce. CRA requires GST/HST registrants to file returns electronically via GST/HST NETFILE and to issue invoices satisfying the prescribed particulars under section 3 of the Information Returns Regulations and the Excise Tax Act (supplier name or trading name, BN and GST/HST account number, date of invoice, description of supply, tax amount, and total amount). Electronic records must be retained in machine-readable format for at least six years. CRA access to third-party platform data (including Apple App Store and Google Play) is exercised through normal audit request powers, with no API-based pre-clearance.

CRA audits export zero-rating claims with a normal four-year reassessment window, extended to ten years in cases of misrepresentation, neglect, or false statements. Under section 285 of the Excise Tax Act, knowingly making a false statement or misstatement in a GST/HST return, or knowingly participating in a false zero-rating claim, attracts a civil penalty equal to the greater of $250 and 50% of the tax that was or would have been claimed, plus arrears interest at prescribed rates. Retrospective clawback of a zero-rating claim re-characterizes the supply as taxable HST at 13%, with reassessed net tax, interest, and installment penalties. Criminal tax evasion referrals to the RCMP under subsection 239(1) of the Income Tax Act and the Excise Tax Act may result in a fine up to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment for up to five years. Directors and officers may be personally liable under section 323 of the Excise Tax Act for unremitted HST where the corporation becomes unable to pay, including HST arising from clawed-back zero-rated digital supplies.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada is an IMF Article VIII jurisdiction with full convertibility for both current and capital account transactions. The Canadian dollar is freely convertible and no Bank of Canada authorization is required for profit remittance, dividend distribution, loan servicing, or capital repatriation. The primary frictions are not exchange controls but statutory tax obligations, principally Part XIII non-resident withholding taxes on dividends (25% statutory, potentially reduced by treaty) and the Investment Canada Act notification thresholds for non-resident acquisitions of control.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory export-proceeds surrender rule, mandatory conversion ratio, repatriation deadline, or requirement to sell foreign currency receipts to a Canadian bank. Export and platform proceeds may be retained in original foreign currency accounts, including USD and EUR, at a Canadian Schedule I bank. The only relevant compliance point is FINTRAC reporting: international electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more must be reported by the financial institution, and the corporate treasury must retain supporting invoices, platform payout statements, and source-of-funds documentation.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (central bank) and the Department of Finance Canada (monetary and currency policy administration); there is no separate foreign exchange control commission in Canada.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium. A foreign fintech/EMI is not permitted to take deposits in Canada unless it is licensed as a bank or authorized foreign bank. However, a foreign digital payment service provider may operate through a Canadian-registered payments entity under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA), supervised by the Bank of Canada, and may obtain a partnership with a chartered bank for settlement accounts. For an Ontario MMORPG publisher relying on Apple/Google payouts, an offshore EMI as the primary treasury bank is legally fragile; the recommended structure is a Canadian operational account at a Schedule I bank supplemented by an RPAA-registered payment processor only for merchant settlement flows.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital must arrive by direct SWIFT MT103 from the shareholder's regulated bank account; transfers from payment aggregators or crypto exchanges are unacceptable for equity capitalization. Field 70 should read 'Capital contribution to authorized share capital under Ontario Business Corporations Act' and field 71A should be set to 'OUR' to ensure that all fees are borne by the sender and the full amount reaches the Canadian beneficiary account. Use field 57A for the receiving Canadian bank's SWIFT/BIC and field 59 for the precise Ontario corporation legal name and account number. For same-day CAD clearing, use Payments Canada Lynx (RTGS); for USD inflows, use the Canadian bank's US correspondent clearing route. Pre-notify the branch for wires exceeding CAD 100,000 and deliver source of wealth evidence for the transfer. For the MMORPG treasury specifically, Apple and Google net platform payouts land into the operating account as settlement credits and must never be routed as unrestricted third-party trade payments. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

27.0% (15% federal + 12% Ontario provincial general corporate rate)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII Canadian non-resident withholding tax on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder, unless reduced by tax treaty.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return must be filed within 6 months after the end of the corporation's fiscal year (e.g., for a December 31 year-end, due June 30); balance due is due at the same 6-month deadline before interest begins accruing, although instalments apply during the year.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: The Canadian Income Tax Act and Canada Revenue Agency transfer pricing memorandum (TPM-09) require contemporaneous documentation (Local File and Master File) for transactions between Canadian-resident entities and non-resident related parties where the aggregate transactional value exceeds CAD 5,000,000 (gross revenue method: total cost plus expenses greater than CAD 5 million, or total gross revenue exceeding CAD 20 million under the OECD-aligned simplified threshold); for entities satisfying the de minimis threshold (fewer than 100 employees and total transactions under CAD 1 million or revenue under CAD 5 million), the CRA waives documentation, but the burden of proof remains and penalties apply where tax paid is less than the amount determined on an arm's length basis.

Economic substance scope: Canada applies the economic substance doctrine under the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR, ITA s.245) and for transfer pricing (s.247), requiring that intercompany contracts reflect actual economic substance and business rationale. For Canadian corporations in the MMORPG sector, the CRA will challenge arrangements where the Canadian entity lacks employees, premises, or functions to justify the recognition of profit (e.g., a 'cash-box' licensee that merely distributes revenue without development, marketing, or risk-bearing functions). While Canada does not have a standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime akin to the UAE, the rules in s.247(2) (adequate return commensurate with functions, assets and risks) effectively impose an economic substance requirement; a foreign holding company or Canadian subsidiary that is merely a shell with no employees and no active business could have its deductions denied and profit reallocated; for a Canadian subsidiary with a foreign parent, the CRA may invoke s.247(2)(a) to adjust consideration where the Canadian entity is stripped of its profit margin, especially where a foreign related 'IP holding' company earns royalties that dwarf the Canadian operational entity's return. The OECD Pillar One/Pillar Two implications also apply: a Canadian ultimate parent (for a global MMORPG group) with substantial revenue may face a global minimum top-up tax under the Income Tax Act for large multinational groups with annual revenue over EUR 750 million, beginning January 1, 2024.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False or Deceptive Filing

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act s.239(1) and Ontario Revenue Act, tax evasion is an indictable offence punishable by a fine between 50% and 200% of the tax evaded (at least 100% in aggravated cases) and imprisonment for up to 2 years; upon summary conviction, a fine of 50% of tax evaded or up to CAD 5,000 plus imprisonment up to 2 years; civil penalties under s.163.2 can reach 50% of the understated tax for false statements (gross negligence penalties) plus interest, and a CRA criminal conviction triggers a 10-year ban on relief under taxpayer relief provisions; Ontario additionally can assess a penalty of 10% of the provincial portion.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for the penalty imposed under s.163.2 (as a debt of the corporation) to the extent the directorial act or acquiescence caused the false statement; under CBCA/OBCA s.119(1), directors who vote for or acquiesce in a distribution that renders the corporation insolvent are personally liable; in CRA collections, a director's personal liability for unremitted payroll (s.227.1) applies regardless of corporate veil, and the Ontario Ministry of Finance can issue a director's assessment; fraud-based evasion can trigger civil restitution orders, preliminary asset freezes under s.462.33 Criminal Code restraint orders, and travel bans via passport seizure upon formal criminal charge.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance / Inadequate Documentation

Statutory sanction: Under ITA s.247(3), a transfer pricing penalty of 10% of the total adjustment (where the adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 and 10% of the taxpayer's gross revenue) is applied, plus interest (prescribed rate compounded daily); the adjustment also triggers reassessment of the Canadian entity's taxable income at the federal and Ontario rate; penalties are doubled for repeated non-compliance or concealment; the CRA may impose a documentation penalty of CAD 1,000 per incident (s.247(4)) and refuse to reduce the adjustment if documentation is absent; Ontario's corporate tax is recalculated on the adjusted income automatically via the CRA single-window filing.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for the corporation's tax debt only in respect to source deductions and GST/HST under s.227.1 (unremitted withholding), not ordinary income tax; however, a director who signs a false transfer pricing certificate or knowingly permits negligent documentation may be liable for the s.163.2 gross negligence penalty as a 'person' who participated in the false statement; in egregious cases, the CRA can include a director's personal net worth in a civil fraud action, and criminal charges for transfer pricing documentation fraud can result in imprisonment; travel bans may be imposed under the Criminal Code if the offence exceeds CAD 5,000.
Global Minimum Tax / Pillar Two Inaction (For Large MNE Groups)

Statutory sanction: From 2024, the Canadian Globe anti-avoidance rule (s.12 multipart rules) imposes a 15% global minimum top-up tax on excess profits of each constituent entity, enforced through CRA assessment with penalties for non-filing (T106 late filing penalty of CAD 100 per day, minimum CAD 1,000, maximum CAD 7,500 for each entity; the new 'Pillar Two' information return also carries a CAD 2,500 per month penalty up to CAD 25,000); understated tax is subject to normal reassessment and interest; Ontario will receive its share of any federal top-up under the 2021 allocation arrangement.

Director personal liability: Directors of a Canadian ultimate parent entity that fails to exercise due diligence in implementing a Pillar Two compliance system can be held civilly liable for the resulting penalties as an 'officer' under the CRA's director liability framework only if the penalty arises from an unremitted withholding obligation; for ordinary income tax, there is no direct director liability, but knowing participation in deliberate non-filing of T106/T114 forms can be prosecuted as tax evasion; criminal penalties (fine plus imprisonment up to 2 years) can attach, and a travel ban may be ordered under criminal bail conditions.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance / Sham Arrangement

Statutory sanction: GAAR (s.245) permits the CRA to deny the tax benefit; reassess to include deemed amounts; interest and penalties apply (s.163.2 for false statements); s.247(2) can reallocate the profit to the Canadian entity; the 'substance' doctrine in Canadian case law (W.T. Ramsay Ltd. v. IRC, Canada v. Golden) allows the CRA to disregard intermediary steps; Ontario can assess provincial tax using the 'adjusted net income' under the Corporations Tax Act, and the Minister may impose a 10% provincial penalty; if the arrangement is also a sham, criminal sanctions for making false statements under s.380 Criminal Code (fraud) may reach CAD 5,000,000 or 20 years imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors who incorporate or maintain a shell for tax avoidance without substance may be personally liable for the corporation's tax debt under s.227.1 if payroll or withholding remittances are involved; case law (Smith v. Canada) limits director liability only to unremitted source deductions/GST/HST, but under the Ontario Business Corporations Act, a director who authorizes a fraudulent conveyance or dividend while insolvent is personally liable for the full amount of the distribution; Canadian immigration/Passport Act permits refusal and revocation of a passport where an arrest warrant under the Criminal Code is issued for tax fraud, serving as an effective travel ban; under the Income Tax Act s.244(3), a director can be made party to a tax debt if the corporation is deemed to be an agent of the director.
Anti-Money Laundering (AML) / Failure to Report e-Transfer & Merchant Payouts

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), an unregistered money services business is liable to a fine of up to CAD 500,000 for a corporation and CAD 100,000 for directors/officers; administrative monetary penalties (AMPs) for missing STR/LCTR filings can be up to CAD 1,000,000 for corporations per violation, with aggravated penalties of over CAD 500,000; the Criminal Code authorizes forfeiture orders for proceeds of crime traced to the intercompany account; Ontario Securities Commission registration and FINTRAC registration of foreign MSBs are required if the Canadian subsidiary acts as paying agent.

Director personal liability: Directors of a FINTRAC-reporting entity that is not registered, or who knowingly allow a nominee shareholding arrangement, are personally subject to the PCMLTFA's officer liability (s.64(5)) — every officer and director 'knowingly engaged' in the offence can be fined up to CAD 500,000 or imprisoned for up to 5 years; the Criminal Code also imposes personal liability for money laundering (indictable offence, up to 10 years imprisonment) and travel bans are commonly imposed as bail conditions; under the Ontario Business Corporations Act (OBCA s.130) directors who participate in a laundering scheme can be personally liable to the corporation's creditors for resulting losses.
Unreported Offshore Income of Controlled Foreign Affiliate (FAPI / T1134)

Statutory sanction: A failure to file T1134 by the Canadian corporation results in a penalty of CAD 100 per day per form (minimum CAD 1,000, maximum CAD 7,500 per form); for repeated non-filing, penalties increase; unreported FAPI is taxed in Canada at the full corporate rate less a foreign tax credit; additionally, s.163.2 penalties for misrepresentation can reach 50% of the understated tax; the CRA's non-resident trust/foreign affiliate audit automatically expands to a transfer pricing audit; Ontario corporate tax is adjusted concurrently.

Director personal liability: Directors who fail to file T1134 on behalf of the corporation when they control the foreign affiliate may be liable for the penalty personally if the CRA designates them as a 'responsible officer' under s.162(3) (joint liability applies to every person who fails to comply with a demand to file); because the T1134 is part of the T2 bundle, the standard director liability shield applies, but under the Criminal Code a director who signs false T1134 data commits a false statement offence; travel bans can be ordered pending fraud prosecution.
