Governing statute

Quebec Business Corporations Act (QBCA), CQLR c S-31.1, ss. 6-11 (articles of incorporation), ss. 48-54 (registered office and records), ss. 106-137 (directors and officers); Act respecting the legal publicity of enterprises, CQLR c P-44.1, ss. 17-40 (enterprise registration). Formation / constitutional filings

Articles of Incorporation (statuts de constitution) filed electronically with the Registraire, accompanied by each first director's signed consent to act; upon approval the Registrar issues the Certificate of Incorporation (attestation de constitution) and the enterprise registration certificate (certificat d'immatriculation).

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – Tax Services Office and My Business Account portal for federal corporate income tax and Business Number administration; for Quebec, Revenu Québec – Revenu Québec online services and CLICK portal administers the Quebec Sales Tax (QST), Quebec corporate income tax, and, through a federal-provincial collection agreement, the GST/HST for businesses established in Quebec.

E-government filing portal

A foreign corporation must first obtain a Quebec enterprise number (NEQ) through the Registraire des entreprises du Québec if it has a physical establishment or otherwise carries on activities in Quebec, and then register for a CRA Business Number (BN) with a GST/HST account. In Quebec, the same BN and registration are used for Revenu Québec purposes under the combined General Application for Registration (form LM-1-V). The foreign entity must provide certified constitutional documents, a foreign corporate registry excerpt, the Canadian permanent establishment address, the appointed agent for service, and evidence of Canadian residence or permanent establishment for income tax. Registration is mandatory when taxable supplies exceed the CAD 30,000 small supplier threshold in Canada over four consecutive calendar quarters, or when the enterprise otherwise carries on business in Canada.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a U.S.-style check-the-box election. A federal or provincial business corporation is automatically resident in Canada and taxed at the entity level; income cannot be passed through to shareholders. Statutory elections relevant to cross-border commerce include the Income Tax Act s.85 rollover election for transferring property into the corporation, s.216 and s.217 elections for non-residents' rental and employment income, and GST/HST-related elections for closely related registrants. Hybrid mismatch arrangements are neutralized by self-executing rules in the Income Tax Act rather than elective mechanisms.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of taxable supplies in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters for GST/HST and QST small supplier purposes; once exceeded, registration and collection become mandatory, though voluntary registration can be elected earlier to recover input tax credits.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A zero-rated export claim is available where a Canadian supplier sells and physically exports goods to a destination outside Canada, including fast-fashion apparel and footwear sold through e-commerce, using its own delivery vehicles or a common carrier, courier, or mail service. Offshore services and digital supplies qualify for zero-rating only when supplied to a person outside Canada and not in connection with real property situated in Canada, tangible personal property situated in Canada, or services performed wholly or partly in Canada for a recipient present in Canada. For the fast-fashion retail model, the sale must be fulfilled from a Canadian warehouse or shipment point to a confirmed foreign address, and the exporter must obtain documentary proof before claiming the zero rate or refund.

  • Canadian Export Declaration via CBSA Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) or legacy Form B13 for controlled goods and shipments above applicable value thresholds
  • Bill of Lading, air waybill, or courier waybill showing final foreign destination and date of export
  • Commercial invoice, sales contract, and purchase order evidencing the cross-border sale
  • Packing list and cargo manifest matching the zero-rated line items
  • Inward Remittance Certificate, SWIFT MT103, or bank credit advice evidencing settlement of the export receivable
  • Carrier certificate, in-transit inventory reports, and e-commerce fulfilment records proving physical removal from Canada

Canada and Quebec do not currently impose real-time fiscal clearance, API tax stamping, or mandatory government pre-clearance of electronic invoices. GST/HST and QST are self-assessed and remitted through periodic returns. Mandatory electronic filing applies to larger registrants; invoices may be issued electronically, including through e-commerce platforms, provided they contain the supplier's registration numbers and the tax charged. Revenu Québec continues to develop e-invoicing/e-reporting policy, but no operative QR-code or real-time clearing mandate exists for fast-fashion e-commerce retailers.

CRA and Revenu Québec may reassess within the normal four-year limitation period, and beyond it where misrepresentation or fraud exists. If the exporter cannot substantiate physical export, the zero-rating is reversed and GST/QST becomes payable with arrears interest. A false or incomplete export claim can result in disallowance of input tax credits, clawback of refunds, false-statement penalties of up to 25%, and criminal prosecution for tax evasion under the Excise Tax Act and Quebec's Tax Administration Act. Export records should be retained for at least six years.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is freely convertible for ordinary current-account and capital-account transactions. There is generally no exchange-control registration, quota, capital-repatriation approval, mandatory use of a domestic bank, or statutory foreign-currency surrender requirement for a Canadian corporation. A Quebec corporation may generally receive foreign investment, convert currencies, pay overseas suppliers, remit dividends, repay shareholder loans, and repatriate invested capital, subject to corporate-law solvency requirements, tax withholding, transfer-pricing rules, anti-money-laundering controls, sanctions, contractual bank controls, and applicable reporting. Dividends and certain interest, royalty, management-fee, and similar payments to non-residents may be subject to Canadian withholding tax, commonly reduced by an applicable income-tax treaty. Thin-capitalization, interest-limitation, transfer-pricing, shareholder-benefit, non-resident withholding, customs, GST/HST, Quebec QST, and beneficial-ownership considerations must be reviewed before execution. Payments involving sanctioned jurisdictions, sanctioned persons, restricted goods, or unusual third-party beneficiaries may be delayed, rejected, frozen, or reported.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no statutory requirement to surrender or mandatorily convert export proceeds, no prescribed conversion ratio, and no general statutory deadline for converting export receipts. Export proceeds may normally remain in Canadian-dollar or foreign-currency accounts and may be converted at the corporation's treasury discretion. The company must retain commercially credible invoices, shipping and customs records, payment evidence, customer contracts, and accounting records. Foreign assets and accounts may trigger CRA reporting, including Form T1135 where the applicable specified-foreign-property threshold and filing conditions are met. Foreign-currency balances and transactions must be recorded under applicable Canadian accounting and tax rules, with exchange gains and losses appropriately recognized.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada. Canada has no general foreign-exchange administration requiring approval for ordinary commercial remittances. Relevant complementary authorities include the Department of Finance Canada, the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), the Autorité des marchés financiers (AMF) for Quebec-regulated derivatives and financial-market matters, and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for federally regulated financial institutions.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for payment collection and treasury support; Low as a substitute for a primary Canadian operating bank. An offshore electronic-money institution or payment institution generally cannot be assumed to provide Canadian deposit-taking, Canadian payment services, Canadian-dollar clearing, or safeguarded funds access without an appropriate Canadian legal and regulatory structure. A non-bank payment service provider serving end users in Canada may be subject to registration and operational obligations under the Retail Payment Activities Act and related Bank of Canada supervision, depending on its activities, timing, and transitional status. FINTRAC registration and money-services-business obligations may also apply. Offshore EMI accounts should not be used to obscure beneficial ownership, bypass Canadian sanctions or AML controls, avoid tax reporting, or route unrelated third-party funds. Shopify Payments and Klarna availability depends on merchant eligibility, underwriting, prohibited-business rules, settlement terms, chargeback performance, Canadian entity and bank-account verification, and the applicable Canadian service agreements.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Route subscribed share capital, shareholder loans, and other funding through an account held in the exact legal name of the Canadian corporation. Use a regulated bank or appropriately registered payment service provider, with full originator and beneficiary information. Before remittance, prepare the subscription agreement or loan agreement, board approval, capitalization table, beneficial-ownership records, source-of-funds evidence, and intended-use schedule. Instruct the remitter to identify the legal purpose accurately, such as share subscription, shareholder loan, intercompany loan, or working-capital funding. Canada generally has no universal statutory FX purpose-code system; the bank may nevertheless require an internal purpose, transaction classification, invoice, contract, or remittance explanation. Reconcile the incoming wire to the corporate ledger and preserve payment messages and bank advices. Do not route capital through a personal account, unrelated group entity, undisclosed nominee, or third-party collection account. Confirm whether a shareholder loan is arm's-length, whether interest is payable, whether withholding tax applies, and whether thin-capitalization and interest-limitation rules affect deductions.Use the Canadian operating account or a documented foreign-currency account to pay genuine suppliers and service providers. Standard bank wires may be sent using SWIFT MT103 or the bank's ISO 20022 successor message, with complete originator, beneficiary, bank, invoice, and purpose information. For larger or higher-risk purchases, use a commercial documentary letter of credit, standby letter of credit, documentary collection, or controlled open-account process. A commercial LC should specify applicant, beneficiary, issuing and advising banks, amount, currency, expiry, shipment terms, Incoterms, required transport and customs documents, inspection requirements, discrepancy rules, and sanctions clauses. Match invoices, purchase orders, bills of lading or airway bills, certificates of origin, customs entries, proof of delivery, and payment records. Apply sanctions, supplier, forced-labour, product-origin, and export-control screening before release. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Combined general corporate rate of 26.5% (federal 15% + Quebec 11.5%). The effective rate is reduced to approximately 12.2% on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income eligible for the federal Small Business Deduction (federal 9%) and the Quebec Small Business Deduction (Quebec 3.2%).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid to non-resident shareholders, including deemed dividends on shareholder appropriations; the rate applies to dividends from Canadian-resident corporations.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

T2 must be filed within 6 months after the fiscal year-end. The tax balance is payable within 2 months after year-end for a non-CCPC, and within 3 months after year-end for a CCPC claiming the federal Small Business Deduction.

Deadline: · Frequency:

CO-17 must be filed within 6 months after the fiscal year-end. The Quebec tax balance is payable within 2 months after year-end, or within 3 months for a CCPC eligible for the Quebec Small Business Deduction.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Form T106 must be filed when the aggregate value of non-arm's length cross-border transactions with a Canadian resident exceeds CAD 1,000,000 in a fiscal year; Country-by-Country Report (CbCR) and Master File requirements apply to MNE groups with consolidated annual group revenue of at least CAD 750,000,000. Contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation should be maintained for all intercompany transactions regardless of size, with preparation within a reasonable time before the filing due date.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone Economic Substance Regulations for ordinary corporations, but the common-law economic substance doctrine applies through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA s. 245; Quebec equivalent in the Taxation Act) and the sham transaction doctrine. A fast-fashion e-commerce entity with no employees, no Quebec office, no management presence, and no operational controls can be recharacterized; CRA may assert a permanent establishment is present in Quebec based on dependent agents, storage premises, or digital infrastructure, thereby subjecting the e-commerce revenue to Quebec and federal corporate tax. GAAR can also deny treaty benefits where the principal purpose is to obtain treaty relief.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: ITA s. 239(1): fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years; Quebec Tax Administration Act s. 65 provides parallel penalties; CRA may also impose gross negligence penalties of 50% of the understated tax and seize business assets.

Director personal liability: Directors who directed, participated in, or acquiesced in the evasion face criminal prosecution, personal fines, civil restitution, seizure of personal assets, and imprisonment; bail conditions may include passport surrender and travel bans.
Gross Negligence and Late Filing

Statutory sanction: Federal late-filing penalty of 5% plus 1% per month (up to 12 months); repeat failure raises the base to 10% plus 2% per month (up to 20%); Quebec late-filing penalty of 5% plus 0.5% per month; gross negligence penalty of 50% for false statements; interest accrues daily.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for unremitted income tax withholdings, GST/QST, and employer source deductions under ITA s. 227.1 and the Excise Tax Act s. 323; CRA can assess directors personally without first exhausting corporate recovery, and may place liens on personal residences.
Shell Company and GAAR

Statutory sanction: GAAR recharacterization under ITA s. 245 denies the tax benefit; the new GAAR penalty of 50% of the net tax benefit applies; sham doctrine may result in the transaction being ignored entirely; Quebec GAAR applies similarly and can trigger reassessment with interest; civil penalties up to CAD 100,000 for specified misleading statements.

Director personal liability: Canadian courts may pierce the corporate veil where the entity is an instrumentality of fraud; directors and controlling shareholders may be held personally liable for corporate tax debts, charged with conspiracy, subjected to asset freezes, and banned from leaving Canada pending resolution.
Transfer Pricing Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: ITA s. 247(3) imposes a penalty of 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment; failure to file T106 attracts daily administrative penalties under ITA s. 162; CRA may apply the new CbCR penalty for groups above the CAD 750,000,000 threshold; Quebec applies the same assessments through the taxation agreement.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign T106 information returns without reasonable care may be jointly liable for filing penalties; willful misstatement in TP documentation can be prosecuted as taxpayer fraud; Revenu Quebec and CRA joint audits can request director interviews and assert director liability for unremitted payroll-derived taxes.
Sales Tax (GST/QST) and Payroll Remittance Failure

Statutory sanction: Excise Tax Act s. 323 makes directors personally liable for unremitted GST/QST; penalties of up to 10% of unremitted amounts, plus 20% for a repeat failure, and daily interest; Revenu Quebec can certify and register a legal hypothec on personal property; criminal fines of CAD 10,000 to CAD 500,000 apply for fraudulent sales-tax evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors can be assessed personally for 100% of unremitted GST/QST and source deductions, subject to the due-diligence defence; failure to exercise reasonable care results in personal bank account garnishments, director travel restrictions, and disqualification from serving as a director of any Canadian corporation for up to 5 years.
