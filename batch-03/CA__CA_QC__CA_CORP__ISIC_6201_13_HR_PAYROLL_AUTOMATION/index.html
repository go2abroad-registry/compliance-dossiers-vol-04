Competent corporate registry

Registraire des entreprises du Québec (Quebec Enterprise Registrar), filing channel via Services Québec enterprise portal (services.registraire.gouv.qc.ca).

Governing statute

Business Corporations Act (Québec) — Loi sur les sociétés par actions, CQLR c S-31.1, principally Part I (incorporation and organization), ss 11 (articles of incorporation), 19 (registered office), 32 (organizational meeting), 113-120 (by-laws and officers); Charter of the French Language, CQLR c C-11, for mandatory French-language standard documents.

Formation / constitutional filings

Articles of Incorporation (statuts constitutifs) filed with the Registrar and recorded in the Registre des entreprises du Québec, resulting in a certificate of incorporation and enterprise number (NEQ).

Incorporation processing time

⚡ 20 business days (SOP sum)

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital and no mandatory escrow account under the QBCA; authorized and issued share capital is fixed in the articles, and issued shares must be fully paid in money, property or past services as consideration. Shareholder identification is disclosed through a mandatory declaration of information filed with the Registraire des entreprises.

Funding & unlock path: Fire safety compliance certificate from the competent municipal fire safety service, building occupancy permit confirming structural load capacity for office use, and employer registration with the CNESST under the Act respecting occupational health and safety, including installation of first aid equipment, emergency exits, workstation ergonomics, and workplace access compliance.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), including the Business Registration Online (BRO) portal and My Business Account; for Quebec provincial tax administration, Revenu Quebec, including the clicSÉQUR–Entreprises portal. The CRA administers federal corporation income tax, GST/HST, payroll deductions, and the federal Business Number (BN). Revenu Quebec administers Quebec corporate income tax, Quebec Sales Tax (QST), Quebec source deductions, and Quebec enterprise registrations.

E-government filing portal

A foreign corporation carrying on business in Canada, registering for GST/HST, maintaining payroll obligations, or requiring CRA program accounts must obtain a nine-digit Business Number from the CRA through Business Registration Online, by telephone, or through the prescribed non-resident registration process. The corporation must provide its legal name, jurisdiction and date of incorporation, registered office, directors, authorized representative, Canadian business activities, fiscal period, and non-resident contact details. It must then request the applicable CRA program accounts, including RC for corporate income tax, RT for GST/HST, RP for payroll deductions, and RM where import-export activities apply. A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada generally files a T2 Corporation Income Tax Return even if treaty relief is claimed. A Quebec registrant must separately register with Revenu Quebec for QST and, where applicable, Quebec source deductions and corporate income tax. The registration package should retain incorporation documents, proof of legal existence, ownership and control information, authorization documents, Canadian business-contact information, expected taxable supplies, and banking or payment evidence. GST/HST and QST registration must occur before the applicable registration effective date; voluntary registration may be available below the small-supplier threshold, subject to filing and collection obligations.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a general United States-style check-the-box election for corporations. A corporation incorporated in Canada is generally a separate taxpayer, and a foreign corporation is classified under Canadian law based on its legal characteristics and residence or Canadian business activities. Relevant elections may include a section 85 rollover for qualifying transfers of property to a taxable Canadian corporation, subsection 216(1) elections for certain Canadian rental income of non-residents, section 217 elections for specified pension and similar income, and treaty-based positions concerning permanent establishment and residence. Canadian partnership, trust, and corporate classifications must be determined under Canadian law; foreign hybrid-entity treatment can create mismatch, withholding, foreign tax credit, and treaty issues. Quebec generally follows federal entity characterization but has separate provincial elections and filing requirements.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, determined under the applicable small-supplier rules, generally measured over the relevant preceding four-quarter period or the current quarter. Exceeding the threshold generally requires GST/HST registration and collection from the effective date prescribed by the Excise Tax Act. Quebec generally applies a CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold for QST registration, subject to separate Revenu Quebec rules. Voluntary registration is generally possible below the threshold. A non-resident supplying digital services to Canadian consumers may have simplified GST/HST registration obligations under the cross-border digital-economy rules, while a supplier carrying on business in Quebec may require ordinary QST registration.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

For GST/HST, exported goods are generally zero-rated where the statutory export conditions are satisfied, including delivery or transfer outside Canada and retention of prescribed evidence that the goods left Canada. Exported services may be zero-rated where supplied to a non-resident person and the service is not in respect of real property, tangible personal property, or another excluded Canadian-connected matter, and where the recipient does not use or consume the service in Canada. Payroll automation SaaS supplied to a non-resident business may qualify where the contractual recipient is genuinely non-resident and the platform and related services are not used in Canada; services supplied to or used by Canadian establishments, employees, or operations require transaction-specific analysis. QST may be zero-rated for qualifying exported services and goods under Quebec rules, but the QST zero-rating analysis is separate from GST/HST. Input tax credits or input tax refunds require valid tax documentation, commercial-purpose substantiation, and a direct connection to commercial activities. Zero-rating is not established solely by foreign currency settlement, a foreign bank account, or a non-Canadian billing address.

  • Commercial invoice identifying the supplier, purchaser, description, value, destination, and contractual terms
  • Bill of lading, airway bill, courier record, or equivalent transport evidence for exported goods
  • Proof of delivery or receipt outside Canada
  • Customer contract, purchase order, and tax invoice identifying the non-resident recipient and exported service
  • Evidence of the recipient's non-resident status and the place of use or benefit of the SaaS or other service
  • Foreign exchange or bank settlement records where relevant; Canada does not generally require an inward-remittance certificate as a condition of GST/HST or QST zero-rating
  • GST/HST and QST registration records, general ledger, tax return workpapers, and input-tax-credit support

Canada and Quebec do not generally impose a nationwide real-time electronic invoicing clearance mandate for ordinary B2B or B2C SaaS supplies. Tax invoices must contain the prescribed GST/HST or QST information, including supplier identity and registration number, date, consideration, tax rate, tax amount, and sufficient description of the supply. Electronic invoices are generally acceptable if integrity, authenticity, accessibility, and audit-readability are maintained. CRA and Revenu Quebec generally permit electronic books and records subject to retention and audit-access requirements. Businesses must file GST/HST and QST returns electronically where mandatory thresholds or administrative rules apply; payroll remittances and certain corporate filings may also require electronic filing. Public-sector procurement or specific contract arrangements may impose separate electronic invoicing standards, but there is no general continuous transaction-control, fiscal-stamping, or mandatory government pre-clearance system for this activity.

Canada has no general statutory requirement to repatriate export proceeds within 180 days and no general inward-remittance certificate requirement for export zero-rating. CRA and Revenu Quebec may deny zero-rating, assess uncollected GST/HST or QST, reverse input tax credits or refunds, and impose arrears interest where export conditions or documentary support are not established. Gross-negligence penalties may apply where a false statement or omission reflects gross negligence; repeated or deliberate non-compliance can attract additional administrative penalties, civil collection measures, and prosecution. False customs declarations may result in reassessment, monetary penalties, seizure, and other consequences under the Customs Act and CBSA enforcement programs. Directors may face personal liability for certain unremitted GST/HST, QST, and payroll source deductions, subject to statutory due-diligence and limitation rules. Assessment periods may be extended or reassessments made beyond ordinary periods in cases of misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, wilful blindness, or fraud.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada has a freely floating Canadian dollar and no general exchange-control regime. Current-account transactions, including payment for services, software subscriptions, payroll-related services, imports, and ordinary commercial expenses, are generally freely convertible and transferable through Canadian financial institutions. Capital-account transactions, including equity subscriptions, shareholder loans, intercompany funding, dividends, interest, royalties, and return of capital, are generally permitted without prior foreign-exchange approval. Banks may nevertheless require source-of-funds evidence, corporate resolutions, loan or subscription agreements, beneficial-ownership information, tax documentation, sanctions screening, and a commercially credible payment purpose. Dividends and other distributions are subject to applicable corporate-law solvency tests, available corporate funds, withholding-tax rules, transfer-pricing requirements, thin-capitalization rules, and any treaty relief conditions. Payments to non-residents may require Canadian withholding tax, commonly 25 percent absent a reduced treaty rate, exemption, or statutory exception. Quebec tax registration, payroll obligations, privacy requirements, and corporate records do not create a general capital-control restriction but can affect the bank's risk assessment and payment release.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement, mandatory conversion ratio, or general deadline requiring exporters to convert foreign-currency receipts into Canadian dollars. Export proceeds may normally remain in foreign-currency accounts or be converted at the company's discretion. The company must maintain accurate books, support the commercial purpose and beneficial recipient of receipts, comply with applicable GST/HST and Quebec tax reporting, maintain records for tax and audit purposes, and satisfy bank anti-money-laundering, sanctions, source-of-funds, and transaction-monitoring requirements. Foreign-currency balances and related gains or losses must be accounted for under the applicable financial-reporting framework and Canadian tax rules.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada; the Department of Finance Canada is responsible for federal financial-sector policy; the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) administers federal anti-money-laundering and anti-terrorist-financing obligations; the Autorité des marchés financiers (AMF) supervises Quebec-regulated financial-market and derivatives activities.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for payment collection, foreign-currency conversion, expense management, and treasury support; low as a substitute for a Canadian operating bank account where the provider lacks Canadian authorization or direct access to domestic clearing. Canada does not use a single European-style electronic-money-institution licence. A payment service provider performing in-scope payment functions in Canada may be required to register with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act and maintain operational-risk, safeguarding, incident-reporting, and compliance controls. Money-services-business activities may require FINTRAC registration and related AML compliance. An offshore electronic-money institution cannot assume that its home-country licence authorizes Canadian payment services, deposit-taking, safeguarding of Canadian customer funds, or access to Canadian clearing. The company should verify Canadian regulatory status, client-money safeguarding, settlement-bank identity, insolvency treatment, sanctions controls, data location, withdrawal rights, and whether funds are held as deposits, payment funds, or trust property. Fintech balances should generally be supplementary to, rather than a replacement for, a Canadian bank operating account.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Route equity through a Canadian bank account in the exact legal name of the corporation, preferably from the subscribing shareholder's account or a clearly documented group treasury account. Use a SWIFT wire or another bank-supported international transfer method with complete originator, beneficiary, intermediary-bank, and ultimate-beneficiary information. Identify the payment as equity subscription, paid-in capital, or shareholder loan, using the bank's available payment-purpose field; Canada generally has no statutory universal purpose-code regime for ordinary inbound capital transfers, so the bank's internal code and narrative should be used accurately. Before receipt, provide the bank with the subscription or loan agreement, board and shareholder approvals, capitalization table, ownership chart, source-of-funds evidence, lender identity, tax-residency information, and expected amount, currency, frequency, and countries. Ensure the wire beneficiary name, account number, transit details, and legal entity name match exactly. Reconcile the receipt to the general ledger, issue the relevant share or debt documentation, retain records, and assess thin-capitalization, transfer-pricing, withholding-tax, foreign-reporting, corporate-law solvency, and GST/HST or Quebec tax implications. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium for payment collection, foreign-currency conversion, expense management, and treasury support; low as a substitute for a Canadian operating bank account where the provider lacks Canadian authorization or direct access to domestic clearing. Canada does not use a single European-style electronic-money-institution licence. A payment service provider performing in-scope payment functions in Canada may be required to register with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act and maintain operational-risk, safeguarding, incident-reporting, and compliance controls. Money-services-business activities may require FINTRAC registration and related AML compliance. An offshore electronic-money institution cannot assume that its home-country licence authorizes Canadian payment services, deposit-taking, safeguarding of Canadian customer funds, or access to Canadian clearing. The company should verify Canadian regulatory status, client-money safeguarding, settlement-bank identity, insolvency treatment, sanctions controls, data location, withdrawal rights, and whether funds are held as deposits, payment funds, or trust property. Fintech balances should generally be supplementary to, rather than a replacement for, a Canadian bank operating account.
