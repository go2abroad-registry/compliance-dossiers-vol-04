Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital and no escrow or capital deposit requirement for a Québec limited partnership. Each limited partner must validly make the contribution agreed in the partnership agreement; a limited partner who has not contributed acquires status only upon actual contribution. The general partner has full capacity to bind the partnership and is unlimitedly liable for its obligations.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada — federal income tax and GST/HST administration; portal: https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html. In Québec, Revenu Québec (RQ) administers the QST and provincial tax system; portal: https://www.revenuquebec.ca/en/. The Registraire des entreprises du Québec (https://www.registreentreprises.gouv.qc.ca) issues the provincial NEQ.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Register the limited partnership with the Registraire des entreprises du Québec to obtain the NEQ (Quebec enterprise number). Step 2: Apply to the CRA for a federal Business Number (BN) using Business Registration Online (BRO) or Form RC1; request a GST/HST account (for the LP's taxable supplies) and a T5013/Partnership Information Return account. Step 3: Register for QST with Revenu Québec using Form LM-1 or the clicSÉCU Affaires online service, referencing the NEQ; RQ issues a separate QST account number. Non-resident partners may file via an authorized representative (CRA Form RC59 / RQ authorization mandate) and are allocated income through the partnership's T5013/TP-600 information returns; the LP itself remains transparent and is not a taxpayer.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — a Québec LP is fiscally transparent under ITA s. 96 and the Québec Taxation Act: no entity-level tax; each partner includes its allocable share computed as if the partnership's activities were carried on by the partner directly. Non-resident partners remain taxable in Canada on Canadian-source partnership income (Part I) and may be subject to Part XIII withholding on certain passive allocations.

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a US-style check-the-box election. The transparent LP treatment is the default and is modified only via narrow statutory elections: (i) ITA s. 96(3) (with the parallel Québec provision) permits a partnership, on a joint prescribed-form election by all partners, to be deemed a corporation for limited income-tax purposes in specified cross-border contexts; (ii) a partnership may elect its fiscal period for tax purposes under ITA s. 249.1 and the Québec equivalent (December 31 remains the statutory default for most LPs); (iii) no hybrid-mismatch 'curative election' is available — Canada's anti-hybrid/EIFEL rules (ITA ss. 18.2/18.3 and related provisions) automatically disallow deductions for hybrid mismatch amounts; CRA administrative entity-classification guidance (Income Tax Folio S4-F3-C1) only recognizes foreign check-the-box outcomes for previously 'orphan' hybrid entities and cannot convert a domestic Québec LP into an opaque corporation for Canadian tax.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — the GST/HST and QST 'small supplier' threshold. Registration is mandatory when the LP's worldwide taxable supplies (other than zero-rated supplies) exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or in the four consecutive calendar quarters, measured equivalently for GST/HST (ETA s. 148) and QST (Québec Sales Tax Act); exports of SaaS services to non-residents that qualify as zero-rated are excluded from the threshold computation.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating (0% GST/HST and QST) is available for a supply of a 'specified service' — including SaaS/software services — made to a non-resident recipient, provided the service is not in respect of real property situated in Canada and not in respect of tangible personal property situated in Canada, and provided the recipient is outside Canada at the time the service is rendered (ETA Schedule VI Part V s. 7; mirror QST rule). For the PropTech/real-estate platform, the supply must be characterized as a software/platform service delivered electronically to non-resident clients; if the platform also manages, markets, or screens Canadian real-property portfolios (i.e., the supply relates to Quebec real property), the zero-rating is unavailable and the full supply is taxable at 14.5%. Intermediary B2B supplies to non-resident resellers may also qualify if the reseller's onward supply is to other non-residents and the Canadian supplier has no knowledge of supply to Canada.

  • GST/HST and QST export invoice issued electronically showing 'Export / Zero-Rated (0%)' and the non-resident recipient's billing address outside Canada
  • Signed SaaS Master Subscription Agreement / order form identifying the non-resident client, jurisdiction of use, and delivery method (no Canadian land nexus)
  • Non-resident status evidence (foreign business registration extract or tax-residence certificate, plus the client's declaration that it is not resident in Canada)
  • Delivery and usage records (server/API access logs, IP geolocation evidence, customer onboarding records) demonstrating actual offshore receipt of the SaaS
  • Payment/consideration records (Stripe Billing payout statements, ACH/SWIFT receipts) tracing consideration from the non-resident payer
  • Where tangible hardware (e.g., IoT sensors for property management) is exported: CBSA export documentation (B13A / eManifest export reporting), bill of lading or airway waybill, and proof of export within a reasonable time
  • If applicable, a Form GST189 or CRA-issued non-resident registration proof for the purchaser (only for goods consigned to Canada for export)

Canada and Québec do not currently mandate CTC (continuous transaction control) real-time clearance, digital fiscal stamps, or government pre-approval of invoices. Invoices must contain the prescribed GST/HST particulars and be issued for all taxable supplies. However, Revenu Québec has announced a phased mandatory e-invoicing and e-reporting regime for Québec taxable suppliers (pursuant to its 2023–2027 digitalization plan), commencing with large enterprises in the 2026–2027 window and extending to smaller businesses such as the subject LP in subsequent phases (target full-scope application before 2031); in-scope taxpayers must transmit structured e-invoices (e.g., PEPPOL/UBL format) through certified service providers and provide RQ with electronic sales reporting. For visibility at present, the LP must keep its SaaS billing records (Stripe Billing, ACH) in an auditable electronic format and file GST/HST/QST returns electronically.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is freely convertible on both capital and current accounts. Canada accepts IMF Article VIII status, meaning there are no exchange-control restrictions on payments or transfers for current international transactions, no prior approval for profit remittances, and no restriction on holding foreign currency in Canada or abroad. Capital account flows, including non-resident limited partner capital contributions, partnership distributions, and repatriation of invested funds, are generally unrestricted; the only material hurdles are standard AML/KYC requirements, FINTRAC electronic funds transfer reports for transactions equal to or above CAD 10,000, sanctions screening under the United Nations Act and the Special Economic Measures Act, Investment Canada Act review for large or national-security-sensitive acquisitions, and ordinary Canadian and Quebec income tax compliance including any applicable treaty-based withholding characterization. For a Quebec PropTech SaaS limited partnership, profit remittance presents no FX-control blockage and is constrained only by tax reporting, account documentation, and banking compliance.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Not applicable. Canada imposes no statutory export-proceeds surrender regime, no mandatory conversion timeline, and no minimum conversion ratio for export receipts. Exporters may lawfully retain foreign currency in Canadian or offshore accounts and convert to CAD at commercial market rates whenever commercially desired. Documentary compliance is nonetheless required: retain executed export or SaaS subscription contracts and payment records for CRA purposes, comply with CBSA export declaration requirements where goods are exported, and ensure the receiving bank files any required FINTRAC EFT report when an incoming wire equals or exceeds CAD 10,000. There is no central-bank directive requiring repatriation of export proceeds within a fixed number of days.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), with the foreign exchange policy function formally assigned to the Minister of Finance and administered through the Bank of Canada; prudential supervision of federally regulated banks is performed by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI). Canada does not operate a centralized foreign-exchange control administration or exchange-control licensing bureau.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium as a payment-processing auxiliary but Low as a primary treasury relationship. An offshore digital EMI cannot act as a bank in Canada unless authorized under the Bank Act; for payment services it must be registered as a money services business with FINTRAC and, if operating in Quebec, must hold a money-services-business permit issued by the Autorité des marchés financiers (AMF). A non-resident EMI is also subject to the federal Retail Payment Activities Act and may be required to register with the Bank of Canada once the registration phase is fully in force. The compliant architecture is to maintain the principal Escrow and Operating Accounts at a Tier 1 Canadian Schedule I bank and use a licensed EMI or PSP solely as a supplementary disbursement rail, with daily zero-balance sweeps back to the Canadian bank. Investment of partner capital or dividend distribution must not be warehoused through an unregulated offshore EMI.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Non-resident limited partner capital injections should be routed as a single clean wire through SWIFT MT103 or the equivalent ISO 20022 pacs.008 message to the Capital Escrow Account held at the selected Schedule I Canadian bank. The remittance information field must clearly state: CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION, followed by the exact legal name of the Quebec LP and the partner reference. Canada imposes no national mandatory purpose-code taxonomy for cross-border wires, so the bank's portal should be completed with the reason for payment categorized as capital investment or treasury contribution rather than trade invoice; where an ISO ExternalPurposeCode is mandatory in the correspondent bank system, use the bank-accepted commercial payment or capital transfer code displayed by the sending bank's treasury portal. The verification package must include the executed limited partnership subscription agreement, capital call notice, source-of-funds documentation for each contributing partner, tax identification data, and a FINTRAC record where the wire is equal to or exceeds CAD 10,000. Contributions above CAD 100,000 will normally trigger enhanced due diligence and may require the partner's audited financials or constitutional documents. Payments must never be fragmented to avoid FINTRAC reporting thresholds.Outbound disbursements should be executed through the primary bank using the following clearing hierarchy: (i) SWIFT MT103 / ISO 20022 pacs.008 for international vendor payments, non-resident partner distributions, and cross-border service fees, with a meaningful purpose narrative in the remittance field; (ii) Fedwire or CHIPS via the bank's U.S. correspondent for high-value USD wires; (iii) NACHA ACH via the bank's U.S. clearing account for USD ACH Direct payouts to U.S.-based vendors and contractors; (iv) Payments Canada ACSS for domestic CAD direct debit and credit payments, and LVTS for high-value same-day CAD transfers; and (v) commercial letters of credit, issued by SWIFT MT700 or MT710, for hardware procurement or other equipment imports used in the PropTech solution, with confirmation from an overseas correspondent bank where supplier risk requires it. Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined federal and Quebec statutory corporate income tax rate for a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) earning active business income eligible for the federal small business deduction, before provincial abatement adjustments; general federal rate 15%, Quebec rate 11.5%, resulting in 26.5% for non-CCPC or general income; active business income under the CCPC small business limit is subject to a reduced combined rate of approximately 12.2% (federal 9% effective after abatement plus Quebec 3.2% small business deduction rate), with the federal small business limit of CAD 500,000 and Quebec small business limit of CAD 500,000, subject to phase-out rules.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% statutory withholding on dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident beneficial owner, under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act, and 25% under Quebec's equivalent withholding provisions (Quebec Taxation Act); for a limited partnership, withholding is not triggered by the mere distribution of the partner's tax-paid earnings except where the distribution is characterized as a dividend from a corporation deemed to be resident in Canada or where the partnership makes a payment of interest, rent, royalties, or management fees to a non-resident partner; the higher statutory rate applies unless a tax treaty reduces it.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: For Canadian federal tax purposes, Transfer Pricing Master File (TPMF) and Local File (TPLF) documentation are required for taxation years beginning on or after July 1, 2020, where: (a) the aggregate fair market value of transactions with non-resident related parties exceeds CAD 1,000,000 for acquisitions, dispositions, or loans; (b) the total amount of intercompany services charged exceeds CAD 1,000,000; or (c) the entity has gross revenue over CAD 50,000,000; Master File must be produced within 3 months after the corporation's fiscal year-end and Local File within 6 months after the fiscal year-end; Quebec's provincial transfer pricing documentation threshold mirrors the federal requirements under the Taxation Act, and Revenu Québec may demand production of prescribed documentation (Form CO-552) when transactions with non-residents exceed CAD 1,000,000 per year; for this PropTech SaaS platform with a cross-border US revenue stream, mandatory Local File and Master File thresholds are triggered when gross royalty-like payments, intercompany management fees, technology license fees, or any related-party loans from non-residents exceed CAD 1,000,000; the Canadian documentation must include the transactional business strategy, contractual terms, economic analysis, and benchmark comparability study.

Economic substance scope: Under Canada's general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) in section 245 of the Income Tax Act, a transaction must have a bona fide purpose, economic substance, and cannot be structured solely to avoid taxes; although Quebec does not have standalone Economic Substance Regulations modelled on the UAE or Cayman ESR, the federal income tax system and Revenu Québec's anti-avoidance provisions (Quebec Taxation Act sections 1074.1 to 1074.3) apply an economic substance doctrine requiring that the entity carry on real business activity, employ staff, incur operating expenses, assume economic risk, and exercise real decision-making functions (people functions) in Canada; a Quebec limited partnership used for a PropTech SaaS platform must demonstrate that the commercial substance, intellectual property ownership, and profit attribution align with the actual performance of significant people functions (i.e., product development, sales, customer support, and management) in Quebec; the CRA's transfer pricing rules under section 247 and the OECD's profit attribution principles in the Authorised OECD Approach (AOA) apply to attribute profits to a permanent establishment, requiring capital and risk allocation consistent with decision-making; a purely passive limited partnership holding IP in Quebec with its developers and managers located elsewhere or in a low-tax jurisdiction will be challenged as a sham or as a conduit, with the result that profits are reattributed to the jurisdiction of value creation; there is no regime of formal 'ESR filings' in Canada, but the CRA's Form T106 and Schedule 91 disclose economic substance indicators, and failure to maintain substance triggers GAAR reassessment, 10% transfer pricing penalty under subsection 247(3), gross negligence penalties under subsection 163(2), and possible criminal liability under subsection 239(1).

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Under subsection 239(1) of the Income Tax Act and section 1027 of the Quebec Taxation Act, tax evasion is an indictable offence subject to imprisonment for up to 5 years, fines of up to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, or both; the CRA may impose a civil penalty under subsection 163(2) equal to 50% of the understated tax (gross negligence penalty), plus interest and arrears; Quebec imposes an equivalent gross negligence penalty of 50% on the federal penalty plus a Quebec-specific penalty of 50%; the court may order restitution, forfeiture, and costs; repeated evasion triggers escalated fines not exceeding 200% of the tax evaded under both federal and provincial statutes.

Director personal liability: Directors of the Canadian corporate general partner may have their personal liability pierced under subsection 227.1(1) of the Income Tax Act and section 325 of the Quebec Taxation Act for failure to withhold or remit Part XIII withholding tax, employee source deductions, or GST/HST/QST amounts; directors may be personally liable for divided penalties and the Crown's right of recovery under section 227.1 may be enforced even if the corporation is dissolved; where tax evasion is committed with fraudulent intent, the court may impose a travel ban, a criminal conviction creating a criminal record affecting US ESTA and Canadian immigration status, seizure of personal assets via a civil fraud judgment, and imprisonment of up to 5 years; the CRA's Voluntary Disclosure Program may shield taxpayers only before a formal investigation or compliance enforcement notice is issued.
Transfer Pricing Penalty and Thin Capitalization

Statutory sanction: Under subsection 247(3) of the Income Tax Act, a penalty of 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment, with a minimum penalty of CAD 5,000 for failure to make or to file complete and accurate T106 information, and CAD 2,500 additional penalties for repeated failure; in addition, the denial of interest deductions on excessive debt under thin capitalization rules (subsection 18(4) and section 212(1)(b)) subjects the excessive amounts to a 25% withholding tax on deemed interest, and the CRA may impose a gross negligence penalty of 50% of the reassessed tax under subsection 163(2); Revenu Québec applies equivalent penalties and adjustments under sections 441.34 and 1018.1 of the Quebec Taxation Act, including a 10% penalty on the Quebec transfer pricing adjustment and a 50% penalty for false statements.

Director personal liability: Directors of the general partner may be personally liable under section 247(5) of the Income Tax Act where the penalty arises from the directors' direction or consent, or where the corporation fails to withhold Part XIII tax on the excess debt interest; the doctrine of piercing the corporate veil applies under Quebec civil law (Civil Code of Quebec, articles 317 and 316) where the corporation is a mere agent or alter ego, making the directors personally responsible for the mischaracterized payments; the CRA may issue a director liability assessment under subsection 227.1(2) requiring immediate payment of unremitted withholding tax, interest, and penalties; personal liability can extend to the non-resident partner if it directs the management of the partnership and is de facto controlling the general partner, and can also be enforced through a Fraude de la loi action under civil law, leading to receivership over personal assets and prohibition on leaving Canada.
Shell Company / Sham and GAAR Abuse

Statutory sanction: The CRA will invoke the general anti-avoidance rule (section 245) to deny the tax benefit and recharacterize the transaction, and may impose a penalty of 50% of the tax avoided under subsection 163(2) if the misrepresentation is attributable to culpable conduct; Revenu Québec invokes its equivalent GAAR (section 1074.1 of the Taxation Act) to deny the benefit and imposes penalties up to 50% of the Quebec tax avoided; a shell company may be refused treaty benefits under the principal purpose test (PPT) in the Canada-US multilateral instrument or the LOB clause in the Canada-US treaty; tax consequences include complete reassessment of all years within the normal reassessment period (three years, unaffected by fraud) and criminal sanctions under subsection 239(1) for misleading statements.

Director personal liability: Under Quebec civil law, the corporate veil is pierced under articles 316–317 of the Civil Code of Quebec when the corporation or partnership is used to commit fraud, abuse of rights, or contravention of public order; directors are personally liable for the full amount of taxes, penalties, and interest, as well as for damages to the Crown; travel restrictions under s. 19.1 of the Tax Court of Canada Act may apply while an appeal is pending, and an officer with a conviction for tax evasion faces a travel bar to Canada and the US (under the US Immigration and Nationality Act section 212(a)(2)(A)(i)(I) for crimes involving moral turpitude); officers and directors may also be jointly and severally liable under the OECD's new tax transparency rulings, and bank secrecy treatment does not protect the personal tax identity.
Anti-Money Laundering (AML) and Proceeds of Crime

Statutory sanction: Under the PCMLTFA, failure to comply with registration, record-keeping, and reporting obligations can result in administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 500,000 for individuals and CAD 1,000,000 for entities per violation, and criminal penalties under the Criminal Code for laundering proceeds of crime carry imprisonment up to 10 years; Quebec's Act respecting the prevention of money laundering and terrorist financing may impose fines and suspension of licences; if the payment rails are used for a prohibited predicate offense, the funds are subject to forfeiture; the Canada Revenue Agency can also impose a 50% civil penalty on the unreported income under subsection 163(2), and bank account freezing orders under the Criminal Code section 462.33.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers can be charged as parties to the offence under the Criminal Code for knowingly directing, authorizing, or acquiescing to the laundering or false reporting; the corporate veil is pierced under articles 316–317 of the Civil Code of Quebec, exposing personal assets; they are subject to imprisonment of up to 10 years for money laundering, 5 years for false accounting; they may face travel bans and civil asset forfeiture; FINTRAC compliance orders can be issued against individuals as designated officers without requiring a separate corporate shell; banking relationships are terminated, and directors become personally liable to banks for losses caused by the breach.
