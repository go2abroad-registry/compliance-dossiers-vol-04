Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – Business Registration Online (BRO) portal at canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/register-your-business.html

E-government filing portal

A foreign-incorporated entity carrying on business in Canada must obtain a CRA Business Number (BN) by applying online through Business Registration Online, by mail using Form RC1, or by telephone; the BN programme issues a 9-digit account number and can be combined with an RT account for GST/HST if required. For a Canadian federal or provincial business corporation, incorporation under the Canada Business Corporations Act or Ontario Business Corporations Act is a prerequisite; the CRA then recognizes the same BN for corporate income tax, GST/HST, payroll, and import/export accounts. A non-resident corporation without permanent establishment must nonetheless register for GST/HST if it makes taxable supplies in Canada unless the small-supplier threshold applies.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a check-the-box classification regime analogous to the United States; corporations are categorically treated as separate taxable entities under the Income Tax Act. The principal available statutory election is the joint election under subsection 256(2) for associated corporations to allocate the federal small business deduction, and the section 216 election for non-resident corporations to make an alternate tax return for rental or timber income. For indirect tax purposes, a person may elect under section 240 of the Excise Tax Act for quarterly filing or to use a fiscal year different from the taxation year; a closely related corporation may also make a section 156 election for nil consideration between itself and another GST/HST registrant. Hybrid mismatch elections under Canadian law are not available in the style of OECD Model Rules; rather, the Income Tax Act addresses hybrid arrangements through specific avoidance rules in sections 18.2 and 18.3 without elective treatment.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual worldwide taxable supplies from commercial activity carried on in Canada, excluding zero-rated supplies and casual supplies; once exceeded, mandatory registration as of the beginning of the quarter in which the threshold is surpassed or the day the determination is made, except for certain non-resident recipients of zero-rated supplies. A person below the threshold may voluntarily register, which permits input tax credits but also imposes output tax obligations.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of tangible personal property exported by the vendor or by a non-resident recipient whose exported property is subsequently supplied outside Canada are zero-rated under section 23 and section 42 of the Excise Tax Act, provided the property is exported as soon after the supply as reasonable, and satisfactory evidence of export is retained. Services of virtual office and mail scanning provided to non-resident clients are eligible for zero-rating as exported services if delivered to recipients outside Canada, with no link to real or immovable property situated in Canada, the recipient is not a Canadian resident, and the service is performed outside Canada in subsection 7(1)(a) of Part V of Schedule IX of the Excise Tax Act; the supplier must maintain evidence that the recipient is non-resident and that the service was performed outside Canada. On-site services in Canada to non-resident clients are not zero-rated; however, mail scanning and virtual office services performed wholly from Canada for non-resident clients can still be zero-rated as services rendered to a non-resident where the services are not of a type ordinarily performed for a person resident in Canada and the services are not incidental to a taxable supply of property made in Canada. Zéro-rating does not apply to advertising, data processing, or services that are directly connected to real property in Canada; CMRA operators must carefully segregate any physical mail forwarding to Canadian addresses from digital scanning services.

No real-time fiscal clearance, API stamping, or mandatory digital e-invoice clearance regime exists in Canada at the federal or Ontario provincial level. GST/HST registrants must issue a prescribed invoice containing the supplier's name, BN, date, and consideration, but electronic invoices are permitted without CRA approval. The CRA does operate the GST/HST NETFILE service for returns and the Custom GST/HST Return for registrants with foreign operations; however, this is not a continuous transaction control or coding mandate. Ontario does not require electronic invoicing or digital stamping for the provincial HST component.

CRA auditors may reject zero-rating claims where contemporaneous evidence of shipment or foreign recipient status is deficient; if zero-rating is found to be improperly claimed, the CRA will reassess the output tax at 13% HST plus statutory late-filing penalties of 5% plus 1% per complete month up to 12 months, and daily 0.139% interest on unpaid amounts. Raising, issuing, participating in, or assenting to a false GST/HST return giving rise to a rebate or credit claim can attract a gross negligence penalty of 50% of the overstated rebate or credit under subsection 285(1) of the Excise Tax Act, and completed fraudulent export transactions may be prosecuted as tax evasion with fines up to 200% of the amount evaded and imprisonment under section 327 of the Excise Tax Act. A zero-rated claim will also be clawed back retroactively if the foreign exchange proceeds are not brought back into Canada within the statutory period, unless acceptable documentary proof establishes an earlier effective repatriation date; for GST/HST purposes the CRA generally applies a 180-day deadline linked to the original statement date of account, but service providers should negotiate a 360-day foreign exchange repatriation clause in their client contracts to align with banking realities while maintaining a 180-day internal documentary cutoff. CRA may apply the doctrine of unjust enrichment to recover rebates and input tax credits incorrectly claimed in conjunction with exported supplies, and may waive penalties only where the taxpayer demonstrates due diligence based on J.K. Read v. The Queen.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible for both current-account and capital-account transactions. Canada has accepted Article VIII obligations under the IMF Articles of Agreement and imposes no restrictions on payments or transfers for current international transactions. Dividends, interest, management fees, loan principal repayments, and repatriation of subscribed capital by non-resident shareholders may be remitted without Bank of Canada or government approval. The only structural hurdles are tax-based: Part XIII withholding tax on passive payments to non-residents (default 25% on dividends, treaty-reduced to 5%–15% depending on treaty, shareholding threshold, and holding period) and CRA compliance on foreign-currency gains. For a Canadian business corporation in Ontario, there is no profit-remittance approval barrier; the practical burden is bank-level enhanced due diligence given the Medium-Risk (Enhanced) CMRA classification.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There are no statutory export-proceeds surrender rules, mandatory conversion ratios, or repatriation timelines in Canada. Exporters and cross-border service providers (including virtual-office and mail-scanning operators) may retain foreign-currency receivables in permitted foreign-currency accounts at Canadian chartered banks or convert them at arm's-length spot, forward, or swap rates through the bank's dealing desk. Foreign-currency gains and losses are recognized for income-tax purposes and must be translated into Canadian dollars for CRA reporting under the Income Tax Act and related administrative guidance. No FINTRAC report is required purely for maintaining or converting export proceeds, but a Suspicious Transaction Report, Large Virtual Currency Transaction Report, or Large Cash Transaction Report applies where statutory thresholds or prescribed indicators are triggered.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) is the monetary authority and financial agent of the Government of Canada for foreign exchange operations; prudential oversight of federally regulated deposit-taking institutions and clearing and settlement systems rests with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); AML/CFT supervision of financial institutions, including money services businesses and foreign payment service providers, rests with FINTRAC. Canada maintains no separate foreign-exchange control administration, exchange-control law, or central-bank licence requirement for ordinary commercial fund transfers.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium as a secondary settlement and FX conversion rail; Low as a primary treasury or deposit account. Foreign digital EMIs (e.g., Wise, Airwallex, Payoneer, Mercury) are not Schedule I or Schedule II banks under the Bank Act and cannot accept regulated Canadian demand deposits or provide CDIC deposit insurance. They may lawfully operate as FINTRAC-registered Money Services Businesses (MSB or Foreign MSB) and can access the Canadian payment ecosystem only through settlement accounts at domestic chartered banks. Legal constraints: (i) mandatory FINTRAC MSB registration before transacting; (ii) no direct Payments Canada clearing membership; (iii) no CDIC insurance; (iv) PIPEDA and Ontario privacy obligations; (v) platform-specific enhanced due diligence that frequently restricts or declines CMRA/virtual-office/mail-forwarding verticals. The correct structure is a primary Tier-1 Canadian bank account for settlement and capital flows, with EMIs limited to small-value disbursements or cost-effective FX conversion.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

No exchange-control approval is required in Canada. Recommended compliant protocol: the non-resident shareholder's bank originates a SWIFT MT103 (or ISO 20022 pacs.008) to the corporation's CAD account at a Canadian Tier-1 chartered bank, with beneficiary name matching the corporate registry, correct bank identifier and account number, and a payment-purpose memo specifying 'Equity capital contribution' or 'Shareholder loan - arm's length'. Canadian banks apply know-your-client, beneficial-ownership, and purpose-of-transfer checks on all inbound wires, with enhanced due diligence for amounts above CAD 10,000; the bank may require a source-of-funds questionnaire, executed share-subscription agreement, and board resolution. There is no FINTRAC large-value wire-reporting requirement per se, but a Suspicious Transaction Report is obligatory where red flags remain. For Stripe-recurring inflows, funds settle through the Stripe treasury network into the CAD operating account under merchant-category-code-aligned descriptors; bank-side conversion of foreign-currency settlements should be executed at the bank's transparent spot rate. Every inbound wire should be tagged with a unique reconciliation ID traceable to the capital ledger.Domestic CAD disbursements clear through Payments Canada rails: the Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) for time-critical payments above approximately CAD 50,000, the Automated Clearing Settlement System (ACSS) for small-value and next-day items, and the Real-Time Rail (RTR) for immediate low-value transfers. Cross-border disbursements use SWIFT MT103 or ISO 20022 pacs.008 wires in CAD, USD, EUR, or GBP with UETR reference numbers for suppliers, contractors, and intercompany obligations. For higher-value or documentary trade, irrevocable commercial letters of credit under UCP600 with a confirming bank are permissible for procurement of software, equipment, and services; bank demand guarantees under URDG 758 may underpin contractual performance. Dividend or surplus repatriation to non-resident shareholders must occur only after Part XIII withholding tax (default 25%, treaty-reduced to 5%–15% depending on jurisdiction and shareholding) is remitted to the CRA and Forms NR4 or NR601 are completed. No Ministry of Finance, Bank of Canada, or OSFI approval is required for any outbound payment.Deliverable outright FX forward contracts in CAD/USD and CAD/EUR with tenors of 1 to 24 months under an ISDA 2002 Master Agreement and Credit Support Annex with a Schedule I Canadian bank.Plain-vanilla European-style OTC FX options enabling the corporation to lock a worst-case rate while retaining upside for USD/CAD and EUR/CAD exposures.Cross-currency swaps (CCS) and cross-currency interest-rate swaps for long-term intercompany financing, capital-account repatriation, or matched fixed/floating cash-flow management.Rolling spot and FX swap facilities at the bank's dealing desk for short-term working-capital liquidity and settlement-date mismatches.Cash-settled non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) are generally unnecessary and not commonly offered for CAD because the Canadian dollar is fully deliverable in an active onshore market; Tier-1 banks quote deliverable forwards instead, and NDFs should be used only where a counterparty cannot access the onshore CAD market.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium as a secondary settlement and FX conversion rail; Low as a primary treasury or deposit account. Foreign digital EMIs (e.g., Wise, Airwallex, Payoneer, Mercury) are not Schedule I or Schedule II banks under the Bank Act and cannot accept regulated Canadian demand deposits or provide CDIC deposit insurance. They may lawfully operate as FINTRAC-registered Money Services Businesses (MSB or Foreign MSB) and can access the Canadian payment ecosystem only through settlement accounts at domestic chartered banks. Legal constraints: (i) mandatory FINTRAC MSB registration before transacting; (ii) no direct Payments Canada clearing membership; (iii) no CDIC insurance; (iv) PIPEDA and Ontario privacy obligations; (v) platform-specific enhanced due diligence that frequently restricts or declines CMRA/virtual-office/mail-forwarding verticals. The correct structure is a primary Tier-1 Canadian bank account for settlement and capital flows, with EMIs limited to small-value disbursements or cost-effective FX conversion.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined: 15% federal general corporate rate + 11.5% Ontario general corporate rate

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on taxable dividends paid or deemed paid to non-residents of Canada, unless reduced by an applicable tax treaty.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$5,000,000 aggregate fair market value of all controlled cross-border transactions between the Canadian taxpayer and non-arm's length non-residents is the administrative triggering threshold for contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation under CRA Information Circular 87-2R and Form T106 reporting. For members of a multinational enterprise group with consolidated group revenue equal to or exceeding C$750,000,000, a Country-by-Country Report (Form RC5188) is mandatory.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations code, but the Canada Revenue Agency vigorously applies the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (Income Tax Act section 245) and common-law sham and substance-over-form doctrines. A virtual office and mail scanning operation that lacks real personnel, operational decision-making, bank account management, and contractual substance may have its intercompany arrangements recharacterized, its tax benefits denied, and its treaty access cancelled if the arrangement is found to have no bona fide economic substance.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False or Misleading Returns

Statutory sanction: Criminal tax evasion under Income Tax Act subsection 239(1): on indictment, a fine of not less than 100% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment up to five years; on summary conviction, a fine of the same range and imprisonment up to two years less a day. Civilly, the CRA will reassess amounts without regard to the normal reassessment period, charge interest, and apply a 50% gross negligence penalty under subsection 163(2). Late filing penalties apply independently.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly participate in, assent to, or acquiesce in an act of evasion may be jointly and severally liable for the corporation's unremitted withholding and Part XIII taxes under subsection 227.1(1). In fraud-related convictions, directors face personal criminal liability, civil restitution and corporate veil-piercing, and travel restrictions could be imposed through bail conditions or agency action if material flight risk exists.
Shell Company / Conduit Abuse and Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: Under the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (section 245), the CRA may deny any tax benefit, reattribute income to the true economic owner, impose interest and penalties. A 50% penalty for gross negligence can apply to any resulting understatement. Ontario corporate law provides for reinstatement or dissolution of corporations that do not maintain real registered activities, and under the Canada Business Corporations Act failure to file or update the beneficial ownership register is an offence punishable by OSC and Corporations Canada fines of up to C$1,000,000 or imprisonment for individuals.

Director personal liability: Where the corporate form is a sham or facade, courts will pierce the corporate veil and hold directors personally responsible for tax liabilities and damages resulting from fraudulent conveyances. Directors may also be criminally charged for scheme to defraud, which can lead to restitution orders, homeland border seizure notices, bail-related travel bans, and indeterminate passport withdrawal pending criminal proceedings.
Anti-Money Laundering and Proceeds of Crime Violations

Statutory sanction: Under the Criminal Code subsection 462.31(1), laundering the proceeds of crime is an indictable offence punishable by imprisonment up to 14 years. Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, failure to register, report, or maintain compliance controls can result in administrative monetary penalties, with maximum penalties for non-compliance varying by class; a court can impose fines up to C$500,000 per violation for corporations. Asset freezing and forfeiture may also be ordered.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly participate in, benefit from, or are grossly negligent regarding laundering operations face personal exposure under the Criminal Code; corporate veil-piercing allows confiscation of personal assets and civil recovery. Convictions may trigger severe travel restrictions, and a jail term up to 14 years directly curtails liberty. FINTRAC and police enforcement may issue judicial orders freezing bank accounts and imposing financial restitution obligations on the entire control group.
