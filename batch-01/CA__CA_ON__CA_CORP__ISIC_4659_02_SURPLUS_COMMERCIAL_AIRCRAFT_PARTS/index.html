Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — Business Registration Online (BRO) portal (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/registering-your-business.html); Ontario participates in the federal GST/HST administration, so no separate provincial HST authority applies.

E-government filing portal

Foreign corporations must first complete federal incorporation or extra-provincial registration in Ontario, obtain a Business Number (BN) from CRA through Business Registration Online, and register for the GST/HST account (RT), import/export account (RM), payroll account (RP) if employees, and any Ontario-specific employer accounts (e.g., Employer Health Tax) through the Ontario Ministry of Finance. Non-resident corporations may voluntarily register for GST/HST under Excise Tax Act s. 240(1), even without carrying on business in Canada, and must submit charter documents, director details, registered office address, and, where required, a Canadian representative for service. The CRA assigns a nine-digit BN with program account numbers for each tax obligation.

It is a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) only if controlled by Canadian residents; otherwise it is a non-resident-controlled corporation (NRCC) with a restricted small business deduction limit of CAD 50,000 per year. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — the Canadian Business Corporation is a separate taxpayer under the Income Tax Act. It is a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) only if controlled by Canadian residents; otherwise it is a non-resident-controlled corporation (NRCC) with a restricted small business deduction limit of CAD 50,000 per year. There is no default flow-through treatment for corporations.

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a check-the-box-style election for entity classification; classification follows incorporation and central management and control (CMC). Relevant elections and statutory mechanisms include: (1) GST/HST s. 156 election for closely related corporations to treat intercompany supplies as nil; (2) ETA s. 240(1) voluntary GST/HST registration for non-residents; (3) Income Tax Act s. 216(1) election for non-resident rental/leasing income (not applicable to aircraft parts trading); (4) hybrid mismatch deduction denial rules under ITA s. 18.2 and s. 18.3; (5) treaty-based withholding tax relief applications through CRA Form NR301 or similar.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — the small-supplier threshold is exceeded when taxable supply revenue, including zero-rated exports, exceeds CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or across the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters; upon exceeding this threshold, mandatory GST/HST registration applies. Non-residents carrying on commercial activity in Canada may be required to register irrespective of the CAD 30,000 threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies under section 1 of Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act: a supply of tangible personal property, including certified civil aircraft rotables and avionics surplus, is zero-rated when delivered to a non-resident purchaser, possession transfers in Canada, and the goods are removed from Canada as soon as is reasonable and within the statutory period, unless a CRA ruling extends the timeline. The supplier must retain evidence of export in accordance with the Export and Import of Goods Regulations. Logistical and ancillary services supplied to a non-resident in respect of exported goods may also qualify as zero-rated. Zero-rated exports allow the registrant to claim input tax credits for domestic inputs (parts acquisition, warehousing, logistics, brokerage) and obtain a GST/HST refund for the resulting credit balance.

  • Customs Export Declaration — CBSA electronic export report or Canada Customs Coding Form B13A where required, including the export reference number.
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill evidencing the physical removal of the aircraft parts from Canada to the foreign destination of the non-resident buyer.
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or equivalent verifiable bank payment evidence (SWIFT MT103, bank credit advice, or commercial invoice settlement record) confirming receipt of export proceeds by the Canadian supplier.

Canada does not require real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or mandatory fiscal invoice APIs. CRA requires electronic filing of GST/HST returns through GST/HST NETFILE or GST/HST Internet File Transfer, and all registrants must maintain electronic records in an approved format. For exports from Ontario, the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) requires electronic export reporting through the Canadian Export Reporting System (ERS), with an export reference number; no additional VAT clearance document is imposed.

The CRA may reassess zero-rated supplies when documentary proof of export is insufficient, clawing back input tax credits and assessing GST/HST. Penalties include: ETA s. 280(1) failure-to-comply penalties up to CAD 500; s. 285(1) false-statement penalties equal to 25% of tax evaded or attempted to be evaded; gross negligence penalties up to 50% of the tax under s. 285(1). The standard reassessment period is four years, but is unlimited where misrepresentation, fraud, or wilful default is present. Criminal prosecution for wilful evasion is available under the Excise Tax Act and the Income Tax Act. Export documentation must be retained for six years. Absence of proof of export or failure to repatriate proceeds within 180 days may result in deemed consideration adjustments, transfer-pricing penalties under s. 247, and interest charges.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada is a fully convertible, open capital-account jurisdiction and an IMF Article VIII member. Both current-account and capital-account transactions are unrestricted without prior exchange-control approval, including foreign direct investment, portfolio flows, trade payments, loan repayments, and profit repatriation. Practical hurdles are limited to non-exchange-control frictions: (i) Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (Canada) imposes a 25% statutory dividend withholding tax, reduced to 15% under the Canada-U.S. tax treaty for eligible U.S. beneficial owners and to 5% for qualified U.S. corporate shareholders owning at least 10% of voting stock, subject to treaty certification; (ii) the Investment Canada Act requires a filing or, above the C$2.256 billion WTO-specific review threshold for 2025 (business acquisition by a WTO investor), a net-benefit review; the surplus-commercial-aircraft-parts distributor falls below the threshold and is not a cultural business; and (iii) financial institutions may levy their own FX-spread and KYC due-diligence conditions. There is no statutory restriction on remitting profits, dividends, royalties, or principal on shareholder loans, provided the underlying transactions are arm's-length and properly documented for transfer-pricing purposes under Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) administrative guidance.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no export-proceeds surrender obligation and no statutorily mandated conversion ratio. Exporters may retain and deploy foreign currency freely, including in U.S. dollar operating accounts, and may hedge forward without central-bank consent. Reporting obligations are limited to: (i) CBSA customs declarations under the Customs Act and the U.S.-Canada cross-border data exchange for exportations; (ii) mandatory FINTRAC large-value reporting (C$10,000 or its foreign equivalent) for all cross-border wire transfers and physical currency movements through financial intermediaries; and (iii) Statistics Canada / CUSMA-related data collection on selected trade transactions. The aviation-parts trading entity should nevertheless document all export proceeds in a U.S.-dollar or CAD treasury ledger with source-receipt reconciliation to satisfy CRA income-attribution requirements and to support an FX-forward hedge programme.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); monetary authority and lender of last resort responsible for CAD operations and FX market intervention. Canada maintains no dedicated foreign-exchange control administration comparable to SAFE or CBI; oversight is exercised jointly by the Department of Finance Canada and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for chartered banks, with FINTRAC enforcing the cross-border transaction reporting and AML/CFT framework under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). The Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) administers cross-border currency reporting for physical movement of funds.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: MEDIUM-LOW for offshore digital EMIs (Wise, Revolut Business, Airwallex, Mercury, etc.) as primary treasury infrastructure. Legal constraints: (i) any foreign money-services business or EMI that transfers funds to or from a Canadian client must be registered with FINTRAC as a money services business (MSB) under the PCMLTFA, or the Canadian client risks a compliance defect in its own AML programme; (ii) the EMI must not be a shell and must maintain its own FINTRAC-required Canadian agent-records and transaction-monitoring arrangements; (iii) Canadian dollar clearing through the Automated Clearing Settlement System (ACSS) is restricted to participants of Payments Canada, so an offshore EMI cannot independently acquire on-us settlement without a sponsorship arrangement with a Schedule I bank; (iv) Canadian Schedule I banks are increasingly de-risking relationships with unregulated or low-capit alised offshore EMIs, particularly for an aviation-parts distributor that itself sits in a Medium-Risk Enhanced AML tier. Recommended configuration: use a Canadian Schedule I bank as the primary settlement spine, and retain a FINTRAC-registered EMI as a secondary payment conduit for low-value operational disbursements only, never for escrowed aviation-trade proceeds or IPO-stage capital injection flows.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined general corporate rate (federal 15% net rate after abatement and general rate reduction; Ontario 11.5% provincial rate). A Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) benefits from a 12.2% combined rate on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income (federal 9% small business deduction rate plus Ontario 3.2% small business rate).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax under ITA s.212(2) levied on the gross amount of all dividend distributions paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return must be filed no later than 6 months after the end of the taxation year (e.g., a December 31 year-end is due by June 30 of the following year). The balance of tax payable is due 2 months after the taxation year-end for all corporations except a CCPC claiming the federal small business deduction with taxable income up to the business limit, for which the balance is due 3 months after year-end; instalment pre-payments apply during the year.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File (contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation) is mandatory when the aggregate gross fair market value of transactions with non-arm's length non-residents exceeds CAD 1,000,000 in a taxation year. Master File is required for MNE groups with annual consolidated revenue of at least CAD 500,000,000 and that also have cross-border related-party transactions in excess of the prescribed threshold for the fiscal year; Country-by-Country reporting is required for MNE groups with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750,000,000 (or the Canadian regulatory equivalent).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not facilitate stand-alone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) as applied in zero- or low-tax hubs; rather, economic substance is enforced through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA s.245), the Multi-Lateral Instrument's principal purpose test, and the permanent establishment threshold in the OECD Model. Circular structures interposed solely to shift profits from surplus commercial aircraft parts distribution will be recharacterized, treaty benefits denied, and the Canadian branch tax under Part XIV (25%) may apply to unrepatriated earnings.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Willful Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: ITA s.239(1): upon conviction, a fine of between 50% and 200% of the amount of tax sought to be evaded, and imprisonment for up to 5 years; civil gross negligence penalty under ITA s.163(2) of 50% of the understated tax; CRA publicly lists the corporation in the name-and-shame program; forfeiture of the aircraft parts and related proceeds is available.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorize, participate in, or sign fraudulent returns are jointly and severally liable under ITA s.227.1 for unremitted withholdings; ITA s.160 imposes joint liability for transfers at less than fair market value to non-arm's length persons; Criminal Code s.380 (fraud over CAD 5,000) carries up to 14 years' imprisonment; travel bans and passport surrender are judicially imposed in international-evasion indictments.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: ITA s.247(2): CRA restates the transaction to the arm's length result and applies deemed interest adjustments; s.247(3)(a) penalty of 10% of the net adjustment where documentation is absent; s.247(3)(b) penalty of 10% of the unrecorded transaction amount; prescribed-rate interest compounds daily on the re-assessed tax.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign incorrect transfer-pricing certificates with knowledge of the inaccuracy are personally assessable under the certificate-liability rule; gross negligence penalties can render the corporation insolvent, and CRA may enforce against directors and controlling shareholders under ITA s.160 for undervalue asset transfers.
Shell Company / Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: GAAR under ITA s.245(2) disallows the tax benefit and recharacterizes diverted amounts as deemed dividends or royalties subject to 25% Part XIII withholding; the MLI principal purpose test operates to deny treaty benefits; Part XIV branch tax at 25% is applied to unrepatriated Canadian earnings; CRA may impose dual withdrawal of losses, deemed-interest, and reassessment penalties.

Director personal liability: Unwinding the shell structure exposes directors to ITA s.160 joint liability for under-valued intercompany transfers; surplus-stripping rules (ITA s.84(2), s.246(1)) can produce personal shareholder assessments; a director who is the beneficial controller may be personally assessed for all taxes avoided plus penalties, and the CRA/border-services record may trigger immigration refusal or port-entry denial.
AML / Sanctions Violation (Aviation Spares Export)

Statutory sanction: PCMLTFA administrative monetary penalties of up to CAD 1,000,000 per violation for a corporation and corresponding penalties for individuals; Criminal Code s.462.31 money laundering (indictable) carries up to 10 years' imprisonment and forfeiture of the proceeds of crime; RCMP/CRA coordinated seizure of aircraft parts and trading assets; AML compliance program failures attract mandatory FINTRAC audit and compliance orders.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly direct laundering, sanctions-busting, or false reporting are personally criminally liable with imprisonment potential; travel bans are imposed as judicial bail/parole conditions with required passport surrender; civil forfeiture reaches the director's direct appreciation of the smuggling proceeds; if the corporation is struck for failure to maintain compliance, directors remain personally liable for unpaid corporate obligations and statutory defaults under the Ontario Business Corporations Act.
