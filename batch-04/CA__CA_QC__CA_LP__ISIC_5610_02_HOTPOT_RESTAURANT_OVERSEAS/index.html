Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible: For Canadian federal income tax and Quebec income tax, the limited partnership is fiscally transparent under section 96 of the Income Tax Act, with each partner reporting its share of partnership income or loss on its own return and no entity-level income tax being imposed on the LP. However, for GST/HST and QST purposes, the partnership is itself a distinct person that must register, collect, and remit; it is not eligible for treatment as a tax-exempt zone entity. Territorial scope: a Canadian-resident partner is taxed on worldwide income, whereas a non-resident partner is taxed only on Canadian-source and Quebec-source income, subject to Part XIII withholding rules on certain payments from the partnership.

Available election forms

None equivalent to a US check-the-box election exists in Canada; entity classification follows the common law and statutory characterization, and the LP remains transparent for income tax while being a separate person for GST/HST and QST. Available elections relevant to this structure include: (i) a partnership fiscal-period election under section 249.1 of the Income Tax Act permitting a non-calendar year-end where all partners elect and conditions are met; (ii) an elective rollover of property transferred to the partnership under subsection 97(2) of the Income Tax Act; (iii) a partnership capital cost allowance election under subsection 96(2.1) of the Income Tax Act; (iv) the GST/HST Quick Method election under section 220 of the Excise Tax Act and its QST counterpart, allowing a restaurant operator to remit using a reduced percentage of taxable sales; and (v) voluntary GST/HST and QST registration by a small supplier with turnover below CAD 30,000. Canada's hybrid-mismatch measures provide no protective entity-classification election for a non-resident limited partnership; every cross-border payment must be tested on its facts against the transfer-pricing rules of section 247 and the general anti-avoidance rule of section 245 of the Income Tax Act.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

A person whose worldwide tax-inclusive revenues from taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over the four preceding consecutive calendar quarters ceases to be a small supplier and must register for GST/HST; the same CAD 30,000 threshold applies for QST registration. For this hotpot restaurant operation in Quebec, registration is mandatory immediately upon commencement of business because the direct-operated dining premises constitute a permanent establishment, billing through POS merchant acquiring generates taxable supplies in Quebec from the first day, and projected annual turnover will far exceed the small-supplier threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Export zero-rating applies to the condiment-supply component only where the Quebec limited partnership supplies packaged sauces and food condiments for export and the goods are shipped directly by the supplier out of Canada, with title passing to the non-resident purchaser outside Canada, or where the goods are delivered to a common carrier for export at the point of exit; the governing authority is Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act, in particular section 5 for exported goods, and the parallel QST zero-rating rules. Services that are reasonably regarded as ancillary to the exported goods, such as handling, storage, and transport performed in Canada for the exported property, are also zero-rated. Dine-in hotpot meal sales and sales of prepared or heated meals for take-away are standard-rated supplies in Quebec at the combined 14.975% rate; the take-away exemption for basic groceries is unavailable where the food is supplied heated, pre-cooked, or packaged as a meal ready for consumption. To substantiate the zero-rating of exported condiments, the LP must obtain from the non-resident customer a written purchase order, shipping instructions, transport documentation, and evidence of foreign-currency settlement; the LP must also hold a CBSA export declaration confirming the actual departure from Canada.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A, filed electronically with the Canada Border Services Agency through the Canadian Export Reporting System or the Canadian Integrated Export Declaration portal for shipments over CAD 2,000 or of controlled goods)
  • Commercial Invoice stating complete HTS classification, country of origin, unit prices, and total export value
  • Bill of Lading or airway waybill showing carrier name, voyage or flight number, port of exit, and foreign destination
  • CBSA approved export-report confirmation number evidencing the electronic export declaration
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank SWIFT/MT103 credit evidence linking foreign-currency settlement to the exporting LP's bank account
  • Packing list and container or shipment tracking records showing the goods physically left Canada

Canada and Quebec impose no real-time fiscal clearance, no fiscal memory or cryptographic stamping requirement, no continuous-control SAF-T mandate, and no government-API invoice certification scheme. Electronic invoicing is permitted and interoperable through the Peppol network on a voluntary basis; mandatory electronic filing applies once annual taxable turnover exceeds CAD 1.5 million, requiring GST/HST returns via GST/HST NETFILE or an approved EDI channel and, for QST, requiring returns transmitted electronically through Revenu Québec's Transmission électronique service. A restaurant's POS system need not be government certified; however, each invoice or simplified point-of-sale receipt issued in Quebec must show the registrant name, the nine-digit BN, the GST rate and amount, and the QST rate and amount, and records must be retained for six years. Revenu Québec may cross-check reported sales data against payment-card and merchant-acquirer aggregate data, and it has expressly flagged the restaurant sector for electronic-receipt verification.

The Excise Tax Act imposes a false-statement or omission penalty of not less than CAD 250 and 25% of the denied refund or of the excess of reported tax, and Revenu Québec applies equivalent penalties under the Act respecting the Quebec sales tax for false declarations and omissions. CRA may reassess GST/HST net tax and any rebate within four years, without time limit in the case of misrepresentation, fraud, or failure to file, and Revenu Québec mirrors this reassessment power for QST. Interest on arrears accrues daily and the penalty rate is materially higher for repeat or deliberate non-compliance. For deliberate evasion, fines reach 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, with terms of imprisonment available on conviction. Export zero-rating claims are subjected to intensive audit scrutiny through CBSA export-data matching, carrier shipping logs, warehouse records, and foreign-bank remittance tracing; although the ETA does not codify a 180-day repatriation rule, CRA administrative practice expects export proceeds to be received within the 180-day benchmark, and failure to demonstrate timely repatriation, absent documented commercial reasons, triggers transfer-pricing review under section 247 of the Income Tax Act, deemed-receipt recharacterization, clawback of all input tax credits previously recovered on the zero-rated supply chain, denial of future zero-rating, and cumulative penalties and interest.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible on both current and capital accounts without licensing, quotas, prior central-bank approval, or end-use restrictions. Profit distributions from a Quebec limited partnership (Civil Code of Quebec arts. 2236-2255) to resident and non-resident partners are unrestricted; the only frictions are Income Tax Act Part XIII withholding for non-resident partners (15%-25% treaty-dependent) and CRS/FATCA self-certification obligations, not exchange-control clearance. The binding convertibility constraint for the overseas direct-operated hotpot chain lies in the host jurisdiction (e.g., onshore RMB capital controls in China or other emerging-market restrictions), not in Canada. To smooth repatriation and bank source-of-funds validation, the LP should maintain audited financial statements, CRA and Revenu Quebec tax-clearance certificates, and transfer-pricing documentation for the condiment supply line.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no repatriation timeline on export proceeds, service income, or intercompany trade receipts. Export earnings from condiment and equipment sales to the overseas restaurant may be retained offshore indefinitely in any convertible currency. The only obligations are (a) arm's-length pricing and contemporaneous documentation under Income Tax Act s. 247 for related-party exports, (b) FINTRAC reporting of electronic fund transfers and large cash transactions at the CAD 10,000 threshold, and (c) CBSA export declarations for goods above the low-value threshold. Treasury design should centralize USD export collections in a Schedule I bank account under the Quebec LP and convert via natural payroll/purchase cycles to minimize FX drag.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), Ottawa; exchange-control policy is functionally distributed between the Department of Finance Canada (currency sovereignty and sanctions), OSFI (prudential supervision of Schedule I/II banks), FINTRAC (cross-border transaction reporting under the PCMLTFA), and the Investment Canada Act directorate (foreign-investment review). Canada operates a free-floating currency regime and has had no dedicated foreign-exchange-control authority since the Exchange Control Act was repealed in 1952.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Low as a primary banking rail; viable only as a supplementary payment layer. Canada does not recognize 'electronic money institution' as a licensed category; foreign EMI licences (FCA/EMD2) confer no passporting rights into Canada, and only chartered banks may take deposits under the Bank Act. A FINTRAC-registered money services business (MSB) or payment fintech (e.g., Wise, Convera, Corpay) may legally provide cross-border payments and multi-currency wallets without deposit-taking, but cannot hold CDIC-insured balances, issue import letters of credit, or provide Payments Canada Lynx membership. Recommended architecture: retain capital escrow and core operating accounts at a Schedule I bank; use a FINTRAC MSB only for low-value trade disbursements and FX conversions below CAD 25,000 per transaction, subject to dual-control approval and monthly reconciliation against the main ledger.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Low as a primary banking rail; viable only as a supplementary payment layer. Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

A Quebec limited partnership is generally a fiscally transparent partnership, not a corporation subject to federal or Quebec corporate income tax on its partnership income. For comparison, the general combined corporate income tax rate for an ordinary corporation carrying on business in Quebec is generally 26.5% (15% federal plus 11.5% Quebec), subject to eligibility, income type, and special rules.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Not applicable to a partnership allocation: an LP does not ordinarily distribute corporate dividends. A payment by a Canadian corporation to a non-resident shareholder is generally subject to 25% Part XIII withholding tax, potentially reduced by an applicable income tax treaty if the recipient qualifies and procedural requirements are met.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general monetary threshold that relieves a taxpayer from the Income Tax Act section 247 contemporaneous-documentation requirement for reportable non-arm's-length cross-border transactions. The principal transfer-pricing penalty threshold generally concerns a net transfer-pricing adjustment exceeding the lesser of CAD 5 million and 10% of the taxpayer's gross revenue, subject to statutory conditions. Canadian law does not impose a universal OECD-style Local File and Master File filing threshold; Country-by-Country reporting generally applies to qualifying multinational groups meeting the CAD 1 billion consolidated-revenue threshold.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a general United Arab Emirates-style Economic Substance Regulations regime. Canadian tax results remain subject to transfer-pricing rules, the general anti-avoidance rule, specific anti-avoidance provisions, beneficial-ownership requirements, and applicable substance-over-form and sham principles. Quebec may apply its own interpretive and anti-avoidance rules.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Failure to file partnership information returns

Statutory sanction: Federal and Quebec late-filing, information-return, and false-statement penalties may apply, together with interest or other statutory consequences. Penalties depend on the specific failure, duration, and applicable provisions.

Director personal liability: An LP has no directors solely by virtue of its partnership form. General partners and responsible persons may face statutory assessment or collection exposure where legislation applies; limited-partner status does not protect a person from liability for that person's own acts or from liabilities arising where the person is legally treated as a general partner.
Non-resident withholding and Canadian-source income

Statutory sanction: The payer or other responsible withholding agent may be assessed for unremitted tax, interest, and applicable penalties. Treaty relief is not available solely on the basis of a partner's non-resident status.

Director personal liability: Where a corporation is the withholding agent, directors may be personally liable for specified unremitted source deductions under statutory director-liability rules, subject to statutory defences and due-diligence requirements. An LP's general partner and responsible persons require a fact-specific liability analysis.
Transfer-pricing non-compliance

Statutory sanction: The CRA may adjust income under section 247, assess consequential tax and interest, and impose a transfer-pricing penalty when statutory conditions and thresholds are met. Reassessment periods may be extended in specified circumstances.

Director personal liability: Transfer-pricing adjustments are generally assessed against the taxpayer. Directors are not automatically personally liable for corporate tax solely because of an adjustment, but separate statutory, fraud, misrepresentation, or unremitted-withholding liabilities may apply.
Tax evasion, false books, or fraud

Statutory sanction: Federal and Quebec tax statutes provide civil penalties, reassessment, interest, and criminal prosecution. Criminal convictions can result in substantial fines and imprisonment; the precise sanction depends on the charge and facts.

Director personal liability: Individuals who authorize, participate in, or counsel an offence may face personal criminal liability. Civil tax liability does not automatically pierce the partnership or corporate veil; statutory liability and the individual's conduct must be assessed separately.
Partnership classification and limited-liability misuse

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include reclassification, denial of deductions or treaty benefits, reassessment, interest, penalties, and exposure under applicable Quebec partnership and civil-liability rules.

Director personal liability: A limited partner may lose limited-liability protection to the extent provided by applicable law and the facts. The general partner ordinarily bears responsibility for partnership obligations; the precise exposure depends on the legal form, conduct, guarantees, and governing law.
Anti-money-laundering and criminal proceeds

Statutory sanction: Criminal Code and applicable proceeds-of-crime legislation may provide for asset restraint or forfeiture, fines, and imprisonment. Reporting and recordkeeping duties depend on whether the person or business is a regulated reporting entity and on the transaction.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly participate, direct, or facilitate prohibited conduct may face personal criminal liability. A restaurant or LP is not automatically a reporting entity for every AML obligation; applicability depends on the activity and statutory classification.
Quebec sales-tax and payroll-tax failures

Statutory sanction: CRA and Revenu Québec may assess tax, interest, late-remittance penalties, and other statutory penalties. Separate restaurant licensing, employment, and recordkeeping obligations may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate employer or registrant may be personally liable for specified unremitted source deductions or sales taxes under applicable statutory provisions, subject to statutory defences. LP liability must be assessed by reference to the general partner, responsible persons, and the specific statute.
