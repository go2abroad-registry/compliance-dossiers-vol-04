Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) - Canada.ca; GST/HST, corporate and partnership registration via Business Registration Online (CRA My Business Account portal)

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity registering as an Ontario limited partnership must first appoint an Ontario registered attorney for service, then apply to the Ontario Ministry of Public and Business Service Delivery for an Ontario Business Registry number (ONBRS), and subsequently register with the CRA using the Business Number (BN) as the statutory tax identifier. The partnership is required to open a GST/HST account by filing Form RC1, 'Request for a Business Number', through the CRA portal. A foreign limited partnership without a permanent establishment in Canada must still obtain a BN/GST/HST account if it makes taxable supplies in Canada or imports goods, and must provide its non-resident status declaration. For income tax purposes, the partnership itself is transparent; each partner must obtain its own Canadian tax identification number (BN for corporations or SIN for individuals) unless exempt under a treaty.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not have a 'check-the-box' entity classification election. Under the Income Tax Act, a limited partnership is deemed a separate person for certain GST/HST and income tax procedural purposes but is fiscally transparent for taxation of income. Partners may make a joint election under the Income Tax Act to treat an eligible Canadian partnership as a separate taxable entity for a taxation year (Section 96.1 election for partners that are non-resident), and may elect under subsection 216(1) in respect of property rental income, but no general pass-through election is available. For GST/HST, a partnership may elect to have one partner act as the registrar/representative for the partnership, and the partnership may make a joint election with its members to have the partnership account for tax under section 273 of the Excise Tax Act. Hybrid mismatch rules under Sections 93.2 and 94.2 may require the variation of income inclusions for partnership interests described in the OECD Hybrid Mismatch Recommendations; no elective domestic hybrid mismatch election exists.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual worldwide taxable supplies (other than zero-rated supplies) made by a non-resident registrant carrying on business in Canada; a non-resident without a Canadian permanent establishment is not required to register solely for B2B exported goods, but must register to claim input tax credits and to charge 13% HST on taxable supplies made in Ontario

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating for GST/HST may be claimed on supplies of goods exported from Canada (including cement clinker in bulk) where the supplier is a GST/HST registrant and the goods are exported by the supplier, at the supplier's direction, or by the purchaser as a commercial export carrier, as part of a supply to the non-resident purchaser. Offshore services relating to exported goods and certain transportation services are zero-rated under Schedule VI of the Excise Tax Act. The exporter must maintain documentary evidence establishing that the goods were physically delivered to a destination outside Canada within a reasonable time, that the goods were exported in accordance with the Customs Act, and that the supplier effected or caused the export. A non-resident limited partnership may also claim input tax credits for GST/HST paid on inputs used to produce zero-rated export supplies, applying for refunds under the Electronic Tax Services for non-residents if not resident but carrying on business in Canada.

  • Customs Export Declaration — Canada Border Services Agency eManifest export report (B13A form or electronic export declaration; required for cement clinker exports exceeding CAD 2,000 or controlled goods)
  • Documentary Evidence of Export — carrier receipt, port terminal receipt, or final destination delivery note evidencing departure from Canada
  • Bill of Lading — original or electronic negotiable ocean bill of lading issued by the common carrier for the bulk cement clinker cargo, evidencing the shipper, consignee, load port and discharge port
  • Inward Remittance Certificate — commercial invoice matched with bank SWIFT/TT wire transfer records showing proceeds received in foreign currency from the non-resident buyer
  • Cement Clinker Non-Exclusion Certificate — where applicable, CBSA export permit or restricted/controlled goods documentation if the clinker originates from a listed environmental or export-controlled source

No mandatory electronic invoicing or real-time fiscal clearance regime currently exists under Federal or Ontario law. CRA permits electronic invoices and retains records digitally. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically if annual taxable supplies exceed CAD 1,500,000. Electronic data interchange (EDI) can be used for customs declarations, and the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) requires advance electronic export information for all exports, including the Canadian Export Declaration eManifest portal, but there is no government fiscal stamping or API real-time clearance for B2B invoices. Registered supply must be reported on CRA Form GST34-2 or electronic remittance via CRA My Payment.

CRA may reassess a zero-rated export supply and claw back input tax credits if documentary requirements are not satisfied, with reassessment periods generally extending four years from the filing date (or up to ten years in cases of misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, or fraud). Penalties under the Excise Tax Act may include a failure-to-register penalty of the greater of CAD 250 and 1% of net tax, a 5% failure-to-file penalty plus 1% per month to a maximum of twelve months, and a gross negligence penalty of 50% of any tax or net tax understatement attributable to gross negligence. Under subsection 239(1) of the Income Tax Act and section 327 of the Excise Tax Act, fraudulent export claims can result in fines up to 200% of the tax evaded, imprisonment, and permanent revocation of reduced-frequency filing privileges. A non-resident purchaser's failure to repatriate proceeds within the 180-day documentary deadline does not itself affect the zero-rating, but it undermines the inward remittance evidence and may trigger reassessment and interest on uncollected HST.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current-account and capital-account convertibility on a free-floating currency basis. There are no general exchange-control permits, no prior central-bank approval for foreign direct investment in a limited partnership, and no restrictions on repatriation of partnership profits or capital distributions. Practical hurdles are limited to tax compliance and documentary proof: non-resident partners carrying on business in Ontario may be subject to Part I Canadian income tax on allocated partnership income and Part XIII non-resident withholding tax on certain Canadian-source amounts; a tax treaty relief or NR301/T106 documentation may be required. Inward investments above the WTO threshold may require an Investment Canada Act net-benefit review if the acquisition is not purely passive, but a wholesale trading LP is generally below that threshold or qualifies for review exemption.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export-proceeds surrender rule and no mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts. The Ontario limited partnership may invoice in USD, EUR, CAD, or any freely usable currency and may retain export earnings in a foreign-currency bank account in Canada. Relevant compliance obligations are: FINTRAC cross-border currency reporting for currency or monetary instruments exceeding CAD 10,000, CBSA customs declarations for goods, and Statistics Canada mandatory trade-data reporting for transactions above prescribed thresholds.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada). Canada does not operate a specialised public foreign-exchange control administration; currency and monetary policy rest with the Bank of Canada under the Bank of Canada Act, while banking safety and soundness are supervised by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI). The regulatory control lever for cross-border monetary movements is applied through FINTRAC reporting and OSFI governance rather than exchange controls.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low to Medium. An offshore digital EMI is not a Canadian banking entity and cannot provide direct LVTS/Lynx clearing membership or a true Canadian deposit account. It may operate only as an unregulated or MSB-registered payment intermediary, and any access to Canadian-dollar clearing requires a partnership with a licensed Schedule I bank or a Canadian money-services business licence under FINTRAC and the Retail Payment Activities Act. Use of a foreign EMI as a primary liquidity rail is legally constrained for a wholesale cement trader; acceptable uses are limited to secondary FX execution and settlement of non-regulated international invoices.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 (Form T106 reportable transaction filing threshold); CRA also expects contemporaneous TP documentation for any specified cross-border transaction exceeding CAD 500,000.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone ESR; however, GAAR (s.245) and TP rules (s.247) require genuine economic substance. An Ontario LP with non-resident partners must maintain substantive distribution activity, local decision-making, bank accounts and commercial records in Ontario; artificial structures can be recharacterized.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Failure to File

Statutory sanction: Gross negligence penalty of 50% of understated tax (s.163); criminal tax evasion fines from 50% to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment up to 5 years (s.239).

Director personal liability: General partner and controlling directors are personally and jointly liable for unpaid source deductions and Part XIII withholding (s.227.1) and can be assessed under s.160 for asset stripping; travel bans, seizures, and imprisonment apply in fraud cases.
Transfer Pricing Violations

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing adjustment; s.247(3) penalty equal to 10% of the net adjustment where the documentation/reasonable-effort standard is breached; overdue interest and compound interest; secondary adjustments create deemed dividends/PUC and Part XIII withholding tax.

Director personal liability: General partners who sign incorrect returns are jointly and severally liable for penalties; directors may be assessed for unremitted withholding tax; severe violations can trigger Ministerial Enforcement, temporary asset freezes, and travel restrictions.
AML / Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative penalties up to CAD 1,000,000 per violation under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act; criminal money-laundering offence under the Criminal Code: up to 10 years imprisonment; GAAR reassessment and penalties for sham transactions.

Director personal liability: Directors and beneficial owners risk criminal prosecution, extradition, passport revocation and travel bans; courts can pierce the LP veil to impose restitution and forfeiture of assets; general partners have unlimited personal liability.
