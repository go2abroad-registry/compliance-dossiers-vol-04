Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no U.S.-style 'check-the-box' classification regime; a business corporation is a separate person and resident taxpayer under Part I of the Income Tax Act. Elective mechanisms for tax structuring include s. 85(1)/(2) rollovers on property transfers into a corporation, s. 83(2) capital dividend elections, and the s. 216 and s. 219 non-resident election regimes. GST/HST elections include s. 156 group registration, s. 157 qualifying joint venture election, and s. 273(1)(e) elective distribution-disregard treatment. Hybrid mismatch outcomes are governed by income tax treaties and the OECD hybrid rules, not by domestic entity elections.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies in any single calendar quarter or cumulatively over the four most recent consecutive calendar quarters (small-supplier threshold under Excise Tax Act s. 148(1)); registration is mandatory on breach of the threshold for commercial activity in Canada, including remote and digital sales, with voluntary registration available.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of goods physically exported by or on behalf of the supplier are zero-rated under Excise Tax Act s. 22 where the goods are exported as soon as is reasonably possible after the goods are delivered; services supplied to non-residents are generally zero-rated under s. 23 if performed for a non-resident who is not registered for GST/HST and the benefit is received entirely outside Canada. For cold-chain keg wholesale and export of artisanal hazy IPAs, zero-rating requires offshore passage of title and risk under the agreed Incoterms (e.g., FOB, EXW, CIF) and documentary proof of export held by the supplier for stated verification periods. Domestic Ontario interprovincial sales remain subject to 13% HST, and excise duty on beer under the Excise Act, 2001 is not relieved by GST/HST zero-rating.

  • Canada Export Declaration (B13A) filed electronically via the Canadian Export Reporting System for goods exceeding CAD 2,000 destined outside Canada (with exceptions for certain low-value US-bound shipments)
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill issued by the carrier evidencing physical exit of the goods from Canada
  • Commercial Invoice and purchase order identifying SKU, keg count, Incoterms, unit values, and Canadian excise identifiers
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / SWIFT MT103 or bank payment confirmation evidencing foreign currency repatriation
  • CBSA export confirmation (where applicable), packing list, and cold-chain temperature logger records supporting the export supply chain

Canada does not currently mandate real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or API-based continuous transaction controls for B2B/B2C supply. CRA requires electronic filing of GST/HST returns for many registrants and electronic reporting of export data. The Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Framework adopts Peppol BIS 3.0 on a voluntary basis and is increasingly required for public-sector suppliers, including Ontario government contracts. CRA is also piloting real-time GST/HST data exchange and digital tax reporting for large businesses, so cold-chain keg exporters must retain machine-readable export evidence such as EDI/Peppol invoices, carrier telematic proof, and B13A export declarations.

CRA may reassess zero-rated export claims within the normal 4-year reassessment period and without limitation where a false statement was made negligently, carelessly, or wilfully. Civil penalties include ETA s. 281.2 (greater of CAD 250 and 25% of any improperly claimed ITC, rebate, or refund) and ETA s. 285 penalties for false statements or omissions (summary conviction fines up to CAD 25,000 and/or 12 months imprisonment; indictable fines up to CAD 100,000 and/or 5 years imprisonment). Wilful evasion under ETA s. 327 triggers fines up to CAD 100,000 on indictment or double the tax evaded, imprisonment up to 5 years, retroactive clawback of refunds, interest under ETA s. 280, and continued denial of zero-rating privileges. Customs Act ss. 109.1 to 109.3 penalties may additionally apply for export declaration misstatements.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully and unconditionally convertible on both current and capital accounts, consistent with Canada's Article VIII obligations under the IMF Articles of Agreement. There are no residual exchange controls, quantitative limits, or licensing requirements affecting profit remittance, dividend distributions, interest, royalties, or management fees to non-resident shareholders. Non-resident remittance taxes arise solely under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (statutory 25% withholding, reduced to 15% for portfolio holdings and 5% for qualifying ownership under the Canada-U.S. Treaty, with analogous relief under Canada-U.K. and CETA arrangements); the only documentary friction is the NR4 / NR4-S information return and CRA verification of beneficial ownership before relief is allowed. The practical settlement friction is standard bank pricing rather than regulatory convertibility restriction.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory timeline for repatriation of export proceeds and no mandatory conversion ratio. An Ontario-licensed exporter of craft beer may invoice, receive, and hold USD, EUR, or any freely convertible currency indefinitely in a domestic or foreign bank account. Compliance obligations are limited to: (i) statistical returns to Statistics Canada (BOP-71 / trade surveys); (ii) CRA record-keeping in support of the T2 corporate tax return; (iii) FINTRAC large-transaction and EFT reporting for transactions at or above C$10,000; and (iv) normal AGCO / CBSA export documentation. No foreign-currency deposit ceiling, conversion deadline, or surrender rule exists.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), with prudential oversight coordinated through the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and AML/CFT enforcement through FINTRAC; no separate foreign-exchange administration agency exists because Canada operates a fully floating exchange-rate regime without exchange controls.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: MEDIUM (narrow use-case only). Wise for Business, Airwallex, Payoneer, and Stripe Treasury operate in Canada as Money Services Businesses (MSB) registered with FINTRAC, or as unregulated foreign fintech entities, rather than as Schedule I banks under the Bank Act. Legal constraints: (i) they cannot accept banking-law 'deposits' or provide demand-deposit protections; (ii) they are excluded from CDIC deposit insurance; (iii) they cannot extend credit, overdrafts, banker's acceptances, or commercial letters of credit without a Schedule I/II bank partner; (iv) they hold funds in pooled safeguarded ledgers, creating liquidity-cascade and subordination risk; (v) they must comply with Ontario MSB licensing and FINTRAC EFT reporting and cannot accept cash. They are viable only as a secondary USD collection rail for cross-border licence fees and small receivables, while the primary operating and escrow accounts remain with a Tier-1 domestic bank to satisfy AGCO licensing and LCBO distribution counterparty requirements.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant protocol: (a) inbound SWIFT MT103 / ISO 20022 transfers with the payment-purpose field referencing 'Capital / Equity Contribution' or 'Intercompany Loan' and the Ontario share-register entity; Canada operates no purpose-code approval regime equivalent to SAFE, so the bank's narrative field must simply match the underlying board documents. (b) For a non-resident parent capital injection, the bank requires the share certificate, updated register of members, and the FX ticket; if the transfer exceeds C$1M, a Statistics Canada foreign-direct-investment survey return (CF 1033) must be completed. Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

For an Ontario corporation, the general combined federal and Ontario corporate income tax rate is 26.5% (15% federal plus 11.5% Ontario). A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) may generally receive the small business deduction on up to CAD 500,000 of qualifying active business income, producing a combined rate of 12.2% (9% federal plus 3.2% Ontario), subject to business-limit reductions and eligibility rules. The federal business limit is shared with associated corporations and is reduced for taxable capital employed in Canada above CAD 10 million and, for taxation years beginning after 2018, for adjusted aggregate investment income between CAD 50,000 and CAD 150,000. A non-CCPC or a corporation controlled by non-residents generally cannot claim the CCPC small business deduction.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The domestic Part XIII withholding tax rate on dividends paid or credited by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident is generally 25%, subject to reduction under an applicable tax treaty. A treaty reduction commonly requires the beneficial owner to qualify for treaty benefits and the payer to obtain appropriate residence and beneficial-ownership documentation before applying the reduced rate.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose an OECD-style Master File or Local File filing threshold as a general rule. Under Income Tax Act section 247, contemporaneous documentation must be prepared by the documentation-due date for non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents. The special transfer-pricing penalty generally applies where the net transfer-pricing adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5 million and 10% of the taxpayer's gross revenue, unless the adjustment is 10% or less of gross revenue; this is a penalty threshold, not a documentation exemption. Form T106 reporting is generally required when the total reportable transactions with non-arm's-length non-residents exceed CAD 1 million in the year, subject to the form's rules and exceptions.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no general United Arab Emirates-style Economic Substance Regulations regime. Canadian transfer-pricing rules require arm's-length pricing and contemporaneous documentation for relevant cross-border non-arm's-length transactions. Other substance-related rules include the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty anti-abuse provisions, beneficial-ownership requirements, thin-capitalization rules, interest and financing expense limitation rules, and foreign-affiliate rules. The actual functions performed, assets used, and risks controlled should support the allocation of income and expenses.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Corporate income tax evasion or false reporting

Statutory sanction: Civil penalties may include a gross-negligence penalty generally equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the understated tax or overstated credits. Tax evasion prosecuted under the Income Tax Act may result in a fine of 50% to 200% of tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years; additional offences can carry separate sanctions.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for corporate income tax merely because they hold office. Personal exposure may arise for participation in an offence, director-liability provisions applicable to specific debts, or a court-imposed fine or sentence. Corporate separateness does not protect an individual from personal criminal liability for their own conduct.
Failure to remit source deductions or non-resident withholding tax

Statutory sanction: Unremitted amounts are recoverable with interest and applicable penalties. Failure-to-remit penalties can reach 10% of the amount not remitted, or 20% for a repeated failure or knowing or grossly negligent conduct. Directors may be jointly and severally liable for specified unremitted amounts, subject to statutory due-diligence and limitation rules.

Director personal liability: A director may be assessed personally for specified unremitted source deductions and other statutory remittances if the corporation fails to pay, subject to the applicable statutory conditions and due-diligence defence. This liability is not a general personal liability for corporate income tax.
Transfer-pricing non-compliance

Statutory sanction: The CRA may adjust income or deductions to an arm's-length amount. The transfer-pricing penalty is generally 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment where the statutory threshold is exceeded and reasonable efforts were not made. Separate penalties may apply for late or incomplete information returns, with interest on resulting tax.

Director personal liability: Transfer-pricing assessments and penalties are generally imposed on the corporation. Directors may face personal exposure for their own participation in false filings or offences, but are not automatically personally liable for the corporation's transfer-pricing adjustment.
Unlicensed alcohol manufacture, distribution, or excise-duty non-compliance

Statutory sanction: The Excise Act, 2001 and provincial alcohol-control legislation provide for assessments, interest, administrative penalties, seizure or forfeiture, licence suspension or cancellation, and prosecution. Criminal consequences and monetary penalties depend on the offence and facts; no single penalty applies to every licensing or excise breach.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal criminal liability for their own authorization, participation, or other conduct. Statutory director liability may apply to specified unremitted amounts where its legal conditions are met. Licence consequences can prevent the corporation from lawfully continuing the relevant operations.
Fraudulent conveyance, sham entity, or abusive tax arrangement

Statutory sanction: Consequences may include denial of deductions or treaty relief, reassessment, penalties, interest, transaction remedies, and civil or criminal proceedings under the applicable tax, corporate, insolvency, and criminal laws. There is no general rule imposing a travel ban solely for corporate tax non-compliance in Canada.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for their own fraud, statutory breaches, or participation in an offence. Veil-piercing is exceptional and fact-dependent; it is not an automatic tax-collection remedy. Civil recovery or imprisonment requires an applicable legal basis and judicial process.
Money laundering or proceeds-of-crime violations

Statutory sanction: Criminal Code offences may result in forfeiture, fines, and imprisonment; penalties depend on the offence and prosecution. FINTRAC reporting, registration, and compliance duties apply only if the business falls within the statutory definition of a reporting entity or otherwise has an applicable legal obligation.

Director personal liability: Individuals may be prosecuted for their own knowing participation or for applicable corporate-officer offences. Director status alone does not establish criminal liability. A travel restriction is not automatic and requires an applicable court order or other statutory authority.
