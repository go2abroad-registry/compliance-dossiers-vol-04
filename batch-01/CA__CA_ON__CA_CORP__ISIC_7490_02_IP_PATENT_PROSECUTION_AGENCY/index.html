Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency / Agence du revenu du Canada (CRA). Web portals: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-businesses/business-account.html) and Business Registration Online (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-businesses/business-registration-online.html). Ontario corporate income tax is administered by the CRA on behalf of Ontario under the Federal-Provincial Tax Collection Agreement; the Ontario Business Registry (https://www.ontario.ca/page/ontario-business-registry) handles provincial incorporation and Employer Health Tax (EHT) registration.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Incorporate a limited-liability Business Corporation under the Canada Business Corporations Act (CBCA) or the Ontario Business Corporations Act (OBCA) through the applicable corporate registry; obtain Articles of Incorporation and complete the Ontario Initial Return if provincially incorporated. Step 2: Apply to the CRA for a nine-digit Business Number (BN) via Business Registration Online (BRO) or Form RC1; the BN is the universal tax identifier and is supplemented by two-letter program accounts: 'RC' for federal and Ontario corporate income tax, 'RT' for GST/HST, and 'RP' for payroll. Step 3: Register the RC program account to establish the corporate taxpayer identity; a non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada or disposing of taxable Canadian property must file a Canadian corporate income tax return. Step 4: Register the RT (GST/HST) program account if taxable supplies exceed the C$30,000 small-supplier threshold, or voluntarily to claim input tax credits; the RT number is the Canadian GST/HST registration identifier. Step 5: Register the RP payroll account if employees are hired; EHT registration with the Ontario Ministry of Finance is required only if annual Ontario payroll exceeds C$1,000,000. Step 6: For non-resident-owned corporations, provide non-resident director/officer information and Canadian agent/resident representative details on Form RC1, and maintain books and records in Canada or make them available to the CRA.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — a CBCA/OBCA business corporation is a separate taxpayer under the Income Tax Act (Canada). A resident corporation is taxed on worldwide income; Ontario provincial corporate income tax is imposed on income attributable to a permanent establishment in Ontario and is collected by the CRA. No pass-through treatment is available, and the corporation is not situated in a tax-exempt zone.

Available election forms

Canada does not maintain a 'check-the-box' regime for incorporated entities; a validly incorporated business corporation is per se taxable at the entity level. Available elections relevant to a patent prosecution agency include: (1) fiscal-period election under s. 249.1 of the Income Tax Act for a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) to adopt a non-calendar taxation year; (2) GST/HST Quick Method election under s. 229 of the Excise Tax Act for registrants with worldwide taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies) not exceeding C$400,000 in a fiscal year, subject to CRA approval; (3) related-corporation loss-consolidation election under s. 156 of the Income Tax Act; and (4) elections affecting CCPC status via share structure and Articles. Hybrid mismatch arrangements are addressed by ss. 18.4–18.7 of the Income Tax Act and the OECD/G20 BEPS framework, with no material elective carve-outs available to a Canadian patent prosecution agency.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

C$30,000 — the small-supplier threshold. A corporation becomes a mandatory GST/HST registrant once its worldwide taxable supplies (other than zero-rated financial services) exceed C$30,000 in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters or in the current calendar quarter. Patent prosecution, trademark registration, and IP advisory services are taxable supplies, so registration is mandatory once billings exceed C$30,000; voluntary registration is available and generally advisable from inception to permit input tax credit recovery.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of patent prosecution, PCT prosecution support, trademark registration, and associated IP advisory services rendered to non-resident clients may be zero-rated (0% HST) under s. 9, Part V, Schedule VI of the Excise Tax Act provided: (i) the recipient is a non-resident person not registered or required to be registered for GST/HST; (ii) the service is not rendered to an individual present in Canada; (iii) the service does not relate to real property situated in Canada; and (iv) the service does not relate to tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time of performance. Services involving Canadian CIPO patent filings generally qualify as intangible professional services supplied to and benefiting the foreign applicant. Assignments or licences of Canadian patent rights to a non-resident are generally zero-rated under s. 10 of Part V, Schedule VI where the intangible property is not for use in Canada. Domestic supplies to Canadian residents are subject to 13% HST. Zero-rated exports generate full input tax credits; a net HST refund is claimed on the regular GST/HST return when input tax credits exceed output tax, with refunds payable by the CRA without a separate refund application.

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, tax-authority API stamping, or digital stamp regime exists in Canada. The CRA supports a voluntary Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Framework built on the PEPPOL network, but participation is optional. Mandatory requirements include: electronic filing of GST/HST returns by all registrants (through GST/HST NETFILE or a registered data interchange service), electronic remittance of GST/HST amounts exceeding C$25,000, and retention of invoices and records in readable electronic format. Invoices must be issued for all taxable supplies, but no pre-clearance or fiscal approval is required.

CRA audits zero-rated non-resident invoices closely to verify the recipient's non-resident status and the absence of Canadian property nexus. If zero-rating is disallowed, output tax at 13% HST is reassessed with arrears interest compounded daily at the CRA prescribed rate plus a failure-to-remit penalty under s. 280 of the Excise Tax Act equal to 5% of the unreported tax plus 3% per complete month of continued failure, capped at 20%. Gross negligence in claiming zero-rating attracts a 25% penalty under s. 285 of the Excise Tax Act. Wilful evasion is a criminal offence under s. 327 of the Excise Tax Act and the Income Tax Act, punishable by fines of up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment. Retrospective clawbacks generally apply within the normal four-year reassessment window, extended to seven years for misrepresentation and without limitation for fraud. Directors and officers may incur personal liability for unremitted GST/HST under s. 323 of the Excise Tax Act, and the CRA may require security from registrants related to non-residents under s. 306 of the Excise Tax Act.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada is an IMF Article VIII member with unrestricted current and capital account convertibility. There are no exchange-control permits for inbound equity injections, cross-border service fees, profit remittance, or dividend payments. Profit and royalty remittances are freely transferable but are subject to Part XIII non-resident withholding tax of 25% statutory, normally treaty-reduced (e.g., 5% for U.S.-resident direct investors under the Canada-U.S. treaty, where the beneficial owner threshold is satisfied). Patent-prosecution fee receipts from foreign clients are current-account credits and incur no currency conversion restriction; CRA Form T106 and international financial reporting (e.g., T1134 for controlled foreign affiliates) apply if cross-border related-party thresholds are crossed.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender requirement, mandatory conversion ratio, or repatriation timeline for export or service-proceeds receipts. A Canadian patent agency may retain USD, EUR, CHF, or other currencies in foreign-currency operating accounts for subsequent settlement of official patent-office fees. There are only transactional-reporting obligations: FINTRAC large cash transaction reports for transactions above CAD 10,000, suspicious transaction reporting where indicators arise, and general record-keeping under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). Foreign exchange realised gains/losses are taxed as ordinary income under CRA rules and must be tracked per currency-denominated receivable.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) is the central monetary authority. No dedicated foreign-exchange administration agency exists because Canada operates a fully liberalised exchange-control regime; banking supervision rests with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), anti-money-laundering oversight with the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), and tax/withholding administration with the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA). The patent prosecution profession is separately regulated by the College of Patent Agents and Trademark Agents (CPATA) under federal and Ontario statutes.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium for disbursement-only rails; Low-Moderate for trust/escrow balances. Authorised FINTRAC-registered Money Services Businesses and digital EMIs such as Wise, OFX, and Airwallex can serve as efficient cross-border payment gateways for official patent-office fees and associate settlements, including API-driven batch disbursements. They cannot lawfully hold 'client trust funds' in Canada as deposit-taking institutions unless duly authorised under the Bank Act and registered under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA); therefore, client funds held for patent and trademark filing payments should stay within a chartered bank trust account compliant with CPATA rules. Fintech EMIs are viable for treasury efficiency but must not be the sole custody rail for third-party funds.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity subscription wires from a foreign parent or investor are settled via SWIFT MT103 to the Ontario corporation's operating account at a Canadian Schedule I bank. There are no Canadian FX permits or purpose-code mandates, but the remitter should place a precise payment-particulars string such as 'Share capital subscription — [OBCA/CBCA] — [RA Number]' to permit straight-through processing and CRA side-informs. The bank will require FATCA/CRS self-certification for the investor; Canadian corporate counsel should confirm the subscriber's proportional paid-up capital and issue director's resolution. If the shareholder is non-Canadian, counsel should separately assess the Investment Canada Act regime for a new Canadian business (notification) and any national-security review consideration before the wire is honoured. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium for disbursement-only rails; Low-Moderate for trust/escrow balances. Authorised FINTRAC-registered Money Services Businesses and digital EMIs such as Wise, OFX, and Airwallex can serve as efficient cross-border payment gateways for official patent-office fees and associate settlements, including API-driven batch disbursements. They cannot lawfully hold 'client trust funds' in Canada as deposit-taking institutions unless duly authorised under the Bank Act and registered under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA); therefore, client funds held for patent and trademark filing payments should stay within a chartered bank trust account compliant with CPATA rules. Fintech EMIs are viable for treasury efficiency but must not be the sole custody rail for third-party funds.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

25% combined federal (15%) and Ontario (10%) general corporate income tax rate; 12.2% combined rate for qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) active business income up to CAD 500,000 annually.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% statutory dividend withholding tax imposed under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (Canada) on dividends paid to non-resident shareholders; tax treaties may reduce the rate, typically to 15% for portfolio investors and 5% for direct investors owning at least 25% of the shares.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Contemporaneous Local File and Master File transfer-pricing documentation is required when the aggregate fair-market value of all cross-border non-arm's-length transactions with related parties exceeds CAD 1 million in the taxation year; a Country-by-Country Report (CBCR) is mandatory for multinational enterprise groups with consolidated group revenue of at least CAD 850 million.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain a separate Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) codification as seen in offshore jurisdictions; however, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA s.245), the transfer-pricing recharacterization rule (ITA s.247), and common-law doctrines of artificial transactions and sham require the patent prosecution agency to demonstrate actual business functions, personnel, decision-making, and risk-bearing activity in Ontario. Contractual arrangements that shift patent prosecution income without substantive Canadian employees, premises, or management will be disregarded for Canadian tax purposes.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under ITA s.239 imposes a fine not exceeding 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to five years; civil gross-negligence penalty under ITA s.163(2) equals 100% of the understated tax; CRA may also impose s.162(2) late-filing penalties and daily compounded arrears interest.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign or cause the filing of false returns are joint and severally liable in civil fraud for corporate tax amounts when the corporate form is used as a sham; they may be personally indicted, subjected to criminal fines, ordered to make restitution, restrained from leaving Canada, and imprisoned for willful evasion.
Unremitted Withholding Taxes and Source Deductions

Statutory sanction: ITA s.227.1(1) and Excise Tax Act s.323 impose director's liability for the full unremitted amount plus statutory interest and penalties; CRA may garnish bank accounts, impose wages garnishees, and issue director's liability assessments without a court order.

Director personal liability: Director's personal assets, including residential property, can be seized to satisfy the corporation's unremitted withholdings if the director failed to exercise reasonable care, diligence, and skill; a director may face travel restrictions under CRA collection enforcement and upon serious non-remittance, criminal sanctions under ITA s.239 for misapplication of funds.
Artificial Shell Company and Inadequate Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: GAAR under ITA s.245 disallows the tax benefit and reassesses the corporation at statutory interest with penalties; transfer-pricing adjustments under ITA s.247(2) can result in a 10% penalty on net upward adjustments exceeding CAD 5 million; thin capitalization interest deductions are denied under ITA s.18(4); the Ontario Corporations Tax Act may reassess allocated taxable income.

Director personal liability: Courts will pierce the corporate veil where the company is a folly or façade; directors and shareholders may be held personally liable for unpaid taxes and for fraudulent conveyance of corporate assets; senior officers who design the shell structure face personal exposure to GAAR consequences, cease-and-desist orders, and possible criminal tax evasion counts for obstruction.
AML / FINTRAC Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), administrative monetary penalties can reach CAD 1 million per violation for a corporation; criminal proceedings may impose fines and imprisonment up to five years for a wilful failure to maintain a compliance program or report suspicious transactions.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers are liable for corporate AML breaches where they knowingly failed to establish an adequate compliance program or directed the structuring of payments; courts may assess personal fines, seize personal assets, and prohibit individuals from undertaking financial services business activity in Canada; international travel to and from Canada may be restricted under financial-crime investigative orders.
