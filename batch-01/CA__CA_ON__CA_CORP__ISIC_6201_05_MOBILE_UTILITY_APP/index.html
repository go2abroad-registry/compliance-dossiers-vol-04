Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Revenu Québec (for Québec-specific accounts); federal GST/HST registration via CRA My Business Account or Form GST1; provincial Ontario registration integrated with CRA for GST/HST and Ontario corporate tax administered by the Ontario Ministry of Finance through the Corporate Tax Information system (CTIS) / CRA joint administration

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity carrying on business in Canada must register for the federal GST/HST account with the CRA by filing Form GST1 (or through a tax intermediary) before making taxable supplies in Canada. The entity obtains a Business Number (BN) through the CRA's Business Registration Online portal, after which a separate GST/HST program account (RT) is created. For Ontario corporate income tax, the foreign entity must register with the Ontario Ministry of Finance via the CRA's Corporate Tax Information system to obtain an Ontario corporate tax account (incorporation or extra-provincial registration number). A Canadian resident corporation is required to file an initial corporate income tax return (T2) and obtain a federal corporation income tax account; non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada must obtain a non-resident corporation tax account and a program account for Part XIII non-resident withholding where applicable. For GST/HST purposes, the normal registration threshold applies to resident and non-resident persons making taxable supplies in Canada, subject to the special rules for non-resident persons and 'specified supplies' made through digital platforms; the foreign entity must also consider whether its supplies of mobile utility software are subject to the simplified GST/HST registration regime for non-resident vendors and the electronic platform rules requiring platform operators to collect and remit GST/HST on enumerated supplies.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canadian federal income tax does not provide a general 'check-the-box' entity classification election akin to the United States. Foreign entities are classified for Canadian tax purposes based on their legal form and attributes under the Income Tax Act (Canada), including whether they are a 'corporation' for Canadian purposes. A non-resident entity may be treated as a corporation, partnership, or trust according to its civil-law/legal attributes. Hybrid mismatch rules under section 18.15 and related provisions of the Income Tax Act apply to address mismatches in respect of hybrid instruments and disregarded payments, but no elective reclassification is generally available. A Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) status election under subsection 125(7) may apply to determine whether a corporation is a CCPC, and a corporation can elect under subsection 89(1) to be treated as not being a public corporation in limited circumstances, but none of these constitute an entity classification check-the-box election. For GST/HST, a non-resident entity carrying on business in Canada may elect to have a Canadian resident agent act as its representative under section 321 of the Excise Tax Act (Canada) for compliance purposes. An entity may also elect to file a combined GST/HST return with associated corporations under the related corporation election rules (section 156 of the Excise Tax Act), and may make an election to treat a supply of intangible personal property as otherwise than a financial service in certain circumstances. The substantive entity-level taxation model applies, with no pass-through classification available for a business corporation.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (worldwide taxable supplies of the person and associated persons; small supplier threshold); for non-resident vendors making 'specified supplies' through digital platforms, the threshold remains CAD 30,000 in Canadian-source taxable supplies, but non-resident vendors may be required to register if they make taxable supplies in Canada in the ordinary course and are not small suppliers; special mandatory registration applies for operators of digital platforms in respect of specified supplies made by non-resident vendors through the platform, with no threshold for platform operators once any specified supply is made through the platform (subject to transitional rules).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply is zero-rated as an export under sections 1 through 6 of Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act (Canada) if the supplier exports tangible personal property (including software supplied by tangible media) and meets the documentary requirements in section 6 of Schedule VI, which require proof of exportation such as bills of lading, customs entries, or other prescribed documentation. For intangible personal property (including software apps supplied by download), a supply is generally zero-rated where the recipient is a non-resident person, the supply is not a supply of a service, and the intangible property is not to be used in Canada, provided the recipient is not registered for GST/HST (unless the supply is a prescribed supply, such as intangible property used in Canada). Mobile utility application software sold through app stores is generally characterized as intangible personal property; if sold to a non-resident individual or business for use outside Canada, the supply may be zero-rated under section 13 of Part V of Schedule VI (intangible personal property to a non-resident) or section 21 if the supply is made through a digital platform where the platform operator collects tax. However, supplies made through Apple App Store and Google Play are typically facilitated by the platform operator and may be subject to reverse-charge or place-of-supply rules that treat the supply as made in Canada if the recipient is in Canada or the platform operator is a registrant. For goods exported by the corporation, zero-rating also applies to a supply of tangible personal property that is delivered to a common carrier for export by the supplier or the recipient. The exporter must obtain and retain proof of exportation within two years. Where software is delivered by electronic means, no customs export declaration is applicable, and the substantiation bundle differs from goods exports. The corporation must analyze the app store distribution channel because Apple and Google act as commercial agents or disclosed principals; the corporation's direct sales to end users may be deemed supplies to non-residents or supplies made through the platform under the section 21.1 deemed supply rules, with the platform operator required to collect GST/HST if the end user is in Canada. The zero-rating of offshore services: a supply of a service made to a non-resident person is generally zero-rated if the service is supplied to a non-resident who is not registered under the GST/HST, and the service is not related to real property situated in Canada or tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time the service is performed, and the service is not a 'consulting service' or other prescribed service, subject to the exception in section 7 of Part V of Schedule VI. Mobile app maintenance or cloud hosting services supplied to non-residents may be zero-rated if the service is performed for a non-resident and no Canadian tangible property (e.g., servers in Canada) is a 'substantial' part of the service; the place-of-supply rules for services may deem the supply to be made in Canada if the recipient is in Canada under the normal place-of-supply rules for business customers (based on the recipient's province), or for consumers based on the supplier's establishment. The corporation must obtain documentary evidence of the non-resident status of the recipient and retain it with the GST/HST records.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada Border Services Agency Form B13A for goods exports; not applicable to electronic software downloads)
  • Bill of Lading / Airway Bill (for goods exports, if any physical media or hardware associated with the mobile utility app is sold and exported)
  • Proof of Export (CRA-prescribed documentary evidence under section 6 of Schedule VI, including a completed Form GST222 or a commercial invoice with a carrier's signature, customs office stamp, or an authorized exporter's certificate)
  • Commercial Invoice with cross-border supply terms, non-resident recipient name and address, and description of the intangible property or service
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank/payment processor settlement records evidencing receipt of consideration from non-resident purchasers (for substantiation of export proceeds and foreign exchange repatriation)
  • Subscriber or transaction-level records from Apple IAP and Google Play Billing establishing non-resident purchaser status (IP address, Apple/Google account country, App Store territory of download)
  • Written declaration from the non-resident recipient (where the recipient is a business) confirming the recipient is not registered for GST/HST and the intangible property is acquired for use exclusively outside Canada
  • Service agreement or platform terms of service documenting the supply of offshore services and the absence of Canadian real or tangible property nexus

Canada does not impose a mandatory central electronic invoicing clearance or continuous transaction controls (CTC) model comparable to Latin American real-time fiscal clearance system. E-invoicing is not universally mandated at the federal level. The CRA accepts electronic records and invoices issued in digital format, and requires that GST/HST registrants maintain books and records in a readable format. Quebec has a mandatory electronic invoicing pilot and reporting regime (with E-invoicing obligations phased for large enterprises effective from September 1, 2026, and mandatory E-invoicing for all enterprises by September 1, 2027, under Revenu Québec's electronic invoicing platform standards, which are integrated with the PEPPOL network). Ontario has no separate provincial e-invoicing mandate. For GST/HST purposes, a qualifying 'electronic data interchange' (EDI) system or a signed EDI in electronic form may be acceptable without a hardcopy equivalent if the supplier and recipient agree. However, there is no requirement for API-based real-time fiscal clearance, digital fiscal stamps, or pre-clearance of invoices by the CRA. Suppliers must issue invoices containing prescribed information in a manner that permits the recipient to claim input tax credits, and must report certain electronic commerce supplies under simplified rules in the GST/HST return. Non-residents participating in the digital economy may be required to register through the CRA's Online GST/HST Registration for Non-Resident Vendors using a simplified process, with returns filed under the simplified 'specified supplies' regime.

Under the Excise Tax Act, a zero-rated export claim may be denied where the supplier fails to obtain or retain the required proof of export documentation within two years from the date of the return; if documentation is not contemporaneous, the CRA may assess additional GST/HST payable, apply the general six-year reassessment period (or a longer period in cases of misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, or wilful default), and impose penalties under subsections 280(1) for failure to file, and section 285 for false statements or omissions in a return. A person who knowingly or under circumstances of culpable conduct makes a false or deceptive statement in a return, or knowingly participates in a fraudulent zero-rating claim, is liable to a penalty of 50% of the amount of the understated tax or overstated refund under subsection 285(2). Interest applies on unpaid federal GST/HST and corporate income tax at the prescribed rate compounded daily. For corporate income tax, fraudulent or reckless export claims can trigger the penalty for false statements under subsection 163(2) (equal to the greater of $100 and 50% of the understated tax) and potentially criminal prosecution under section 380 of the Criminal Code (Canada). Retrospective clawbacks: if the CRA determines that the zero-rating was not substantiated or that the supply was actually made in Canada, it may reassess the registrant to collect the unremitted GST/HST plus interest and penalties, and may also deny corresponding input tax credits claimed. The Foreign Affiliate Reporting and transfer pricing regimes under sections 233.1 and 247 of the Income Tax Act impose additional penalties for failing to provide documentation of cross-border transactions, including transactions with non-resident distributors or platform intermediaries. The CRA also has the authority to revoke registration and to hold directors personally liable for failure to remit assessed GST/HST under section 323 of the Excise Tax Act. In the context of app store sales, if the corporation misclassifies Canadian consumer sales as zero-rated exports based on the payment processor's billing address, the CRA may assess GST/HST on the full amount of Canadian downloads, apply the 50% gross-negligence penalty, and pursue penalties under the Electronic Commerce rules, including liability of the non-resident vendor for the tax even if the platform operator failed to collect it.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full convertibility on both current and capital accounts. There are no exchange-control restrictions, no surrender requirements, and no central-bank approvals for profit remittances. Non-resident shareholders may receive dividends through ordinary Canadian banking channels, subject solely to reporting under the Income Tax Act and Part XIII non-resident withholding tax (25% statutory rate, generally reduced to 5–15% under the Canada–US Tax Treaty and other applicable treaties). No quota, license, or prior-repatriation filing is required.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Not applicable. Canada imposes no export-proceeds surrender rule, no mandatory foreign-currency conversion ratio, no statutory repatriation timeline, and no requirement to segregate export receipts in designated government accounts. Exporters may retain, hedge, or disburse foreign currency freely through any Canadian chartered bank or authorized foreign-bank branch.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), acting as the sovereign monetary authority; foreign-exchange administration in Canada is not conducted as a system of exchange-control licensing, with complementary supervision by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Low-to-Medium for a pure offshore EMI solution. Canada does not charter standalone Electronic Money Institutions; non-bank payment providers must register as Money Services Businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and then enter into sponsored settlement arrangements with a Tier 1 chartered bank. Offshore digital EMIs cannot directly access Payments Canada clearing or Interac rails; they would generally require a Canadian Ontario subsidiary registered as an MSB and a physical settlement account at a Canadian financial institution.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant routing uses SWIFT MT103 for non-US-sourced shareholder equity injections, or US Federal Reserve / Fedwire for from-US transfers. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined federal and Ontario general corporate income tax rate (15.0% federal Part I general rate plus 11.5% Ontario general rate); applicable to non-CCPCs and CCPC income above the small-business limit.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act on dividends paid by a Canadian-resident corporation to non-resident shareholders; Ontario does not levy a separate dividend withholding tax. Treaty reduced rates commonly apply: 15% for portfolio shareholders and 5% for corporate shareholders owning at least 10% of the voting shares.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose a bright-line transaction amount for preparing a local or master file; contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation is required for all cross-border non-arm's-length transactions under ITA s. 247(4) and CRA IC-87-2R. Simplified documentation applies generally to small and medium enterprises with annual gross revenue under C$20 million and cross-border controlled transactions under C$1 million. A Country-by-Country Report is mandatory for Canadian-parent MNE groups with consolidated group revenue of C$750 million or more.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations, but economic substance is legally relevant through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR), treaty-shopping prohibitions, limitation-on-benefits clauses, controlled-foreign-affiliate/FAPI rules, and the central-management-and-control test for corporate residency. An Ontario mobile utility app company should maintain a real office, Canadian employees, active board minutes, operational decision-making in Canada, and documented transfer pricing allocations to avoid recharacterization as a shell or conduit.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Statements

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under ITA s. 239 with fines not less than 50% and up to 200% of the tax evaded and possible imprisonment; civil gross-negligence penalty under ITA s. 163(2) of 50% of the understated tax; daily compound interest on all unpaid amounts.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who direct, authorize, or acquiesce in tax evasion can be co-charged and personally fined or imprisoned. The corporate veil is pierced in cases of fraud; convictions may also trigger civil restitution, international travel restrictions, and asset-freeze or border-flaggation measures.
