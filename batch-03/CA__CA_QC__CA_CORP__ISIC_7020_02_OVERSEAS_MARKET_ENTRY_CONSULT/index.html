Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — federal tax authority (https://www.canada.ca/en/services/taxes.html); Revenu Québec — Quebec provincial tax authority (https://www.revenuquebec.ca/en/); Business Registration Online (BRO) and the Canada Business Number (BN) portal via CRA; GST/HST, payroll, and corporate income tax accounts are generally opened through the same CRA My Business Account portal, while Quebec-specific QST and corporate tax accounts are administered separately through Revenu Québec's CléNuméro / Mon Dossier d'entreprise.

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity that carries on business in Canada or Quebec must obtain a federal Business Number (BN) from the CRA through Business Registration Online or by using the Incorporation and Business Registration online service. The entity first incorporates or extra-provincially registers under the Canada Business Corporations Act or the Quebec Business Corporations Act, obtains its registration number from Corporations Canada or the Registraire des entreprises du Québec (REQ), then applies to the CRA for a BN with separate program accounts: GST/HST (RT), payroll (RP), corporate income tax (RC), and import/export (RM). For Quebec sales tax, the entity must register for the QST with Revenu Québec and obtain a QST registration number (NEQ-based or separate QST account). A non-resident corporation without a permanent establishment in Canada may still require a GST/HST registration if it supplies taxable supplies in Canada or solicits orders in Canada, subject to the specified exceptions for non-residents; the Canada-Quebec combined TIN procurement typically requires Form RC1, Form LM-1 (Quebec registration), and supporting articles of incorporation, constating documents, and proof of a Canadian agent or registered office.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not maintain a US-style check-the-box regime. Corporations created under the Canada Business Corporations Act or a provincial statute are automatically taxable at the entity level under Part I of the Income Tax Act (Canada) and under the Quebec Taxation Act for Quebec income tax. For federal/provincial coordination, the corporation files a single corporate income tax return under the CRA and, in Quebec, a separate Quebec corporate return (CO-17). Available statutory elections include: (i) the elective deemed-disposition rules for emigration of a corporation from Canada, (ii) the section 216 election for non-resident corporations with Canadian rental or timber royalties income to file a net-basis return, (iii) the Part I.3 and Part IV elections for dividend treatment and refundable tax, (iv) group-related elections for amalgamations and wind-ups under section 88 of the Income Tax Act, and (v) hybrid mismatch adjustments under the hybrid mismatch rules (section 18.2, 18.3, 95(5.1), and the OECD BEPS Action 2 implementation) which deny deductions for hybrid entities or instruments to the extent of double deduction or deduction-without-inclusion outcomes. An eligible corporation may also elect under subsection 89(11) on amalgamations and under section 186 on dividend refunds. There is no elective pass-through regime for a business corporation; limited partnerships and Canadian investment funds are the relevant fiscally transparent vehicles, and an eligible entity may however elect to be treated as a deemed Canadian resident corporation under the treaty tie-breaker rules for dual-resident companies.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 worldwide taxable supplies (before expenses and excluding zero-rated exports) annually for GST/HST; Quebec QST registration threshold is also CAD 30,000 for taxable supplies made in Quebec, but once registered the small-supplier threshold does not apply to certain listed financial services or to a non-resident who solicits orders in Canada; GST/HST registration may be required immediately for non-residents soliciting orders in Canada, regardless of the CAD 30,000 threshold; separately, a non-resident that is not a small supplier and that supplies cross-border digital services to Canadian consumers must register under the simplified GST/HST and QST frameworks for non-resident suppliers of digital services.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

For goods: exported goods are zero-rated under GST/HST and QST if the supplier exports the goods directly and retains evidence of export within the prescribed timeframe; goods supplied to a non-resident for subsequent export and certain transportation services are zero-rated. For services: the consultancy services of an overseas market entry analysis provided to a non-resident client are zero-rated under section 7 of Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act (GST/HST) and Article 28.1 of the Quebec QST regulations, provided the recipient is a non-resident of Canada, the service is not in connection with real property situated in Canada or a performable-in-Canada service that is supplied to an individual present in Canada, and the supply is not an advisory service 'in respect of' Canadian real property or a service rendered to a Canadian-resident GST/HST registrant. For B2B consultancy supplied to a non-resident client that is not registered for GST/HST and not present in Canada, the service is generally zero-rated (or, in Quebec, zero-rated for QST) with input tax credits (ITCs) and Quebec input tax refunds (ITRs) available on related costs. A separate election may be required for financial services but is irrelevant here. Zero-rating claim requires that the supplier has documentary evidence that the recipient is a non-resident and that the service is supplied to the non-resident; the supplier must maintain the evidence for at least six years.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada Border Services Agency form B13A or CAED/AES electronic export declaration, where required)
  • Bill of Lading or air waybill evidencing physical export from Canada or Quebec
  • Commercial invoice showing the non-resident buyer's name and address, complete service description, terms of sale, and the service delivery location
  • Contract or purchase order evidencing the supply to a non-resident recipient
  • Evidence of payment from the non-resident recipient, including SWIFT/Inward Remittance Certificate or Wise B2B settlement statement showing FX conversion and remitter identity
  • Revenu Québec QST registration number (for QST zero-rating substantiation)
  • CR/RC business number and program account (RT/RM) for GST/HST purposes

Canada does not mandate a national real-time fiscal clearance or e-invoicing clearance system; the CRA and Revenu Québec accept electronically issued invoices and data files. Mandatory GST/HST and QST invoicing rules require a supplier to show the supplier's business number and Quebec QST registration number, the date of invoice, the recipient's name and address, a description of the products or services, and the tax amount or a clear indication that the price includes tax. Quebec has implemented a phased mandate for electronic invoicing under the Registraire des entreprises du Québec, with the 'Quebec e-invoicing' framework and the mandatory use of PEPPOL-compliant e-invoices being phased in for large enterprises; however, as of the current policy period, full real-time tax clearance, digital stamping, and mandatory pre-clearance API reporting to CRA or Revenu Québec are not in effect for ordinary B2B consultancy services. Suppliers are instead required to retain full books and records, including electronic records, in Canada or make them available electronically to the CRA and Revenu Québec on request.

The CRA and Revenu Québec may deny zero-rating and reassess uncollected GST/QST plus interest and penalties if the exporter fails to maintain prescribed documents proving export or non-resident status. Penalties include: (i) a minimum 10% penalty (up to 25%) on misstatements or omissions attributable to negligence, carelessness, or wilful default under section 285.1 of the Excise Tax Act, plus a 'gross negligence' penalty of CAD 100 plus 25% of the understated tax under section 280.1; (ii) for knowingly making false statements, a civil penalty not exceeding CAD 25,000 per false statement under section 285.1; (iii) criminal prosecution for tax evasion under section 327 of the Excise Tax Act and section 380 of the Criminal Code with fines up to CAD 200,000 (for summary conviction) and imprisonment up to 5 years, or unlimited fines for indictable offences; (iv) Quebec parallel penalties under sections 105.8, 105.11, and 105.13 of the Quebec Tax Administration Act allowing a 25% penalty for voluntary understatement, and a 26% plus CAD 100 penalty for repeated false statements; (v) retrospective clawback of input tax credits related to the zero-rated supply and reversal of the exporter's net tax calculation for the reporting period; and (vi) interest compounded daily at the prescribed CRA rate (currently approximately 5% for overdue GST/HST, adjusted quarterly) plus Revenu Québec's parallel interest rate. In fraudulent export schemes, limitation periods are extended by 4 years beyond the normal 4-year reassessment window (i.e., up to 8 years) for misrepresentation, fraud, or neglect. The CRA also maintains a 'Dishonest or Fraudulent Conduct' audit program for zero-rated non-resident service providers, and may invoke GAAR to recharacterize artificial planning. For an overseas market entry consultancy, the principal audit risk is inadequate proof that the client is a non-resident and that the service is performed in Canada (from a QST/GST perspective) versus supplied to a non-resident; if the consultant could not substantiate the non-resident recipient, the full 5% GST plus 9.975% QST plus penalties would apply to the invoice gross amount.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a fully liberalized capital and current account regime with no material convertibility restrictions. The Canadian dollar (CAD) floats freely. Profit remittance and dividend repatriation are permitted without exchange control approval, subject only to standard corporate law solvency tests and tax compliance (e.g., withholding tax on dividends under the Income Tax Act). Non-resident investors may freely transfer funds into and out of Canada, but transactions above CAD 10,000 require FINTRAC reporting under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. There is no separate regulatory approval for current account payments such as consulting fees, royalties, or imports, provided the underlying transaction is bona fide.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no mandatory surrender or conversion requirements for export proceeds. Exporters may retain foreign currency receipts in Canada or abroad without repatriation deadlines or conversion ratios. All export receipts must be recorded for customs and tax purposes, and transactions exceeding CAD 10,000 are subject to FINTRAC reporting if they involve cross-border wire transfers. There are no statutory timelines or forced conversion thresholds applied by the Bank of Canada or any other authority.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), with operational oversight by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and anti-money laundering supervision by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

PayPal Fit: High

Feasibility is High for licensed Canadian fintechs and certain foreign EMIs with a Canadian registration under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA). Wise B2B (Wise Canada) operates as an authorized payment service provider and can onboard Quebec corporations remotely for cross-border settlement; however, it does not provide a full current account or CAD clearing chequing facility. PayPal Business and similar EMIs are viable for sub-threshold operational flows but face limitations on high-volume trade settlements and statutory escrow functionality. Legal constraint: unregistered foreign EMIs may not offer payment services to Canadian residents without FINTRAC MSB registration and provincial licensing, unless an exemption applies under the RPAA transitional rules.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

For capital injections from a foreign parent shareholder, compliant routing must use a standard SWIFT MT103 wire with detailed purpose code. Under Canadian guidelines, the purpose description should state 'Subscription for common shares' or 'Contribution to stated capital' in English or French. The correspondent bank must be a designated Canadian clearing member (e.g., RBC, BMO, TD, Scotiabank, CIBC, NBC). Remitting institutions must include the Quebec corporation's name and account number, the final beneficiary is the Capital Escrow Account if applicable, and the sending bank must provide the BIC and intermediary details to facilitate clearing. Verification for FINTRAC requires the originator and beneficiary names, address, and account numbers. For amounts over CAD 100,000, a no-fee EFT or wire may be subject to internal AML review; board resolutions and subscription agreements must be ready.Outbound trade disbursements for consulting and market-entry services must clear via SWIFT MT103 for telegraphic transfers, or via Commercial Letter of Credit (documentary collection) when the transaction involves a bank guarantee. The standard rail for settlement is the Canada-US Dollar clearing via SWIFT (Chip and Fedwire) for USD, or the Canadian Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) for same-day CAD. SWIFT MT103 pays a beneficiary directly and is confirmed by the intermediary; Commercial LCs contain negotiated terms under UCP600. ACH debits via the US/Canadian network are only acceptable for local recurring fees, not cross-border trade. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is mandatory for any non-arm's-length cross-border transaction exceeding CAD 1 million in fair market value in the taxation year. Transactions below that threshold are still subject to the general transfer-pricing rule under section 247. A Canadian corporation with consolidated revenue above CAD 750 million must file Country-by-Country Report on Form T1135 and maintain a Master File and Local File.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not enact a separate Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime comparable to the UAE. Instead, Canadian domestic law applies the statutory General Anti-Avoidance Rule (section 245 of the Income Tax Act), which disallows tax benefits arising from transactions without a genuine commercial purpose or economic substance. In an international context, Canada also enforces treaty anti-abuse principles (MLI Articles 7 and 8) that require a beneficial owner to have substantive income rights and underlying activity. A Quebec consulting entity with no local employees, no office or no decision-making power could have its tax residence or permanent establishment re-characterized, and its intercompany commissions or sub-licensing fees disallowed under transfer-pricing rules.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (Wilful or False Statements)

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under section 239 of the federal Income Tax Act and section 103 of the Quebec Tax Administration Act: fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years for summary conviction or 2 years less a day for indictment. Civil penalty under section 163(2) is 50% of the understated tax plus interest.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participated in the evasion face personal liability for the full amount of tax and penalties, including interest; CRA may impose a lien on personally held assets, issue travel bans, and seek civil restitution.
Shell Company / Artificial Arrangement for Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: GAAR under section 245 of the Income Tax Act applies to re-characterize the transaction, disallow the treaty benefit, assess additional Part XIII tax at 25%, and apply penalties and interest. Quebec may also reassess to deny the treaty benefit and apply Quebec anti-avoidance penalties under the Tax Administration Act.

Director personal liability: Directors and agents who knowingly caused the corporation to be interposed for treaty shopping are jointly and severally liable for the disallowed withholding tax and penalties; if cross-border criminal tax fraud is proven, imprisonment may result.
Transfer-Pricing Penalty for Late or Inadequate Documentation

Statutory sanction: CRA can reassess to adjust income to the arm's-length amount, impose a transfer-pricing penalty equal to 10% of the adjusted gross amount plus interest, and apply a general negligence penalty up to 50% of the tax under-assessed.

Director personal liability: For corporations that are insolvent or dissolved, directors may remain personally liable for unremitted taxes and withholding remittances; heavy sanctions apply as ordinary debt.
Money Laundering / AML (Proceeds of Crime)

Statutory sanction: Federal Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act: for a financial services business, fines up to CAD 1 million for a first offense; for any person, up to CAD 50,000 and/or imprisonment for up to 6 months upon summary conviction. FINTRAC registration and know-your-client compliance are mandatory for certain payment processors.

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate entity may be prosecuted personally for directing participation in AML violations, with restitution, travel bans, prohibition on holding director positions, and up to 5 years of imprisonment.
