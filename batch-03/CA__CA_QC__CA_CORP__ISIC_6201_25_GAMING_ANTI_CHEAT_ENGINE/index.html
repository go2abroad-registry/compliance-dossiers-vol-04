Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (Agence du revenu du Canada) - federal tax authority for GST/HST and federal corporate income tax, official portal: canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html; Revenu Quebec - Quebec provincial tax authority for Quebec Sales Tax (QST) and Quebec provincial corporate income tax, official portal: revenuquebec.ca/en/; the two agencies coordinate registration and exchange taxpayer information for registrants located in Quebec.

E-government filing portal

A foreign- or Canadian-incorporated entity carrying on the gaming anti-cheat middleware business in Quebec obtains its tax identity via CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) or Form RC1: (1) apply for a nine-digit federal Business Number (BN) in the name of the Quebec-registered or extra-provincial corporation; (2) add program account RC-1 for GST/HST and, when applicable, RP-1 for payroll and RM-1 for import/export transactions; (3) register separately with Revenu Quebec for the Quebec Enterprise Number (NEQ) and QST account, or be auto-registered for QST when the corporation has a permanent establishment in Quebec; (4) provide the certificate of incorporation, by-laws, registered office address in Quebec, director identification, and proof of authorized signatory. No minimum share capital is required and no Canadian residency of shareholders is mandated. Once registered, the BN and QST account allow filing via CRA My Business Account and Revenu Quebec online services.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not provide US-style check-the-box or pass-through elections for business corporations; federal and Quebec corporate tax systems tax every business corporation at entity level under Part I of the federal Income Tax Act and the Quebec Taxation Act. Statutory elections relevant to this software exporter include: (i) GST/HST section 156 election to deem taxable supplies between a parent and a closely related corporation (90-percent or greater control) to be at nil consideration; (ii) GST/HST Quick Method election for smaller registrants; (iii) GST/HST annual filing and net-tax threshold elections; (iv) corporate income tax capital dividend election under subsection 83(2) and paid-up capital election under subsection 89(5); (v) for hybrid mismatch arrangements, no elective relief exists and the BEPS Action 2 rule set is implemented mechanically through Income Tax Act sections 18.2 to 18.23 and section 20.2, which deny deductions for hybrid leakage regardless of taxpayer designation. Revenu Quebec harmonizes QST elections with CRA under the intergovernmental single-administration agreement.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST: CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold on annual worldwide taxable supplies; QST: CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold on annual worldwide taxable supplies; a non-resident supplier of digital gaming services to Canadian consumers must register under the simplified GST/QST electronic regime once its taxable supplies to Canadian recipients exceed the same CAD 30,000 threshold; registration is voluntary below the threshold and zero-rated export supplies do not trigger compulsory registration.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies where the anti-cheat middleware software is supplied to a foreign-located game publisher or cloud infrastructure: (a) intangible personal property supplied to a non-resident and not for use in Canada qualifies under Schedule VI, Part II, section 22 of the Excise Tax Act; (b) services supplied to a non-resident, including cloud-hosted middleware APIs, fall under Schedule VI, Part V, section 23 when the service is not rendered to an individual present in Canada and does not relate to real property or tangible personal property situated in Canada; (c) physically exported goods such as development kits, hardware authentication modules, or server appliances qualify under Schedule VI, Part I. The Canadian supplier must document the recipient's non-residency, the foreign place of supply, and the foreign consumption/benefit of the software. For Quebec QST, the same export zero-rating exists under the Quebec Sales Tax Act, and the exporter is entitled to QST input tax refunds plus federal GST input tax credits for expenses attributable to the zero-rated export sales.

Canada does not currently maintain a live fiscal-clearance or digital-stamping mandate for GST/HST or QST invoices; the CRA and Revenu Quebec accept paper, PDF, EDI, or XML invoice records meeting the prescribed content rules (supplier legal name, business number, date, description, tax rate, tax amount, recipient identifiers). Revenu Quebec is transitioning toward mandatory B2B electronic invoicing on the PEPPOL network, with phased obligations projected from September 2027 for large taxpayers (annual turnover above CAD 50 million) and later phases expected to cover SMEs and digital service exporters. No QR-code invoice, API clearance, or real-time pre-approval of invoices is required anywhere in the Canadian GST/QST system.

CRA and Revenu Quebec may reassess disallowed zero-rated export supplies retroactively within four years in the normal audit cycle, extended to eight years where misrepresentation is shown and unlimited where fraud is established. A clawback recovers the full combined 14.975 percent exposure (5 percent GST plus 9.975 percent QST) plus prescribed daily compounded interest from the original reporting deadline. Civil penalties apply under Excise Tax Act section 280 at 25 percent of net tax for false statements and section 285 at 50 percent for gross negligence, with equivalent QST penalties under Quebec's Tax Administration Act. Criminal prosecution under Excise Tax Act section 327 or Quebec tax offence provisions may lead to fines of up to 200 percent of the evaded tax and terms of imprisonment. The exporter must retain all customs, banking, and digital delivery records for six years, and bears the burden of proving the foreign destination, the buyer's non-resident status, and the actual export of the software or service.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible on both the current account and the capital account. There are no exchange-control approvals, no quotas, no surrender obligations, and no central-bank authorization to file for equity injections, dividends, royalties, shareholder-loan repayments, or liquidation proceeds. The only structural frictions are (i) FINTRAC reporting by the financial institution of any cross-border electronic funds transfer of CAD 10,000 or more; (ii) Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (Canada) withholding on non-resident payments — dividends 25% (treaty-reduced to 5% for a US parent owning 10%+), royalties 25% (treaty-reduced to 0–10%), interest 25% on non-arm's-length shareholder loans (treaty-reduced, e.g., 0% under the Canada-US treaty for qualifying arm's-length interest), and arm's-length portfolio interest exempt under domestic law; and (iii) the Investment Canada Act notification for a non-Canadian establishing a new Canadian business with aggregate gross assets exceeding CAD 5M. No Quebec-specific convertibility restriction applies to a software-licensing exporter.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None applicable. Canada does not impose a repatriation timeline, a mandatory conversion ratio, or a holding limitation on export proceeds. The anti-cheat engine's licensing and maintenance revenues invoiced in USD, EUR, CNY, or KRW may be retained indefinitely in foreign-currency bank accounts in Canada or abroad, converted at the company's election, or used directly for outbound trade settlement. The binding obligations are limited to (i) FINTRAC reporting of any cross-border electronic transfer at or above CAD 10,000 in or out of Canada; (ii) the corporate bank's own monitoring of conversion patterns against the declared treasury policy; and (iii) transfer-pricing documentation requiring that the Quebec entity bill non-resident affiliates at arm's-length rates for its software and services.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) — sovereign monetary and currency authority; the Minister of Finance (Department of Finance Canada) holds formal foreign-exchange policy powers under the Currency Act and the Bank of Canada Act; OSFI supervises authorized financial institutions; FINTRAC administers cross-border wire and AML reporting under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA); Revenu Québec administers provincial tax and registration matters. Canada maintains no exchange-control board, licensing, or permit authority.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium-High as a collection gateway (Stripe, Wise Business, Airwallex, Payoneer), but Medium-Low as the primary treasury concentration point. Foreign EMIs are not Canadian Schedule I banks, are not CDIC-insured, and provide payment services in Canada as FINTRAC-registered money services businesses (MSBs). Legally, the Quebec entity may use an EMI to collect low-value B2B licensing fees, but the EMI should be limited to a pre-funded acquisition rail with a daily automated sweep to the primary Schedule I bank account. Holding the full treasury balance at an offshore EMI creates (i) unregistered deposit-taking risk under provincial law, (ii) FINTRAC and sanctions compliance gaps, (iii) no access to Payments Canada Lynx RTGS settlement, and (iv) no eligibility for FX forwards or a derivatives master agreement. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Combined general corporate income tax rate: 26.5% (15.0% federal plus 11.5% Quebec). A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access a 9.0% federal small-business rate on eligible active business income within the applicable business limit; Quebec rates and eligibility rules must be separately satisfied.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The Canadian domestic Part XIII withholding tax rate on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is generally 25%. A treaty may reduce the rate, commonly to 5% for qualifying corporate shareholders meeting ownership conditions and 15% for other portfolio shareholders under many treaties. The Canada-United States treaty commonly provides 5% for qualifying corporate ownership and 15% otherwise, subject to limitation-on-benefits and other conditions.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due within six months after the corporation's fiscal year-end. Any balance is generally payable two months after year-end, or three months after year-end for qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporations meeting the statutory conditions. Quebec Form CO-17 is generally due within six months after the fiscal year-end. Tax installments are due under the applicable monthly or quarterly schedule.

Deadline: · Frequency:

T106 is generally due by the corporation's T2 filing deadline. T1134 generally has a separate filing deadline linked to the taxpayer's tax year. RC4649 is generally due within 12 months after the fiscal year-end of the reporting fiscal year.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose a general monetary threshold before contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is required. The arm's-length requirement applies to cross-border non-arm's-length transactions. Form T106 reporting generally becomes relevant where aggregate transactions with a non-resident related party exceed CAD 1,000,000, subject to statutory exclusions and detailed rules. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to qualifying multinational enterprise groups with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year. Canada has not adopted an OECD-style mandatory Master File and Local File regime using those exact labels; Canadian contemporaneous documentation fulfills the principal local documentation requirement.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate a standalone general Economic Substance Regulation regime equivalent to certain offshore ESR statutes. Canadian tax authorities apply the arm's-length principle, transfer-pricing rules, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule, treaty anti-abuse provisions, beneficial-ownership principles, sham and substance-over-form doctrines, and specific anti-avoidance provisions. A Canadian corporation should maintain real decision-making, personnel, expenditure, technical capability, contractual authority, risk control, intellectual-property functions, and records consistent with its claimed profit allocation.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Return

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act, criminal conviction may result in a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, together with imprisonment for up to two years. Civil reassessment, interest, gross-negligence penalties, and prosecution may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who personally participate in fraudulent conduct may face criminal prosecution, fines, imprisonment, civil recovery, and asset-restraint measures. Ordinary corporate status does not shield personal wrongdoing.
Gross Negligence and Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: The federal gross-negligence penalty is generally 50% of the understated tax or improperly claimed credit, in addition to tax, interest, reassessment, and possible limitation-period extension.

Director personal liability: Personal liability may arise for directors or officers who direct, authorize, or participate in the conduct. The corporation remains liable for the assessment and penalties.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: The CRA may adjust income under the Income Tax Act, deny or recharacterize deductions, impose transfer-pricing penalties generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where statutory conditions are met, charge interest, and apply anti-avoidance rules.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil exposure for breach of fiduciary or statutory duties and may be personally liable where they participated in fraud or knowingly authorized unlawful conduct.
Failure to Remit Non-Resident Withholding

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for the unremitted withholding tax, interest, and penalties. Failure-to-deduct penalties may apply, and repeated or deliberate failures may result in prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable under the Income Tax Act for unremitted source deductions, subject to the statutory due-diligence defence. Personal assets may be pursued after corporate default.
Failure to Remit Payroll and Employment Source Deductions

Statutory sanction: Assessments, penalties, interest, payroll reassessments, and prosecution may apply. CRA may issue director-liability assessments for unremitted source deductions.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted amounts where the corporation failed to remit and the statutory due-diligence defence is unavailable.
GST/HST and Quebec Sales Tax Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Tax, interest, late-filing penalties, failure-to-remit penalties, denial of input tax credits, and audit reassessment may apply. Quebec may impose separate QST assessments and penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors may face statutory liability for certain unremitted amounts, particularly where trust-like sales-tax or source-deduction obligations are involved, subject to applicable due diligence.
Shell Company and Treaty-Conduit Arrangement

Statutory sanction: Benefits may be denied under beneficial-ownership rules, treaty anti-abuse provisions, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule, or principal-purpose tests. Reassessments, withholding tax, interest, penalties, and reporting audits may follow.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur civil and criminal exposure where the structure involves fraud, false records, deliberate evasion, or breach of corporate and fiduciary duties. A shell-company label alone is not an independent Canadian criminal offence.
Corporate Records and Beneficial Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: Corporate-law penalties, regulatory orders, tax-information penalties, account restrictions, and prosecution may apply depending on the statute and conduct.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face fines, disqualification, civil claims, and criminal liability for knowingly false filings or obstruction. Travel restrictions are not an automatic corporate penalty but may arise from criminal process or court orders.
Anti-Money Laundering and Proceeds of Crime

Statutory sanction: The Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act may impose administrative monetary penalties and criminal penalties, including substantial fines and imprisonment. FINTRAC obligations apply only where the entity is a reporting entity or conducts regulated activities; ordinary software development is not automatically a reporting-entity business.

Director personal liability: Individuals who participate in laundering, terrorist financing, obstruction, or reporting evasion may face prosecution, imprisonment, fines, forfeiture, asset restraint, and prohibition orders.
Improper Research and Development or Multimedia Credit Claim

Statutory sanction: Credits may be denied or recovered with interest and penalties. False or grossly negligent claims may attract a 50% penalty and, in fraudulent cases, criminal prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly certify false technical or financial information may face personal civil and criminal exposure.
Electronic Records, Privacy, and Cybersecurity Misconduct

Statutory sanction: Criminal Code, privacy, Quebec privacy, contractual, and regulatory sanctions may apply, including fines, damages, injunctions, breach-notification obligations, and imprisonment for serious offences.

Director personal liability: Individuals may be personally liable for intentional unauthorized access, fraud, data misuse, obstruction, or knowing participation. Corporate indemnification does not protect criminal conduct.
