Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – national tax administration for GST/HST, corporate income tax, and payroll; portal: 'My Business Account' at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services.html. Customs enforcement for export declarations is under the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA).

E-government filing portal

For a Canadian resident business corporation (incorporated federally or in Ontario): after obtaining a certificate of incorporation, the corporation must register with the CRA using the federal business number (BN) registration process, generally via Form RC1 ('Request for a Business Number') or the online 'My Business Account' portal. CRA issues a 9-digit Business Number as the TIN, and a GST/HST program account (RT) is opened once registration is required or requested. Non-resident entities carrying on business in Canada or making taxable supplies in Canada must complete Form RC445 ('Non-Resident Business Registration') or register online; CRA will issue a BN and an RT program account. There is no separate VAT ID distinct from the BN; the BN is the sole statutory tax identifier for GST/HST, corporate tax, and customs purposes.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a check-the-box election or equivalent hybrid-entity classification regime. A Canadian business corporation is a per se taxable entity under the Income Tax Act. No hybrid mismatch elections are available at the entity classification level. Certain rollover and non-recognition elections (e.g., Income Tax Act s. 85, s. 86, s. 88) apply to reorganizations but do not alter the fundamental entity-level tax classification. Non-resident corporations may elect under s. 115 or s. 219 for branch tax purposes, but this is not a check-the-box mechanism.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual worldwide taxable supplies made in Canada during any preceding four consecutive calendar quarters (the 'small supplier' threshold under ETA s. 148). For Ontario, the HST is collected as one composite tax at 13% (5% federal GST + 8% Ontario provincial HST component). Non-resident suppliers making taxable supplies in Canada for the purpose of a commercial activity are also subject to this threshold; registration is mandatory once exceeded, except for suppliers of certain exempt supplies.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of green coffee beans (tangible personal property) may be zero-rated under Item 1 of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act when the goods are exported by the supplier in the course of a sale, or are shipped directly out of Canada by a non-resident purchaser who is not registered for GST/HST, provided the supplier receives proper evidence of export. The supplier must not charge GST/HST on the export sale and may claim input tax credits for related inputs. Key conditions: (i) the goods must actually leave Canada; (ii) the sale must be made to a non-resident who takes ownership or possession outside Canada; (iii) contemporaneous documentary proof of export, such as a customs export declaration, bill of lading, and freight carrier certification; (iv) payment must be received in foreign currency or via an inward remittance, and the exporter must maintain a clear audit trail. Services that are substantially performed outside Canada, or that are associated with exported goods, may also be zero-rated under Schedule VI parts often applicable to international trade.

  • Customs Export Declaration (B13A / CERS electronic export report filed with CBSA)
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill / equivalent carrier document
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank-issued confirmation of foreign currency payment received
  • Commercial invoice / sales contract evidencing the supply to the non-resident purchaser
  • Evidence of the goods' departure from Canada (e.g., carrier certificate, freight forwarding receipt, customs validating stamp)

No mandatory real-time electronic invoicing, fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or API-based tax clearance regime exists at the federal or Ontario provincial level. Canada has adopted the Pan-Canadian PEPPOL e-invoice interoperability standard on a voluntary basis, but there is no legislative e-invoicing or continuous transaction control mandate for GST/HST. GST/HST returns are filed electronically through GST/HST NETFILE or through the CRA's online services. Export declarations must be transmitted electronically to CBSA via the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) at the time of export, which serves as the customs clearance/export reporting mechanism but does not integrate with GST/HST invoicing or fiscal stamping.

CRA may deny a zero-rating claim where the exporter cannot substantiate the export with the required documentation. In such case, the GST/HST is reassessed at the applicable HST rate (13% in Ontario) together with refund clawback, statutory interest, and penalties. Under Excise Tax Act s. 280, any person who knowingly, or in circumstances amounting to gross negligence, makes a false statement on a GST/HST return is liable to a penalty of the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the net tax understated. Additional penalties under s. 285.1 may apply for repeated non-compliance. Fraudulent export zero-rating claims may also lead to prosecution under s. 327 (tax evasion), with fines up to CAD 50,000 and/or imprisonment up to 5 years. CRA's normal reassessment period is four years, extended to seven years where there is neglect or misrepresentation, and with no time limit for fraudulent conduct. Refund claims for zero-rated exports are subject to strict validation, and clawbacks plus arrears interest are applied if documentary requirements are not satisfied.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is freely convertible for both current account and capital account transactions. Canada does not maintain exchange controls, repatriation licensing, or capital account authorization requirements under the Currency Act, Bank Act, or Investment Canada Act. Trade-related payments for green coffee imports and service fees are freely remittable, subject only to sanctions, export/import permit, and anti-money laundering reporting obligations. Profit remittances by a Canadian subsidiary to a non-resident shareholder are similarly unrestricted from an exchange-control perspective, but are subject to Canadian non-resident withholding tax under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (generally 25% unless reduced by an applicable tax treaty), transfer-pricing arm's-length rules, and thin-capitalization limitations. Dividends, interest, royalties, and management fees may be remitted without Bank of Canada approval, but must be supported by appropriate corporate resolutions, audited or financial statements, and tax documentation.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory surrender, repatriation timeframe, or mandatory conversion ratio for export proceeds. Exporters and importers may maintain foreign currency accounts in Canada and may hold, deploy, or convert export earnings freely. For Canadian tax and financial reporting purposes, foreign-currency-denominated receivables, payables, and realized exchange gains or losses must be accounted for in Canadian dollars under the entity's functional currency policy, in accordance with IFRS as adopted in Canada and CRA administrative practice.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); prudential regulation and supervision of federally regulated banks is vested in the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI). Canada has no separate foreign exchange control authority or centralized FX administration.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low to Medium for ancillary payment services, but Low as a primary treasury rail. Offshore digital EMIs are not authorized banks under the Bank Act and generally cannot accept deposits or provide deposit-like account services in Canada without an OSFI-authorized banking licence or an approved foreign bank branch regime. Such EMIs may operate as payment service providers for FX conversion or cross-border transfers, but they cannot replicate the clearing, documentary LC, and regulatory custodial functions of a Schedule I Canadian bank. For a specialty green coffee importer, an offshore EMI wallet is acceptable only for marginal disbursements; the principal liquidity architecture must be maintained through a Canadian-regulated bank.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital injections from the sole foreign shareholder or parent company are routed by SWIFT MT103 from the originating bank to the Canadian operating account through the receiving Canadian bank's correspondent / clearing settlement. The wire instructions must identify the Canadian entity as beneficiary, include the creditor reference or corporate account number, and clearly state the economic purpose in the payment narrative, e.g. 'Equity contribution to CA_CORP' or 'Shareholder loan for working capital'. Canada does not impose mandatory FX purpose codes, but banks require a written source-of-funds declaration, board resolution or shareholder resolution authorizing the transfer, a copy of the intercompany loan agreement if applicable, and, where the amount meets thresholds, the corresponding FINTRAC cross-border wire report. The bank will also verify that the transaction is not prohibited by Canadian sanctions, the United Nations Act, or the Special Economic Measures Act. If the injection constitutes acquisition of control of an Ontario business, the transaction may be notifiable under the Investment Canada Act; a fresh injection into an already incorporated CA_CORP shareholder structure is typically non-notifiable unless it involves a change of ultimate control.Outbound trade disbursements for containerized specialty green coffee purchases use two primary clearing rails: (i) SWIFT MT103 for open-account settlements of pre-shipment deposits, freight charges, and post-shipment balances; and (ii) irrevocable documentary letters of credit issued through the Canadian bank's trade finance desk, transmitted by SWIFT MT700, subject to ICC UCP600. For documentary LC settlements, the payment rail will condition disbursement upon presentation of complying documents, including commercial invoice, certificate of origin, phytosanitary certificate, ocean bill of lading, weight and quality certificate, insurance certificate, and packing list. Incoterms should be aligned with the LC terms (e.g., FOB, CIF, or CFR) to limit discrepancies. Each MT103 instruction should reference the invoice number, container number, and underlying supply contract; all payments must pass the bank's sanctions screening and FATF-based enhanced due diligence on the beneficiary exporter. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% gross under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act on dividends paid or deemed paid by a Canadian corporation to a non-resident of Canada; treaty rates generally reduce this to 5%, 10%, or 15% depending on the jurisdiction and ownership level.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 - the Canada Revenue Agency requires Form T106 (Information Return for Non-Arm's Length Transactions with Non-Residents) when the aggregate fair market value of non-arm's length transactions with a non-resident in a tax year exceeds CAD 1,000,000. Contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation (local file and master file equivalents) must be prepared for all material non-arm's length cross-border transactions irrespective of any minimum threshold, and must be made available to the CRA within three months of a formal written documentation request.

Economic substance scope: Canada and Ontario do not operate a standalone Economic Substance Regulations regime; the general anti-avoidance rule (section 245 of the Income Tax Act) and the OECD/Inclusive Framework substance requirements apply. A commodity-distribution corporation must demonstrate real economic activity in Canada through employees, premises, inventory management, negotiation, logistics, and management decision-making. Substance is assessed by comparing functions performed, assets used, and risks assumed with the transfer-pricing policies, and by ensuring that the Canadian entity is not a mere conduit for foreign shareholders.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Declarations

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction under section 239(1) of the Income Tax Act carries a fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment up to five years on indictment (two years on summary conviction). Civil gross-negligence penalty under section 163(2) is 50% of the understated tax.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable under section 227.1 for unremitted source deductions and unpaid corporate tax; a directing mind who authorizes false returns may be personally prosecuted, with restitution orders, travel bans, and imprisonment applicable upon conviction.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: The CRA may re-determine the transaction at the arm's length amount under section 247(2). A penalty under section 247(3) equal to 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment applies, limited to the lesser of 10% of gross revenue and CAD 5,000,000; assessed tax and interest accrue from the date tax would have been payable.

Director personal liability: A director who authorised a non-arm's length price with actual or constructive knowledge that the price departed from the arm's length standard may be jointly assessed under sections 247(3) and 160; intentional manipulation may lead to criminal tax-evasion charges and director disqualification orders.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: The CRA may disregard the artificial corporation and its transactions under the general anti-avoidance rule (section 245), reattribute income to the true taxpayer, and apply penalties and interest; Ontario courts may pierce the corporate veil where the corporation is a sham, fraud, or abuse of corporate personality.

Director personal liability: Directors and shareholders may be made personally liable for the shell corporation's unpaid taxes and penalties under section 160 where property has been transferred to or from the corporation without adequate consideration; travel bans and asset freezes can be granted in the course of a tax-evasion or money-laundering investigation.
Unreported Withholding / Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: Reassessment at the full 25% Part XIII rate (or higher where treaty benefits are denied) plus arrears interest, delay penalties under section 162(2), and gross-negligence penalties under section 163(2) of 50% of the unwithheld amount. A conduit lacking economic substance is denied treaty relief under anti-treaty-shopping principles.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly fail to withhold are personally liable for the entire unremitted Part XIII amount under section 227.1; deliberate non-withholding may constitute criminal tax evasion, leading to imprisonment, travel bans, and personal restitution orders.
AML / Money Laundering in Cross-Border Payment Rails

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, a corporation may face administrative monetary penalties of up to CAD 1,000,000 per violation; criminal money laundering under section 462.31 of the Criminal Code carries imprisonment up to ten years, and a contravention of a compliance order may attract fines up to CAD 500,000.

Director personal liability: A director who authorises payment with knowledge or wilful blindness that the funds derive from criminal activity may be prosecuted as a participant in money laundering, subject to imprisonment, asset forfeiture, travel bans, and civil liability for the monetary penalties imposed on the corporation.
