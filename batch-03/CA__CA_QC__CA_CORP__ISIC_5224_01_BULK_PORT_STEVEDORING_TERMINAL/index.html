Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital under the QBCA; the board freely fixes the issue price of authorized shares, with consideration paid in cash or property. No escrow account is mandated at incorporation for a stevedoring corporation. However, the applicable Canada Port Authority will require a terminal-operation security (surety bond, irrevocable letter of credit or cash deposit), typically up to 10 per cent of the estimated annual terminal tariff, as a condition precedent to the terminal licence.

Funding & unlock path: Fire safety certification from the applicable municipal fire safety service (service de securite incendie) under the Fire Safety Act (CQLR c. S-3.4) for terminal offices, crane pads and storage sheds; structural load inspection and certification by an engineer licensed under the Building Act (CQLR c. B-1.1) for piers, wharf aprons, conveyor supports and heavy-lift zones; occupational health and safety clearance for longshoring crews under Part II of the Canada Labour Code (R.S.C., 1985, c. L-2) and the Canada Occupational Health and Safety Regulations (SOR/86-304), administered by ESDC and Transport Canada marine safety inspectors, including stevedore certification, personal protective equipment programs, confined-space permits, and CNESST registration for Quebec-based non-port employees.

Paid-in capital rule: No statutory minimum paid-up capital under the QBCA; the board freely fixes the issue price of authorized shares, with consideration paid in cash or property. No escrow account is mandated at incorporation for a stevedoring corporation. However, the applicable Canada Port Authority will require a terminal-operation security (surety bond, irrevocable letter of credit or cash deposit), typically up to 10 per cent of the estimated annual terminal tariff, as a condition precedent to the terminal licence.

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – central federal tax administration (GST/HST, Income Tax) via the Business Registration Online portal (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html); Revenu Québec (RQ) – Quebec Sales Tax (QST) and provincial tax administration (https://www.revenuquebec.ca/en/); Registraire des entreprises du Québec (REQ) – mandatory Quebec Enterprise Number (NEQ) registration for any corporation carrying on business in Quebec (https://www.registreentreprises.gouv.qc.ca/en/); Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) – export reporting and customs declaration clearance.

E-government filing portal

Foreign entity entering Quebec: (1) assess whether a permanent establishment exists under Canada–home-country treaty and whether 'carrying on business in Canada' triggers registration; (2) incorporate or extra-provincially register via Corporations Canada (federal) or REQ (Quebec), and obtain the Quebec Enterprise Number (NEQ); (3) register for the federal Business Number (BN) and GST/HST programme account (RT-0001) using CRA form RC1 or CRA's Business Registration Online; registration is compulsory once worldwide taxable supplies exceed the CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold, but optional and advisable immediately for a zero-rated exporter; (4) register for QST with Revenu Québec via the enterprise number file (QST account, format NEQ + SRxxxxxxx); the QST threshold is also CAD 30,000; (5) for a port terminal operator, complete CBSA importer/exporter programme account (RM) and bonded facility authorization where applicable; (6) designate a Canadian resident compliance contact for CRA/RQ notices; the corporate tax identifier is the 15-character BN combination (9-digit base + RT programme code + reference) used for all GST/HST filings, plus the separate NEQ and QST identifiers for Quebec-specific filings.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — a resident Canadian corporation (federal or provincial) is subject to federal and Quebec income tax on worldwide income; a non-resident corporation is taxable only on Canadian-source income derived from carrying on business through a permanent establishment in Canada (branch-level tax with remittance of branch profits and Part XIII withholding); no US-style pass-through or check-the-box transparency regime exists; the entity is a GST/HST and QST registrant taxed on taxable supplies at the entity level with full input tax credit (ITC) and QST input tax refund (ITR) entitlement on goods and services used in the stevedoring operation; territorial scope is modified worldwide taxation with foreign tax credits for foreign-source income.

Available election forms

No check-the-box election is available under the Income Tax Act (Canada); relevant statutory elections include: (i) section 216(1) ITA election for a non-resident to compute Canadian-source rental/royalty income on a net basis; (ii) treaty-based domestic law election to treat income as not attributable to a permanent establishment, thereby claiming exemption; (iii) GST/HST joint election for closely related corporations and partnerships (for GST25) to treat supplies between them as nil consideration, with the Quebec QST equivalent (LM-18/VD-58); (iv) simplified registration and quick method accounting elections for small suppliers (ETA s. 179 rules); (v) ITA s. 12(1)(x) and s. 18(1)(u) hybrid mismatch rules aligning with OECD BEPS Action 2 to deny deductions for payments giving rise to non-taxable amounts in hybrid entities; (vi) the Quebec option to centralize GST/HST and QST returns with a single designated reporting calendar for large businesses.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — the 'small supplier' threshold computed on worldwide taxable supplies (excluding zero-rated supplies) made in the current calendar quarter and the immediately preceding four calendar quarters (ETA s. 123(1) and Quebec parallel provision); a stevedoring terminal with a permanent establishment, payroll, or fixed place of business in Quebec must register for GST/HST and QST once gross taxable revenue (before zero-rating) exceeds CAD 30,000; non-resident entities without a Canadian establishment may use simplified GST/HST regulatory registration for the sole purpose of collecting tax on imports or claiming input credits of up to CAD 1,000,000 in consideration.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies by the bulk port stevedoring terminal qualify for 0% GST/HST and 0% QST where they fall within: (i) supply of tangible personal property exported in accordance with ETA Sch. VI, Pt. I, with the exporter retaining evidence of export; (ii) supply of services rendered to non-residents where the substantive benefit is consumed wholly outside Canada (ETA Sch. VI, Pt. V, s. 7); (iii) transportation and handling services, including stevedoring, wharfage, and loading/unloading performed in connection with export cargo, provided the service is supplied to a non-resident in respect of goods that will be exported and the service is substantively performed in Canada but directly tied to the export movement; (iv) goods placed in a customs bonded warehouse or transiting through the port terminal under CBSA control; zero-rating entitles the terminal to claim full input tax credits on operating expenses, payroll-related Canadian taxes, wharf improvements, and equipment purchases, and to claim corresponding Quebec ITRs for QST paid on inputs.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A or CBSA electronic GCA/eExport data record containing the 12-digit HS code, gross weight, declared value, exporting carrier identifier, and ultimate foreign destination; marine eManifest pre-load reporting to CBSA no later than 24 hours before vessel departure)
  • Original Negotiable Bill of Lading (or equivalent electronic B/L under the Rotterdam Rules framework) plus mate's receipt and ocean freight invoice evidencing the outbound movement of bulk cargo from the Quebec port (Port of Montréal, Port of Québec, or Sept-Îles)
  • Commercial invoice in foreign currency stating sale terms, cargo description, and the 0% GST/HST/QST notation referencing ETA Sch. VI
  • Inward Remittance Certificate, SWIFT MT103, or bank credit advice evidencing receipt of export proceeds from the non-resident buyer into the operator's Canadian or foreign bank account
  • Stevedoring and terminal service contract, draft survey / weight certificate, tally sheets, vessel turnaround report, and Cargo Handling Agreement evidencing the physical loading operation and the foreign-heading of the exporting vessel

No mandatory real-time electronic invoicing clearance or digital stamping regime exists in Canada comparable to SdI or ZATCA; however, the following mandates apply: (i) GST/HST returns for reporting periods ending on or after January 1, 2024 must be filed electronically through CRA My Business Account or certified software, with a CAD 100–250 penalty for non-electronic filing; (ii) large registrants (taxable supplies exceeding CAD 1.5 million) must remit GST/HST and QST electronically via EFT; (iii) CBSA requires electronic export reporting through the Canada Border Export Declaration (GCA) and, in the marine mode, the eManifest pre-arrival transmission of cargo and vessel data 24 hours before loading for bulk exports; (iv) the PAN-Canadian e-invoice interoperability standard (Peppol-based) exists and is voluntary for B2B; no fiscal API clearance, QR code stamping, or government real-time invoice validation is imposed.

CRA and Revenu Québec may reassess the exporter within four years of the reporting period, with no limitation where the export claim is misrepresented or fraudulently made; the consequence of failing the substantiation bundle is immediate clawback: full GST/HST and QST are assessed at 14.975%, with daily compound prescribed interest (approximately 4–8% annually) running from the original report date; specific penalties under the Excise Tax Act include: (i) failure to register, a CAD 250 penalty plus 5% of net tax; (ii) failure to file, a penalty of 1% of net tax plus 25% for each additional month, minimum CAD 100; (iii) false or incomplete information, CAD 250 per return; (iv) gross negligence penalty equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 25% of the amount that was sought to be avoided; (v) criminal prosecution for knowing participation in false export claims, with summary conviction fines up to CAD 15,000 and imprisonment up to six months, or on indictment fines up to CAD 75,000 and imprisonment up to five years; Revenu Québec mirrors these sanctions under the Tax Administration Act with an additional 25% gross-negligence surcharge and public disclosure of penalties for registered large enterprises; the stevedoring operator's zero-rating position will be scrutinized against operational documentation, terminal reconciliation reports, vessel manifests, and the 180-day foreign-currency receipt cycle during CRA/RQ export verification audits.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is freely convertible for ordinary commercial and financial transactions. Canada generally imposes no exchange controls, capital-import quotas, mandatory conversion of foreign currency, or general restrictions on repatriation of capital, dividends, interest, or sale proceeds. Current-account payments, including trade invoices, freight, port services, and other operating expenses, may ordinarily be settled in foreign currency. Capital-account transactions are also generally permitted, subject to corporate-law authority, lender or shareholder documentation, sanctions, AML/KYC review, tax rules, thin-capitalization and transfer-pricing rules, non-resident withholding tax, beneficial-ownership verification, and reporting under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, the Income Tax Act, the Excise Tax Act, the Investment Canada Act where applicable, and applicable securities or derivatives legislation. Canadian banks may require evidence of source of funds, ultimate beneficial ownership, business purpose, ownership structure, contracts, and tax residency before processing large or unusual transfers. Dividend, interest, royalty, or management-fee remittances to non-residents should be reviewed for Canadian withholding tax, treaty relief, arm's-length pricing, corporate solvency, and required information returns. Quebec entities must also manage provincial tax, language, employment, and port-operational compliance separately from federal banking and tax requirements.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no general statutory requirement to repatriate export proceeds to Canada, surrender foreign-currency export receipts to a bank, convert them into Canadian dollars, or do so within a prescribed statutory period. Export proceeds may generally remain in foreign-currency accounts or be applied directly to permitted commercial obligations, subject to contractual banking terms and tax/accounting records. The corporation should retain export contracts, bills of lading, invoices, customs records, settlement evidence, and foreign-currency ledger records. Export reporting, customs declarations, GST/HST treatment, sanctions screening, and tax reporting remain applicable. There is no mandatory conversion ratio. A bank may nevertheless impose internal documentation, settlement, or transaction-monitoring requirements and may request a commercial invoice, customs reference, export declaration, or explanation of payment purpose.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada. Canada has no general foreign exchange administration comparable to a currency-control authority. The Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) administers AML and anti-terrorist-financing requirements for reporting entities, while the Canada Revenue Agency administers tax reporting, transfer-pricing, withholding, and certain cross-border information obligations.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for supplementary payment and treasury functions; Low as a substitute for a Canadian operating bank where the business requires port-authority payments, payroll, tax remittances, Canadian-dollar settlement, controlled disbursements, borrowing, or local account functionality. An offshore electronic-money institution does not automatically have the legal right to take Canadian deposits, provide deposit-taking services in Canada, operate as a Canadian bank, or conduct regulated payment activities in Canada. The corporation must verify the provider's licensing jurisdiction, safeguarding model, Canadian availability, correspondent-bank arrangements, sanctions controls, chargeback and insolvency protections, foreign-exchange disclosures, data-processing terms, and ability to support Canadian reporting. The Retail Payment Activities Act and related Bank of Canada supervision may apply to payment service providers that perform covered payment functions in Canada, but do not convert an offshore EMI into a Canadian deposit-taking institution. Offshore wallets should not be used to obscure beneficial ownership, bypass sanctions, avoid tax reporting, or route unexplained third-party funds.

Core KYC Rejection Triggers & Defense Dossier

Opaque ownership chain, nominee shareholders, bearer-like arrangements, unexplained trusts, or inability to identify every individual with significant control.

Root cause: Opaque ownership chain, nominee shareholders, bearer-like arrangements, unexplained trusts, or inability to identify every individual with significant control.

Defense evidence: Certificate and articles of incorporation; current corporate profile or Quebec enterprise registry extract; registers of directors, shareholders, and individuals with significant control; group organization chart; certified passports and address evidence for beneficial owners and authorized signatories; shareholder agreements; trust deeds and trustee certificates where relevant; signed beneficial-ownership declaration; legal opinion or corporate secretary certification.
Large inbound capital contribution or shareholder loan inconsistent with stated capital, revenue, or project requirements.

Root cause: Large inbound capital contribution or shareholder loan inconsistent with stated capital, revenue, or project requirements.

Defense evidence: Executed subscription agreement or loan agreement; board and shareholder resolutions; capitalization table; funding-flow diagram; audited or management financial statements; source-of-funds and source-of-wealth evidence; originating bank statements; tax residency certificates; repayment and interest terms; transfer-pricing support; beneficial-owner confirmation; expected-use-of-funds schedule.
High-value or repetitive cross-border payments to unrelated freight, commodity, logistics, port, or intermediary entities.

Root cause: High-value or repetitive cross-border payments to unrelated freight, commodity, logistics, port, or intermediary entities.

Defense evidence: Master service agreements; purchase orders; invoices; bills of lading; terminal and port-service agreements; customs records; counterparty ownership and sanctions screening; commercial rationale; pricing support; delivery or acceptance records; payment approval matrix; reconciliation between invoices, contracts, and bank statements.
Bulk cargo, commodities, maritime counterparties, or jurisdictions associated with sanctions, evasion, corruption, or trade-based money laundering.

Root cause: Bulk cargo, commodities, maritime counterparties, or jurisdictions associated with sanctions, evasion, corruption, or trade-based money laundering.

Defense evidence: Commodity description and end-use statement; vessel, voyage, flag, owner, charterer, and beneficial-owner information; bills of lading; port-call data; sanctions screening records; export and import permits; end-user certificates; restricted-party screening; anti-bribery certifications; compliance escalation record; documented prohibition on sanctioned persons, vessels, and jurisdictions.
Mismatch between NAICS or ISIC activity, stated port-terminal operations, licensing status, and observed payment flows.

Root cause: Mismatch between NAICS or ISIC activity, stated port-terminal operations, licensing status, and observed payment flows.

Defense evidence: Business plan; Quebec and federal corporate registrations; port-authority agreement or terminal concession; stevedoring, occupational-safety, environmental, customs, and other applicable permits; insurance certificates; customer contracts; projected cash-flow model; expected monthly transaction profile; explanation of seasonal and vessel-related payment patterns.
Use of offshore EMIs, virtual-asset platforms, personal accounts, cash-intensive channels, or third-party settlement accounts.

Root cause: Use of offshore EMIs, virtual-asset platforms, personal accounts, cash-intensive channels, or third-party settlement accounts.

Defense evidence: Board-approved treasury policy; list of approved financial institutions; account ownership evidence; payment-chain map; provider licenses and safeguarding terms; documented business rationale; transaction-level invoices; prohibition of personal accounts and unapproved third-party settlement; source-and-destination reconciliation; independent compliance review.
Politically exposed persons, public-port officials, government-linked counterparties, or unusual commissions and facilitation payments.

Root cause: Politically exposed persons, public-port officials, government-linked counterparties, or unusual commissions and facilitation payments.

Defense evidence: PEP and sanctions screening; senior-management approval; beneficial-ownership records; anti-corruption risk assessment; written commission agreement; services evidence; arm's-length pricing; conflict-of-interest declarations; gifts and hospitality register; payment authorization records; investigation and escalation protocol.
Inconsistent tax residency, foreign tax identification numbers, missing GST/HST or Quebec tax registrations, or unexplained permanent-establishment indicators.

Root cause: Inconsistent tax residency, foreign tax identification numbers, missing GST/HST or Quebec tax registrations, or unexplained permanent-establishment indicators.

Defense evidence: CRA business number; GST/HST registration where applicable; Revenu Québec registrations where applicable; tax residency certificates; corporate tax returns; provincial registrations; intercompany agreements; permanent-establishment analysis; transfer-pricing documentation; withholding-tax analysis; authorized tax adviser certification.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Route equity through a Canadian corporate account held in the corporation's legal name using a bank-to-bank wire from the subscribing shareholder or an account demonstrably controlled by that shareholder. Use a clear payment reference such as 'equity subscription under [agreement number]' or 'shareholder loan under [agreement number]'; Canada does not prescribe a universal foreign-exchange purpose-code system for ordinary commercial wires. The Canadian bank should receive the executed subscription or loan agreement, board resolutions, capitalization table, beneficial-ownership information, source-of-funds evidence, originating account details, expected amount and currency, and intended use. The corporation should record the receipt as share capital, contributed surplus, or debt in accordance with the legal instrument and Canadian accounting and tax treatment. Do not combine equity, shareholder loans, trade receipts, and customer funds in one unexplained transfer. Screen all parties, banks, jurisdictions, vessels, and intermediaries for sanctions and AML risk. Convert currency only through the bank or an appropriately regulated dealer, retain FX confirmations, and reconcile the incoming wire to the corporate ledger. Review non-resident withholding, thin-capitalization, transfer-pricing, debt-equity, customs, GST/HST, and foreign-reporting implications before deployment.Use the Canadian operating account or approved foreign-currency account for supplier, freight, insurance, port, equipment, and other trade payments. A bank may process a SWIFT customer credit transfer traditionally evidenced by MT103 data, although payment messaging is increasingly delivered through ISO 20022 formats such as pacs.008; the bank's current format controls the transaction. Include accurate ordering-customer, beneficiary, intermediary-bank, invoice, contract, and payment-purpose information. For documentary trade, use a commercial letter of credit issued or advised through an acceptable bank under the agreed rules, normally UCP 600, with document requirements matched to bills of lading, commercial invoices, packing lists, certificates of origin, inspection certificates, insurance documents, and port or customs records. For open-account trade, use approved counterparties, dual authorization, three-way matching of contract, invoice, and receipt or service evidence, and sanctions screening before release. CAD domestic payments may use Canadian clearing systems, including Lynx for eligible high-value payments and other bank-supported EFT channels; the bank should determine rail eligibility. Maintain records sufficient for CRA, Revenu Québec, customs, audit, FINTRAC inquiries, and transfer-pricing support. Confirm whether withholding tax, GST/HST, non-resident service rules, or import documentation applies before paying foreign service providers.Deliverable CAD/USD and CAD/other-currency forward contracts with a Canadian bank or appropriately regulated dealer, supported by a documented underlying exposure, approved counterparty limits, confirmation, valuation, collateral terms where applicable, and accounting treatment.Non-deliverable forwards for currencies or markets where physical delivery is restricted or commercially impractical, subject to counterparty eligibility, applicable Canadian provincial derivatives rules, trade-reporting or documentation requirements, sanctions screening, and a demonstrable hedging purpose. NDF settlement should be based on an agreed benchmark and should not be used to evade foreign-exchange controls in another jurisdiction.Currency swaps and cross-currency interest-rate swaps for funding or long-dated exposure, documented under an ISDA Master Agreement with appropriate schedule, credit-support terms, collateral arrangements, valuation methodology, termination provisions, and board-approved risk limits.Currency options, collars, and option-based hedges where permitted by the counterparty and applicable provincial securities or derivatives law, with explicit approval of premium risk, knock-in or knock-out features, maximum loss, liquidity, and accounting consequences.Spot foreign-exchange conversions for operational settlement, subject to bank KYC, sanctions screening, transaction records, and segregation of trading authority from payment release authority. The corporation should maintain a written treasury policy prohibiting speculative positions, setting tenor and counterparty limits, requiring exposure documentation, and requiring independent confirmation and periodic mark-to-market review.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium for supplementary payment and treasury functions; Low as a substitute for a Canadian operating bank where the business requires port-authority payments, payroll, tax remittances, Canadian-dollar settlement, controlled disbursements, borrowing, or local account functionality. An offshore electronic-money institution does not automatically have the legal right to take Canadian deposits, provide deposit-taking services in Canada, operate as a Canadian bank, or conduct regulated payment activities in Canada. The corporation must verify the provider's licensing jurisdiction, safeguarding model, Canadian availability, correspondent-bank arrangements, sanctions controls, chargeback and insolvency protections, foreign-exchange disclosures, data-processing terms, and ability to support Canadian reporting. The Retail Payment Activities Act and related Bank of Canada supervision may apply to payment service providers that perform covered payment functions in Canada, but do not convert an offshore EMI into a Canadian deposit-taking institution. Offshore wallets should not be used to obscure beneficial ownership, bypass sanctions, avoid tax reporting, or route unexplained third-party funds.

Turnover: Under the Quebec Business Corporations Act (QBCA), a private non-distributing business corporation is exempt from the mandatory independent audit if all shareholders unanimously consent. A compulsory audit is required for a distributing (publicly listed) corporation or when shareholders holding at least 10% of the voting rights demand an audit. There is no revenue or asset threshold alone that compulsorily triggers an external audit for a private Quebec corporation, although external audit may be contractually required by lenders or port authorities.
