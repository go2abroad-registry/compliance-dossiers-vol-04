Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No minimum paid-up capital is prescribed by Québec law for a limited partnership. Limited partners' contributions are defined in the partnership agreement; funds subscribed must be credited to the partnership's own bank account held in the partnership's name, with third-party escrow required only when a general partner or regulatory authority imposes such condition.

Funding & unlock path: Fire safety certification issued by the Régie du bâtiment du Québec or the local municipality, structural load inspection approved by a licensed engineer, and occupational health and safety clearance from the Commission des normes, de l'équité, de la santé et de la sécurité du travail (CNESST).

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) and Revenu Québec (RQ). Federal portal: CRA Business Registration Online / My Business Account. Quebec portal: Revenu Québec Cliens d'affaires / Mon Dossier d'entreprise.

E-government filing portal

First register the limited partnership in the Quebec Registraire des entreprises to obtain an NEQ (Quebec registration number), including the declaration of limited partnership with the general partner. Then use CRA Business Registration Online or Form RC1 to obtain the 9-digit Business Number (BN) and program accounts, including the GST/HST account. For QST, file the Quebec registration application with Revenu Québec and reference the NEQ and BN. Non-resident LPs must provide a representative for service of process in Canada and identify each general partner. CRA and Revenu Québec issue the federal GST/HST registration number and provincial QST registration number; no separate physical TIN card is issued in Canada, and the BN serves as the statutory tax identifier.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible. Under the Income Tax Act and Quebec Taxation Act, the limited partnership is fiscally transparent for income tax: it files an information return, but tax is paid by the partners on their allocated shares. Non-resident partners are taxable only on Canadian-source income through the partnership. For GST/HST and QST, however, the LP is itself treated as a separate person/registrant and must charge, collect and remit tax on taxable supplies; exported supplies can be zero-rated.

Available election forms

Canada has no US-style check-the-box election and no hybrid-mismatch entity election. A limited partnership's flow-through status is mandatory and derives from law. Permitted elections relating to the LP include ITA s.97(2) (deemed disposition at an agreed amount on contribution of eligible capital property to the partnership), s.98(3)/(5) (partial or complete disposition/continuance of partnership interests), and s.152(4) waiver of normal reassessment rights. Hybrid-mismatch and anti-deferral rules may disallow deductions in respect of amounts allocated through the LP where no corresponding taxable inclusion arises in another jurisdiction.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000. The LP is a small supplier for GST/HST until its taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over the four preceding calendar quarters. Once over threshold, registration is mandatory. Quebec QST small-supplier threshold follows the same CAD 30,000 test.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies to physical exports of action-figure plastic model kits supplied under a sale to a non-resident where possession/property is transferred in Canada and the goods are exported as soon as circumstances permit, under Excise Tax Act Schedule VI, Part V, section 1. Certain services and intangibles supplied to non-residents are also zero-rated under section 7. The LP must retain evidence that the goods left Canada and that the export was not a sham; failure of proof means the supply is taxable at 14.975% in Quebec. QST zero-rating is parallel for qualifying exports. Export sales are not exempt but zero-rated, allowing recovery of input tax credits and input tax refunds on related inputs.

  • CBSA Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A / electronic CAED record)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill
  • Commercial Invoice and Packing List
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / SWIFT payment evidence from non-resident buyer
  • Proof of export, including carrier tracking, terminal receipts and destination customs entry

No real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or mandatory continuous-transaction-control e-invoicing regime exists for Canada or Quebec. CRA and Revenu Québec accept electronic invoices and mandate prescribed invoice content, but pre-clearance of invoices through government APIs is not required. Electronic filing of GST/HST/QST returns is available through CRA NETFILE and Revenu Québec's electronic services.

CRA and Revenu Québec may deny zero-rating, reassess within the normal 4-year limitation period, and may reassess without time limit in cases of misrepresentation, neglect, wilful default, or fraud. Clawbacks of input tax credits/refunds, interest and penalties may be imposed. Gross-negligence penalties of 50% of the amount disallowed can apply. Criminal prosecution for fraudulent export zero-rating can result in fines up to 200% of evaded tax and imprisonment. Records substantiating export, ownership and payment must be kept in Canada at the LP's place of business or such place as the CRA may permit.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada accepts obligations under IMF Article VIII. Both current and capital account transactions are freely convertible without prior approval for capital injections, operational transfers, or profit remittances by a Quebec limited partnership with non-resident partners. Remittance obstacles are exclusively tax-driven: non-resident partners are subject to Canadian withholding tax on distributed ordinary income and certain deemed allocations under subsection 212(5) of the Income Tax Act, with rates reduced by tax treaties. No foreign exchange repatriation restriction or capital controls apply.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender timeline or mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts. Exporters may retain foreign currency balances indefinitely and settle in any chosen currency. Compliance obligations are limited to FINTRAC reporting thresholds under the PCMLTFA: record-keeping and reporting apply to cross-border wire transfers of CAD$10,000 or more, and to large cash or virtual currency transactions.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (currency authority); foreign exchange administration is not centralized because Canada maintains a fully liberalized external payments regime. Supervisory oversight is shared with OSFI (banking regulation), FINTRAC (AML/CFT reporting), and the Department of Finance Canada (policy).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

PayPal Fit: High

Low-to-Medium viability. Overseas non-bank e-money institutions (EMIs) cannot lawfully hold Canadian deposit accounts or provide CAD-denominated clearing without registration as a Money Services Business (MSB) with FINTRAC and, where applicable, provincial licensing. An unregulated offshore EMI may support non-Canadian settlement accounts and FX conversions outside Canada, but cannot be the treasury rail for domestic supplier payments or local distributions. use of a regulated Canadian Schedule I bank remains mandatory for operational banking; a FINTRAC-registered MSB such as PayPal can process customer payments.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

A Canadian limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and does not itself pay federal or Quebec corporate income tax. Income is allocated to partners. For a taxable Canadian corporation, the general combined rate is normally 26.5%: 15% federal and 11.5% Quebec. The combined small-business rate may be approximately 12.2% for an eligible Quebec-controlled Canadian-controlled private corporation: 9% federal and 3.2% Quebec.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The Canadian domestic withholding tax rate on taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is generally 25%. A treaty may reduce the rate, commonly to 5% for qualifying corporate shareholders meeting ownership conditions and 15% for other qualifying portfolio shareholders. An ordinary Canadian limited partnership distribution is not automatically a dividend; its Canadian tax treatment depends on the nature of the underlying amount and the partner's status.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose a general monetary threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is unnecessary. Under Income Tax Act section 247, documentation must generally be prepared by the taxpayer's filing deadline for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions. T106 reporting commonly applies when aggregate reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions exceed CAD 1,000,000. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to a Canadian-resident ultimate parent or surrogate parent of an MNE group meeting the consolidated-revenue threshold of EUR 750,000,000 or the applicable Canadian-dollar equivalent.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone general Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to the regimes in certain offshore jurisdictions. Canadian authorities may nevertheless challenge arrangements under the federal General Anti-Avoidance Rule, the transfer-pricing rules, beneficial-ownership principles, treaty anti-abuse provisions, the principal-purpose test where applicable, sham or mischaracterization doctrines, and the requirement that transactions be legally and commercially supportable. A limited partnership should maintain real decision-making, books and records, Canadian personnel or service capacity where claimed, arm's-length contracts, inventory and manufacturing evidence, banking records, and commercially rational profit allocation.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and false tax reporting

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act section 239 can impose a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment for up to two years, or both. Gross-negligence penalties and arrears interest may apply independently.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, officers, and controlling persons may face criminal prosecution where personal participation or authorization is proven. Civil tax liability generally remains with the taxpayer or partner, but source deductions, GST/HST, and certain other statutory remittances can create personal director or officer liability. Corporate separateness is not a defense to personal fraud or criminal conduct.
Unreported non-resident transactions and transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: Transfer-pricing adjustments may increase Canadian income, deny deductions, impose arrears interest, and trigger transfer-pricing penalties. A penalty of 10% of the adjustment may apply where the statutory conditions are met, including materiality and lack of reasonable efforts. T106 late-filing penalties and gross-negligence penalties may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be personally exposed where they knowingly participated in false reporting, fraud, or obstruction. Ordinary corporate transfer-pricing assessments do not automatically create personal income-tax liability for directors.
Improper withholding and profit repatriation

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for the unremitted withholding, applicable penalties, and arrears interest. Failure to withhold or remit can result in director liability under applicable federal collection provisions and may impair treaty-based reporting positions.

Director personal liability: Directors may be assessed personally for specified unremitted source deductions and other statutory remittances if the legal conditions for director liability are satisfied. Personal exposure can continue after resignation for liabilities arising within the statutory limitation period.
GST/HST and Quebec sales-tax fraud

Statutory sanction: Assessments may include the unremitted tax, interest, gross-negligence penalties, false-statement penalties, and prosecution under applicable tax legislation. Fraudulent tax-credit claims may produce substantial monetary penalties and imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for certain unremitted GST/HST and Quebec source amounts where statutory due-diligence defenses are unavailable. Criminal liability requires evidence of the director's conduct or authorization.
Payroll, source deductions, and employment misclassification

Statutory sanction: The employer may owe unremitted deductions, interest, penalties, reassessments, and employment-insurance or pension-plan amounts. Serious non-compliance may lead to prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable for certain unremitted payroll source deductions and related statutory amounts, subject to statutory defenses and limitation periods.
Shell partnership, sham arrangements, and beneficial-ownership abuse

Statutory sanction: The CRA may recharacterize the arrangement, deny deductions or treaty benefits, apply the General Anti-Avoidance Rule, assess withholding and interest, and impose penalties. Sham transactions and fraud may result in criminal prosecution.

Director personal liability: General partners, promoters, managers, and controlling individuals may face civil restitution, assessment-related liability where statutory rules apply, and criminal liability for fraud or knowing participation. A limited partnership structure does not shield a person from personal wrongdoing.
Anti-money-laundering and proceeds-of-crime violations

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties can be imposed by FINTRAC, with amounts depending on the violation and compliance history. Criminal proceeds-of-crime and money-laundering offences can carry substantial fines, forfeiture, and imprisonment, including imprisonment of up to five years for certain offences and higher penalties for indictable offences under applicable law.

Director personal liability: Individuals who direct, authorize, counsel, facilitate, or knowingly participate may face personal fines, forfeiture, imprisonment, and asset restraint. Directors may also face travel restrictions or passport consequences in connection with criminal proceedings, enforcement orders, or unpaid judgments; a travel ban is not an automatic sanction for ordinary tax arrears.
Customs, import valuation, and product compliance

Statutory sanction: The Canada Border Services Agency may impose duties, taxes, penalties, seizure, forfeiture, reassessment, and prosecution. False customs declarations may also affect GST/HST and income-tax cost-of-goods-sold calculations.

Director personal liability: Individuals involved in deliberate customs fraud may be prosecuted personally. Directors are not automatically liable for ordinary corporate customs debt but may be liable for personal misconduct, statutory offences, or fraudulent conduct.
