Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html; Revenu Québec (Québec provincial tax authority) – revenuequebec.ca. Both administer corporate income tax, GST/HST, QST, payroll deductions, and related registration obligations for a Quebec-based corporation.

E-government filing portal

A foreign-investor entity must (i) incorporate federally under the Canada Business Corporations Act or provincially under the Quebec Business Corporations Act, register with the Registraire des entreprises du Québec, and obtain a Quebec establishment number; (ii) apply to CRA through Business Registration Online for a 9-digit Business Number (BN) with an RM account for corporate income tax; (iii) register for a GST/HST account if taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000; (iv) register for payroll deduction and source-withholding accounts with CRA if the agency employs seafarers or office staff in Canada; (v) register with Revenu Québec for QST, provincial corporate income tax, health-services-fund obligations, and Quebec payroll accounts; (vi) appoint an authorized representative in Canada and file the required Schedule 91 information for non-resident shareholding if applicable.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation – A Quebec-registered Canadian corporation is a taxable entity separate from its shareholders, resident in Canada, and subject to federal Part I tax plus Quebec provincial corporate tax on worldwide income. A branch of a foreign corporation is likewise taxed on Canadian-source business income at the entity level, with an additional federal Part XIV branch tax on after-tax profits.

Available election forms

Canada does not use US-style check-the-box elections or hybrid-mismatch elections for entity classification; all corporations are automatic taxable entities. Available elective mechanisms include: Income Tax Act section 85 rollover election for tax-deferred transfers of qualifying property to a corporation; section 256 amalgamation and artificial-loss restriction elections; section 261 functional-currency reporting election for Canadian corporations; GST/HST group registration election under Excise Tax Act section 156; and the Quick Method election for smaller GST registrants. Revenu Québec allows corresponding elections under the Quebec Taxation Act where federal elections have a provincial equivalent.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

0.14975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies made in Canada (GST/HST small-supplier threshold; QST threshold mirrors the GST threshold for Quebec). Most crew-recruitment agencies exceed this threshold immediately; voluntary registration is available below threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A ship crew recruitment and vessel management agency may claim zero-rated GST/HST and QST treatment when it supplies a service to a non-resident customer (foreign shipowner or foreign manning agency) and the service is not an excepted supply, such as services in respect of Canadian real property, advisory/consulting services consumed in Canada, or services in respect of tangible personal property situated in Canada. Crewing services that support foreign-flag vessels engaged in international commercial voyages, where the foreign customer receives the benefit outside Canada, are generally zero-rated under Excise Tax Act Schedule VI, Part V (supplies to non-residents) and the parallel Quebec QST zero-rating provisions. The exporter must document the non-resident status of the customer, the offshore/international nature of the service, and contemporaneous evidence that the service is not used in Canada.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA Form B13A/B13B or Integrated Import Declaration) where crew stores, ship supplies, or goods are exported alongside crew services
  • Bill of Lading or Sea Waybill for any exported ship chandlery, equipment, or stores
  • Inward remittance certificate or Canadian bank FX credit advice / SWIFT MT103 evidencing receipt of the foreign currency payment
  • Commercial invoice and signed crew management contract with the non-resident shipowner or manning principal
  • Seafarer employment contracts, crew list, MLC 2006 certificates, and evidence of embarkation on foreign-flag vessels
  • Proof of non-resident status of the customer, such as foreign ship registration documents or foreign corporate extract

No real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamp, or API-based fiscal stamping mandate exists under federal or Quebec indirect tax law. CRA accepts electronic invoices in auditable standard formats and requires mandatory electronic filing of GST/HST returns for most registered businesses. Revenu Québec permits electronic invoicing and mandates electronic B2B invoicing for suppliers to certain Quebec public-sector bodies through approved portals; ordinary B2B taxpayer invoices may be issued electronically if retained with complete audit trail.

Fraudulent or unsupported export zero-rating claims are treated as misstatements under the Excise Tax Act and the Quebec Taxation Act. CRA and Revenu Québec may deny the zero rate, reassess the supply as taxable GST/HST and QST, add arrears interest, and issue civil penalties under ETA sections 280 to 285, including penalties of 25% of the wrongly refunded or understated net tax and additional gross-negligence penalties under federal and provincial income tax rules. Knowingly making false export statements can lead to criminal prosecution under ETA section 327(1), fines up to twice the tax evaded, and imprisonment. The normal four-year reassessment period is extended to seven years where misrepresentation or negligence is established; retrospective clawbacks also trigger recovery of input tax credits previously claimed, with interest running from the original due date.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full convertibility under IMF Article VIII. Both current-account and capital-account transactions are freely permitted, including dividend profit remittance and upstream loan repayments. Non-residents may repatriate funds without exchange-approval hurdles, subject to applicable withholding taxes under the Income Tax Act (notably Part XIII on dividends and non-resident service fees) and to documentary proof of source and compliance with the Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act (FATCA) and the Canada-United States Enhanced Cross-Border Information Exchange.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory requirement exists to surrender export proceeds or any mandatory conversion ratio. Export receipts can be retained in a foreign currency at a Canadian chartered bank or an approved subsidiary account, because Canada operates a floating exchange-rate regime with inflation targeting. Reporting obligations are limited to OSFI anti-money-laundering thresholds (e.g., large cash and cross-border wire records), Statistics Canada International Transactions data surveys for transactions exceeding C$1 million per month, and any policy directive under the Special Economic Measures Act applicable to specific sanctioned counterparties.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) with the exchange-equivalent regulatory framework administered by OSFI (Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions) and FINTRAC for cross-border transaction reporting

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Moderate and constrained. Canada does not issue dedicated electronic-money-institution (EMI) licences. Non-bank payment platforms must register as money services businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. Offshore digital EMIs may assist in multi-currency crew payroll disbursement in a shadow-operations role, but they face severely limited viability for holding operational treasury because they cannot open a clearing account at the Bank of Canada nor hold insured Canadian deposit balances. The preferred architecture is a primary Canadian chartered bank account for corporate treasury with an MSB-licensed or OSFI-federally-regulated payroll-lite payment processor for crew card remittances.

Stage 06

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined general corporate income tax rate: 15% federal and 11.5% Quebec. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access a combined small-business rate of approximately 12.2% on eligible active business income within the applicable annual business limit, subject to federal and Quebec eligibility, associated-corporation allocation, passive-income reduction, and other limitations.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident, subject to an applicable tax treaty or other statutory exemption. Common treaty rates may be 5% for qualifying corporate ownership and 15% for other dividends, but the applicable treaty and ownership conditions must be verified.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 and CO-17 are generally due within six months after the corporation's fiscal year-end. Any federal and Quebec corporate tax balance is generally due two months after year-end. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally have a three-month balance-payment period if the statutory conditions are met; the return remains due within six months.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada generally does not prescribe a single monetary threshold that excuses contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation. A T106 information return is generally required for reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents where the aggregate reportable amount exceeds CAD 1,000,000, subject to statutory exclusions and filing rules. The CAD 1,000,000 threshold is not a safe harbor from arm's-length pricing or documentation requirements. Canada has not adopted a standalone OECD-style mandatory Master File and Local File filing regime, but contemporaneous documentation must support material cross-border related-party transactions.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone general Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to the regimes in certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance remains relevant under the arm's-length principle, the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty anti-abuse principles, beneficial-ownership analysis, sham-transaction doctrine, corporate-residence rules, and characterization of services. A ship-crew recruitment agency should maintain Canadian personnel, decision-making, contracts, payroll, operational records, customer management, and risk control proportionate to the income claimed in Canada.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Corporate tax evasion and false reporting

Statutory sanction: Reassessment of tax, arrears interest, gross-negligence penalties generally equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the understated tax or overstated credits, civil tax-evasion penalties, and criminal prosecution. Criminal tax evasion can result in fines of up to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment of up to five years; aggravated cases can attract higher imprisonment exposure under the Income Tax Act.

Director personal liability: Directors may face criminal prosecution, fines, imprisonment, director-liability assessments for unremitted source deductions, and civil recovery. A corporate form does not protect a director who personally participates in fraud or tax evasion.
Failure to remit payroll deductions and source withholdings

Statutory sanction: Assessments for the unremitted amount, prescribed interest, late-remittance penalties, repeated-failure penalties, and possible criminal prosecution. The payer may remain liable even if the amount was not collected from the recipient.

Director personal liability: Directors can be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions where statutory due-diligence defenses are unavailable. Criminal liability may arise for knowing non-remittance or obstruction.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: Adjustment to arm's-length income, transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory conditions are met, interest, and possible reassessment beyond the normal period for misrepresentation or neglect.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil claims, derivative claims, shareholder claims, and personal exposure where they knowingly approve false records, fraudulent tax filings, or unlawful distributions. Ordinary pricing errors do not automatically create personal tax liability.
Treaty abuse and beneficial-ownership failure

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty relief, withholding-tax reassessment, interest, penalties, anti-avoidance adjustments, and possible application of the general anti-avoidance rule or treaty principal-purpose limitations.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be liable for knowingly authorizing false withholding certificates or deliberate under-withholding. The payer corporation remains primarily liable for the tax shortfall.
Unlawful dividends and capital distributions

Statutory sanction: Repayment or recovery of improper distributions, creditor remedies, tax reassessment, deemed-dividend treatment, interest, and penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorize unlawful distributions may be jointly and severally liable to restore amounts, subject to statutory defenses. Fraudulent transfers can lead to civil remedies and criminal consequences.
Money laundering, terrorist financing, or sanctions evasion

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, criminal prosecution, forfeiture, asset freezes, and imprisonment. Proceeds-of-crime and terrorist-financing offenses can carry severe fines and imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be prosecuted for authorization, acquiescence, wilful blindness, or obstruction. Corporate protection does not shield individuals who participate in money laundering or sanctions violations.
Unlicensed maritime recruitment or crewing activity

Statutory sanction: Administrative orders, license suspension or revocation, provincial and federal fines, repayment orders, immigration sanctions, contract invalidation, and criminal prosecution where applicable.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face regulatory prosecution, personal fines, civil claims by recruited workers, and imprisonment for knowing or reckless participation in unlawful placement, document fraud, trafficking, or exploitation.
Payroll misclassification and employment-standard violations

Statutory sanction: Retroactive payroll assessments, source deductions, employer contributions, interest, penalties, wage orders, administrative penalties, and audits by federal or Quebec authorities.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for certain unpaid wages, source deductions, and statutory remittances. Intentional misclassification can also support fraud or tax-evasion proceedings.
Records, books, and beneficial-ownership non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Failure-to-file or failure-to-provide-information penalties, denial of deductions or treaty relief, reassessment based on available information, audit extensions, and prosecution for obstruction.

Director personal liability: Directors may face compliance orders, contempt or obstruction consequences, and personal exposure where records were deliberately destroyed, falsified, or withheld.
