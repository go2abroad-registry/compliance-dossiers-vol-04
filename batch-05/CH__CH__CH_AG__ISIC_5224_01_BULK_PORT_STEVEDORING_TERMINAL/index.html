Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

Swiss AG minimum issued share capital is CHF 100,000. At formation at least CHF 50,000 must be paid up in cash, being 20% of the nominal value on each share and not less than CHF 50,000 in aggregate. The bank executes a blocking confirmation on a licensed Swiss bank account held in the future company's name; no separate state escrow account is required before registration.

Funding & unlock path: Fire safety certification from the cantonal building inspectorate and professional fire brigade, structural load inspection of quay aprons, crane bases and heavy equipment zones, occupational health clearance under the Swiss Accident Insurance Act (UVG), work permits for stevedoring personnel, and harbor security vetting of terminal staff by BAZG and cantonal police.

Paid-in capital rule: Swiss AG minimum issued share capital is CHF 100,000. At formation at least CHF 50,000 must be paid up in cash, being 20% of the nominal value on each share and not less than CHF 50,000 in aggregate. The bank executes a blocking confirmation on a licensed Swiss bank account held in the future company's name; no separate state escrow account is required before registration.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Swiss tax law does not provide a check-the-box system. A Swiss Aktiengesellschaft is inherently subject to entity-level taxation (corporate income tax at Federal, Cantonal, and Municipal levels). Available statutory elections: 1) VAT group election (Mehrwertsteuergruppe) under Art. 21 VAT Act, allowing a Swiss VAT group to be treated as a single taxable person; 2) election under the Swiss participation relief regime for qualifying dividends and capital gains; 3) request for formal ruling on hybrid mismatch treatment or entitlement to treaty benefits. Federal and cantonal levels do not allow consolidated fiscal filing of income tax; each entity is assessed separately.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

8.1%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CHF 100,000 (exclusive of VAT) of annual turnover from taxable supplies in Switzerland; reverse-charge services from abroad are generally not counted for threshold but may trigger registration if received by a Swiss taxable person from a non-Swiss supplier, Art. 34 Swiss VAT Act (as of 2025 threshold).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Stevedoring, cargo handling, and terminal services performed on goods intended for export from Switzerland qualify for 0% VAT (unechte Steuerbefreiung / export zero-rating), provided that all the following conditions are satisfied: (i) the services are directly related to movable goods that are physically transported by a Swiss customs export procedure (Ausfuhrverfahren / export procedure); (ii) the goods are actually exported outside Switzerland and the export is evidenced by a customs export declaration; and (iii) the performance is either supplied at the Swiss port terminal for export or rendered to a non-Swiss recipient, without any nexus with domestic eventual consumption. Services related to the import of goods are not zero-rated, except for goods temporarily under customs supervision. The provider must retain complete export documentation to substantiate zero-rating and recover input VAT.

  • Swiss customs export declaration (Zollausfuhranmeldung / e-dec or ECW) evidencing the export movement and declaring the destination of the goods
  • Waterway Bill of Lading, Rhine bill of lading or equivalent intermodal transport document covering the terminal-to-vessel leg and the export carriage
  • Commercial contract and stevedoring/terminal service agreement confirming the export nature of the cargo handling
  • Inward remittance certificate (bank payment confirmation) or equivalent proof of settlement from the foreign importer/customer
  • Authorized customs officer stamp or electronic export certificate in the Swiss customs system (as required for VAT verification)

Switzerland does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or an authority-approved tax clearance API for VAT transactions. A standard electronic VAT return is submitted via the ePortal (eService eVAT). There is no national mandatory e-invoicing or continuous transaction control. The Swiss QR-bill is a standard payment slip, but it is not a tax clearance instrument. VAT invoices must satisfy Art. 26 VoVAT (VAT Ordinance) irrespective of paper or electronic form.

Swiss tax and customs authorities conduct cross-verification between customs export declarations and VAT accounts, including B/L supporting documents and goods movement status. Unsupported zero-rating is reclassified as taxable supply at the standard rate of 8.1%; the supplier becomes liable for the VAT, default interest (currently around 4.5% per annum, recalculated at ordinary banking rates), and administrative fines under Art. 96-99 of the Swiss VAT Act. Intentional or wilful evasion of Swiss VAT through false export declarations attracts a fine up to three times the tax evaded, retrospective clawback may extend up to 10 years from the end of the year in which the supply occurred, plus criminal proceedings before the Federal Administrative Criminal Court. Stevedoring companies are specifically flagged for export verification of port inventories and Bayle? and any discrepancy is scrutinized against the terminal's electronic stock records and cargo manifest.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Swiss franc is fully and freely convertible. Both the current account and the capital account are liberalized; there are no statutory exchange controls, no restrictions on the purchase or sale of foreign currency, and no prior licensing requirements for cross-border capital transactions. Profit remittances, dividends, repatriation of equity, interest payments, and royalties may be paid abroad without FX approval, subject only to general Swiss tax law, including withholding tax (Verrechnungssteuer) on dividends and interest if applicable, and to commercial documentation requirements under anti-money laundering rules. Treaty-based tax relief or refunds are available to eligible non-residents. No requirement exists to maintain any portion of export earnings in CHF or with a domestic bank.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Swiss law imposes no surrender obligation, no mandatory conversion, and no holding period on export proceeds. Receivables from exports can be held in any convertible currency, freely invested abroad, or repatriated without exchange control authorization. For statistical and balance-of-payments purposes, payments above CHF 15,000 received from abroad and settlement in foreign currency must be reportable to the Swiss National Bank by the account-holding bank, but the exporter is not required to file directly. No forced sale of export currency proceeds to the SNB or a licensed bank exists.

Central bank supervision: Swiss National Bank (SNB), established under the Federal Act on the Swiss National Bank (Nationalbankgesetz, NBG). The SNB is the monetary authority, manages the Swiss franc (CHF), and acts as a lender of last resort; foreign exchange intervention is permitted only for monetary policy purposes. There is no separate foreign exchange control administration; the SNB, in coordination with the Federal Department of Finance (FDF) and State Secretariat for International Financial Affairs (SIF), sets regulatory guidance. Switzerland is not a member of an anti-money laundering regulatory union beyond FATF, but FINMA supervises banks for AML compliance.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Low for primary treasury, Medium as a backup collection/disbursement channel. Swiss licenced fintech companies (FINMA banking-type licence under Art. 1b of the Swiss Banking Act) may offer deposit-taking up to CHF 100 million without full banking licence, but they are mostly retail-oriented. Foreign electronic-money institutions (EMIs) such as Wise and Revolut are not licensed as banks by FINMA; they may operate via Swiss bank partners or hold client funds with licensed banks, but they cannot offer insured corporate accounts or handle capital escrow for a Swiss port terminal company. A Swiss bulk stevedoring terminal should not rely on offshore digital EMIs for statutory capital escrows, LC settlement, or FX hedging; use a regulated Swiss bank as the enduring home.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity or shareholder loan funding must be routed through a licensed Swiss bank using standard SWIFT MT103 or MT202 for interbank transfers. Since Switzerland does not apply foreign exchange controls, the payment purpose is described as 'capital contribution / share capital increase' (Erhöhung des Aktienkapitals) or 'shareholder loan' for loans. The transferring bank's purpose code should correspond to direct investment (for FDI statistics). The receiving Swiss bank must confirm the beneficial owner and the underlying board resolution. For amounts above CHF 10 million (or equivalent), banks generally request the opening of a capital escrow account under a notary's instruction when the capital increase is registered with the commercial registry; the notary is legally required to confirm availability of funds. The payment should be accompanied by a signed board resolution, the capital increase notarization plan, and prospective financial statements. The bank will perform a plausibility check of source of funds before crediting; no SNB authorisation is required. Funds must be available in the currency of accounting; if in EUR or USD, the bank will convert to CHF at commercial FX rate or hold in multi-currency account as per a standing instruction.For business-to-business settlements in stevedoring and port terminal services, the primary clearing rails are: (1) SWIFT MT103 single customer credit transfer for high-value or cross-border payments, especially to foreign vessels, nautical service providers, agents and port authorities; (2) MT202 COV for bank-to-bank cover payments if routed through an intermediary; (3) SEPA Credit Transfer (SCT) for EUR payments within the SEPA area, which is highly efficient for coastal and EU port-service invoices; (4) CH-clearing or CH-EUR for domestic currency clearing, operated by SIX Group. For trade-related disbursements such as bunker fuel procurement, cargo handling equipment purchases, or charter hire, commercial documentary instruments are used: irrevocable at-sight letter of credit (UCP 600), standby letter of credit (ISP98) in favor of the beneficiary, and documentary collection (CAD / D/P) with a Swiss bank as the advising or confirming bank. Payment terms should be net 7/14/30 days depending on disposable liquidity. The Swiss entity should establish an electronic banking mandate allowing two-signatory authorisation for amounts above CHF 250,000, and should use ISO 20022 payment messages where possible to carry structured remittance information, such as invoice number, contract ID, and port terminal reference.FX forward contract (linear OTC contract, deliverable in CHF; no premium paid upfront; standard in Switzerland with 1-month up to 12-month tenors).FX swap (spot leg plus forward leg between CHF and EUR/USD; used for rolling short-term liquidity and cash-flow management without counterparty limit concerns).Cross-currency swap (long-dated, for matching ECB or USD floating rate obligations on terminal infrastructure loans; requires ISDA and CSA documentation).Vanilla FX option (call or put on EUR/USD/CHF; premium paid upfront; permitted for hedging a known overseas capex invoice; mandatory per FINMA derivatives rules if embedded in structured product).NDF (non-deliverable forward) is not necessary for CHF because the CHF is fully convertible with a liquid onshore market; a few domestic banks may restrict NDFs due to unclear legal table but it is not generally illegal. This instrument is listed as technically permissible under Swiss free-market law but ordinarily avoided for treasury straight-through processing.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Swiss federal corporate income tax 8.5% levied on net profit after tax (equivalent to 4.25% effective rate on pre-tax profit); combined federal + cantonal/communal effective rate approximately 11.9% in the Canton of Zug and ranging nationally from about 11.9% to 21.0% depending on canton.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

35% Swiss federal dividend withholding tax ('Verrechnungssteuer') applied at the source to gross cash dividends and all distributions in kind to shareholders of a Swiss AG.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The tax return must be filed within 6 months after the balance-sheet date (e.g., a 31 December year-end requires filing by 30 June of the following year); interim self-assessed quarterly installments are payable throughout the year and the final tax balance becomes due upon issuance of the assessment order (Veranlagungsverfügung); extensions are granted only upon written reasoned request and do not suspend default-interest accrual.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CHF 5,000,000 annual volume of cross-border related-party transactions generally triggers the practical expectation that a Swiss legal entity maintain a full OECD Local File; a group Master File is expected where worldwide consolidated revenue reaches CHF 50,000,000; Country-by-Country Reporting is mandatory for groups with consolidated revenue of at least CHF 900,000,000; contemporaneous arm's-length documentation must nevertheless be maintained for all intercompany transactions regardless of these thresholds.

Economic substance scope: Switzerland does not maintain a statutory Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime comparable to UAE or Caribbean offshore centers; however, the Swiss Federal Direct Tax Act (DBG), the BEPS Multilateral Instrument (MLI) principal purpose test, and the Federal Supreme Court anti-abuse doctrine (including the 141 II 447 substance jurisprudence) require real economic presence: permanent physical office, operative decision-making by the Swiss board, employment of operational and supervisory staff, independent invoicing and bank accounts, and actual execution of stevedoring, cargo-handling, and equipment-management functions in Switzerland; failure to satisfy economic substance voids treaty benefits, recharacterizes income as hidden profit distributions, and may invalidate the canton Zug tax ruling or NID claim.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (Steuerhinterziehung)

Statutory sanction: Under DBG Art. 175, a fine of 25%–100% of the evaded federal tax plus default interest; cantonal fines levied in addition; VAT evasion under the Swiss VAT Act (MWSTG) exposes the company to a fine of up to three times the evaded VAT; aggravated tax evasion involving more than CHF 100,000 of evaded tax per fiscal period is punishable under DBG Art. 176 with imprisonment of up to two years or a monetary penalty, in addition to full repayment, confiscation of hidden assets, and revocation of the Swiss port operating license where applicable.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false tax returns are personally subjected to the same criminal fines and imprisonment exposure as the company; boards cannot hide behind corporate personality where the tax return is knowingly false; Swiss courts pierce the veil and order civil restitution of misappropriated monies under Swiss Code of Obligations Art. 754; travel bans (Ausreisesperre) and passport denial may be imposed by the investigating magistrate to secure collection of evaded taxes and fines.
Shell Company / Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: The Federal Tax Administration denies all DTA relief (dividends, interest, royalties), recharacterizes the artificially retained profit as hidden profit distribution, and reimposes 35% withholding tax on the deemed distribution; additional fines of up to 100% of the reassessed tax, 10-year statute of limitations for undisclosed facts, interest, dissolution proceedings, and blacklisting of the entity by the commercial register for abusive incorporation.

Director personal liability: Directors cannot claim limited liability where the company is considered an artificial sham; they face personal joint and several liability for the full deficient tax, restitution orders, criminal conviction under DBG Art. 176 second paragraph, and judicial travel bans where non-resident board members are under investigation; the corporate veil is pierced where corporate assets are used for private shareholders' benefit.
AML / Money-Laundering Infraction

Statutory sanction: Pursuant to Swiss Criminal Code Art. 305bis, money laundering is punishable by imprisonment of up to three years or a fine of up to CHF 500,000 for gross-negligence complicity; aggravated laundering in connection with a criminal organization carries imprisonment of up to five years; the assets involved are confiscated by the State; FINMA may issue a professional ban against entities acting in the financial-services perimeter; the Stevedoring terminal operating license is revoked upon conviction.

Director personal liability: CEOs, CFOs, and compliance directors are prosecuted personally for willful or grossly negligent conduct; they are excluded from future board service in Switzerland, face immediate travel bans to prevent flight, and remain personally liable for restitution of laundered proceeds under unjust-enrichment principles; medical, familial, and diplomatic privileges provide no shield against extradition on Swiss arrest warrants.
Transfer Pricing Documentation Failure / Artificial Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: The tax administration applies discretionary upward adjustments, denies deductible related-party costs, and reassesses taxable income with default interest; the 10-year assessment period applies for undeclared facts; penalties of up to 40% of the adjusted tax liability can be stacked onto the adjustment, and any disallowed expense or margin is reclassified as a deemed dividend subject to 35% withholding tax; the entire Zug NID and participation-relief claims may be revoked retroactively.

Director personal liability: Directors approving or signing flawed transfer-pricing documentation become personally exposed to fines and imprisonment on an ancillary tax-evasion charge; on audit failure, the Canton Zug fiscal authorities impute willful negligence to the responsible managers, attach personal bank accounts, and impose travel bans until full covered liability is secured; Swiss jurisprudence holds the responsible officer jointly and severally liable for the corporate tax deficiency when the officer signed with knowledge of the underlying TP distortion.
