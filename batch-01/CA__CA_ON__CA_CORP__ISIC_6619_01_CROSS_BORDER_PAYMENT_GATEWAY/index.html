Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – publications and secure portal: My Business Account at canada.ca/en/services/taxes/business, GST/HST NETFILE, and GST/HST Registry System

E-government filing portal

A foreign-incorporated entity or non-resident supplier conducting taxable activities in Ontario/Canada must obtain a CRA Business Number (BN) and a GST/HST program account. Application process: complete Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts), or register online via CRA My Business Account in the 'Register a GST/HST Account' workflow. The applicant must provide: legal name, trade name, Canadian business address or non-resident address, date of first taxable supply in Canada, estimated annual net taxable supplies, corporate identification (federal or provincial articles, foreign registration documents), and particulars of the Canadian agent for service of process. For a federal/provincial Business Corporation, the BN is typically assigned upon incorporation through the applicable Corporate Registry or Companies Online portal. The requirement to register arises once the worldwide non-zero-rated taxable supplies exceed the CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold; registration becomes effective prospectively on the day the application is filed or on the date the person ceases to be a small supplier, as the case may be under the Excise Tax Act (ETA) s. 243.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a US-style 'check-the-box' election; a Canadian Business Corporation is legally and fiscally an entity-level taxable person under Part I of the Income Tax Act (ITA), subject to the rules for ordinary residence, deemed residence, or non-residence. Available elections relevant to cross-border payment gateways include: (i) electing to be registered for GST/HST even if below the small-supplier threshold (ETA s. 240(3)); (ii) financial institutions and non-residents making zero-rated supplies may choose the simplified or specialized rebate/recovery methods under the GST/HST Financial Services (Section 21) Rules; (iii) non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada may elect under ITA s. 216(1) for net-basis taxation on Canadian-source income; (iv) hybrid mismatch and treaty-override choices are constrained by Canada’s anti-hybrid rules (ITA ss. 18.2, 18.3) implementing OECD BEPS Action 2 — such rules do not permit pass-through attribution but may adjust double deduction/no inclusion outcomes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 threshold for a person (including a non-resident) that makes taxable supplies in Canada; if exceeded in any single calendar quarter plus the three preceding quarters, mandatory GST/HST registration applies from the first day of the month following the quarter, excluding zero-rated export supplies for threshold purposes. The same threshold applies for Ontario HST because the provincial component is administered by the CRA.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating for goods is available when tangible personal property is sold for export and possession is transferred to a purchaser who ships/delivers outside Canada, or is exported by the vendor via common carrier to a destination outside Canada; documentary evidence must establish export within the required time period. For cross-border payment gateway services (PSP acquiring/clearing), zero-rating for 'offshore services' applies under ETA Sch. VI, Part VII, s. 23 for supplies of services made to a non-resident recipient (not resident of Canada), provided the service is not attributable to Canadian real estate, not performed in Canada on tangible property to be used in Canada, and not supplied to a non-resident person who is a 'specified financial institution' in respect of the supply. A financial service supply to a non-resident, including payment facilitation, gateway transaction processing, and merchant acquiring, may qualify for 0% if the recipient is a non-resident corporation, bank, insurance company, or trust not deemed resident, and the service is not in respect of a Canadian financial instrument or Canadian bank account. The CRA applies guidance under Interpretation/Technical Bulletin ETA-23 and GST/HST Memo 10-4; the PSP must carry out KYC verification proving the offshore status of the merchant/bank and maintain a business purpose analysis.

  • Commercial Invoice / Pro-Forma Invoice showing supply of services/goods and non-resident purchaser identification

Canada has no mandatory national e-invoicing or real-time fiscal clearance system. No API-driven pre-approval or digital stamping is required for invoices. CRA however imposes mandatory electronic filing (e-file) for GST/HST returns when total annual taxable supplies exceed prescribed thresholds, requires specified remittance by electronic funds transfer once monthly remittances exceed CAD 50,000, and maintains a digital record-keeping mandate — all invoice and export substantiation documents must be produced electronically or in paper form upon CRA request through My Business Account, a Notice of Demand, or during GST/HST audit. The ETA requires books and records to be available for CRA inspection, with penalties for failure to provide.

CRA audit powers permit targeted review of zero-rated export and offshore service claims for at least the normal four-year reassessment period (ETA s. 298), extended to six years for misrepresentation or neglect, and without time limit for fraud. Where a zero-rating claim is denied, the taxpayer is subject to assessment of uncollected GST/HST at the applicable rate (13% in Ontario), plus arrears interest and penalty under ETA s. 280 (failure to provide information/records, minimum CAD 250), and false-statement penalties under ETA s. 273.1 (5% penalty on denied amount plus 1% per month in serious cases, for inaccurate reporting after service of demand) or gross negligence penalties of 50% of the misstatement tax. Retrospective clawback is available for a period of up to six years (or indefinitely if erroneous claim was induced by willful default or negligence), and the CRA may assess a director/officer or principal agent of the PSP where the entity cannot pay. In cross-border payment gateway transactions, CRA requires robust transaction rails, settlement counterparties, and sanctioned electronic files; unsupported zero-rating claims are routinely selected for 'direct transaction verification' and each invoice/credential is audited, with penalties for repeated late or fabricated documentation.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada has a fully convertible currency with no separate exchange control regime. Current account items (trade, services, remittances) and capital account items (FDI, portfolio investment, loans, dividends) may be freely converted into CAD. Profit and dividend remittances are not blocked by FX rules, but non-resident dividend and royalty payments may be subject to Part XIII withholding tax (normally 25% unless reduced by treaty). The only material foreign investment constraint is the Investment Canada Act's review/net benefit test for controlling acquisitions above specified monetary thresholds. Routine foreign investment into a Canadian Business Corporation is permitted without prior FX approval.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory requirement, timeline, or mandatory conversion ratio for export proceeds in Canada. Exporters may hold receipts in foreign currencies, convert them to CAD through any licensed financial institution, or use them directly to settle liabilities. The only reporting obligation is an anti-money-laundering measure: financial institutions must report non-routine large cross-border electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more to FINTRAC; this is not a surrender or conversion rule.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), with foreign exchange and payments supervision shared by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC). Canada maintains no separate foreign exchange administration or exchange control law.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Moderate-High as a treasury and payment routing overlay, but low as a substitute for a Canadian bank account. Offshore digital EMI providers (e.g., Wise, Payoneer, or EU financial-grade EMIs) may offer foreign-currency settlement accounts and SWIFT connectivity, yet they are not licensed to take CAD deposits or operate as a Canadian payment service provider unless they hold the required FINTRAC MSB registration and have registered under Canada's Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) with the Bank of Canada. Cross-border EMIs cannot access Lynx or provide Canadian cheque clearing; a sponsored clearing arrangement with a Schedule I bank or their authorized agent is mandatory for CAD settlements.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Receive funds by SWIFT MT103 direct credit to the Ontario corporation's Schedule I bank settlement account, utilising the Canadian large-value transfer system (Lynx) for CAD wires and, for USD wires, the bank's U.S. Fedwire/CHIPS correspondent. Canada does not impose a government purpose code for inward capital, but the remittance information in MT103 field 70 or the bank's optional narrative should read 'CAPITAL INJECTION' or 'CAP'; the bank will require a directors' resolution corroborating the borrowing or contribution, a source-of-funds declaration from the parent, and, where relevant, confirmation that the Investment Canada Act exemption/threshold is satisfied. The receiving bank should record the transaction in the company's Canadian legal entity ledger for OSFI and FINTRAC large EFT reporting if the wire equals or exceeds CAD 10,000.Cross-border disbursements should be sent by SWIFT MT103 for ordinary treasury payments, documentary letters of credit for trade settlements under ICC UCP600, and MT798 trade-contract messages for documentary issuance. For same-day CAD-clearance treasury payments, use Lynx via the Canadian bank; for USD merchant and supplier settlements, use Fedwire or CHIPS through the corresponding USD account. All outbound payments are permitted without exchange control, but global Canada sanctions, export control, and tax reporting constraints remain live. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Moderate-High as a treasury and payment routing overlay, but low as a substitute for a Canadian bank account. Cross-border EMIs cannot access Lynx or provide Canadian cheque clearing; a sponsored clearing arrangement with a Schedule I bank or their authorized agent is mandatory for CAD settlements.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined federal and Ontario general corporate rate (Federal 15% after statutory 38%, provincial abatement and general rate reduction; Ontario 11.5%).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Canadian Part XIII withholding tax on taxable dividends and deemed dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to non-residents.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate fair-market-value of controlled cross-border transactions per non-resident related party triggers Form T106 reporting and careful contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation; for multinational groups with consolidated revenue over CAD 500,000,000, a CRA Master File (Form RC5101) and Local File (Form RC5102) are expected for BEPS compliance.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not impose the UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations, but the GAAR (ITA s.245), treaty Limitation on Benefits, and common-law substance-over-form apply; a payment gateway must maintain real operational substance in Ontario—employees, office, board meetings, merchant contracts, risk management, and settlement capacity—or CRA can recharacterize flows, deny deductions, and apply Part XIII withholding as if payments were made directly to the ultimate non-resident owner.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Reporting

Statutory sanction: Indictable offense under ITA s.239(1): fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years; civil reassessment plus a 50% gross-negligence penalty under s.163(2) and compounded prescribed interest.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorized false filings can be prosecuted as parties to the offense; a court may pierce the corporate veil, order restitution/disgorgement, impose travel bans pending trial, and imprisonment of up to 5 years.
Shell Company and Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: The general anti-avoidance rule (ITA s.245) allows CRA to disregard the abusive transaction; treaty benefits are denied; Part XIII withholding is recomputed at 25% on outbound payments; transfer-pricing penalty of 10% of net adjustment under s.163(3) may apply; foreign accrual property income rules may attribute income to Canadian shareholders.

Director personal liability: Nominee directors and controlling shareholders who participate in a façade may have the corporate veil lifted for fraud, be jointly responsible for restitution, be restrained from leaving Canada during civil/criminal proceedings, and face imprisonment if the scheme is prosecuted as tax evasion.
AML / Unlicensed Payment Service

Statutory sanction: PCMLTFA administrative monetary penalties can reach CAD 1,000,000 per contravention for an entity; FINTRAC may impose compliance orders, suspend/revoke registration, and public naming; operating without a mandatory license can also lead to Criminal Code money-laundering charges under s.462.31 with imprisonment up to 10 years.

Director personal liability: Senior officers and directors who direct, authorize, or fail to prevent laundering activities are liable as parties to the money-laundering offense; the corporate veil does not protect deliberate participation; courts can order restitution, asset freezing, travel restrictions, and prohibitions on holding corporate office.
Transfer-Pricing Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: CRA may re-determine the arm's-length amount under ITA s.247, impose interest on the underpayment, and assess a transfer-pricing penalty equal to 10% of the net adjustment under s.163(3); late T106 filings are subject to information-return penalties, and increased foreign affiliate reporting penalties (T1134) can be severe.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign or direct misleading intercompany invoices can be implicated in tax evasion, assessed personally for unremitted Part XIII where the payer failed to withhold, made subject to exit controls/travel bans by investigative authorities, and sentenced to imprisonment for up to 5 years in fraud cases.
