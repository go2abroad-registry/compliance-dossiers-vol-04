Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; principal digital portals: CRA My Business Account (MBA) for GST/HST and corporate income tax account management; Business Registration Online (BRO) for the issuance of the Business Number (BN) and program accounts; Represent a Client for authorised tax representatives; GST/HST NETFILE for electronic return filing.

E-government filing portal

For a foreign parent establishing CA_CORP, the Canadian-resident Business Corporation obtains its statutory tax identifier (the 9-digit Business Number, BN) and GST/HST program account (RT0001) as follows: (1) incorporate federally under the Canada Business Corporations Act or provincially under the Ontario Business Corporations Act, and extra-provincially register in Ontario if federally incorporated, obtaining Ontario registration and corporate profile; (2) register with the CRA through Business Registration Online (BRO) or paper Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts), electing the GST/HST account where the entity is not a 'small supplier' (CAD 30,000 threshold) or electing voluntary registration to recover input tax credits on establishment costs; (3) if the entity first opens a GST/HST account as a non-resident applicant (e.g., pre-incorporation foreign joint venture), the CRA non-resident registration process applies and Form RC1 must be filed together with non-resident attestations, and the CRA may require security under ETA s. 240; (4) after incorporation, the Canadian-resident corporation must file form RC1/BRO update to activate its corporate income tax program account (RC0001) for the T2 Corporation Income Tax Return, and the Ontario corporate income tax is administered by the CRA under the federal-provincial tax collection agreement (Ontario Corporations Tax Act); (5) appoint a Canadian resident director or agent for service, maintain a registered office in Ontario, and register the beneficial ownership/control information with Corporations Canada or the Ontario registry under the federal/Ontario corporate transparency legislation; (6) obtain the BN prior to issuing sales invoices, filing returns, or paying GST/HST, and retain the CRA 'Notice of Assessment' of the BN as the definitive TIN evidence.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — CA_CORP, being a Business Corporation incorporated federally or in Ontario, is a separate Canadian-resident corporation subject to entity-level taxation under Part I of the Income Tax Act on worldwide income, plus Ontario corporate income tax (general statutory rate 10% at the provincial level, federal general rate 15%, for a combined headline rate of approximately 25% on active business income; no pass-through eligibility). Qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPCs) may access the Small Business Deduction on the first CAD 500,000 of Canadian active business income. The entity is a distinct taxpayer from its non-resident shareholders and is not eligible for US-style pass-through taxation; it is simultaneously a GST/HST registrant once taxable supplies exceed the CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold or upon voluntary registration.

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a US-style 'check-the-box' entity-classification election; a corporation is taxed at the entity level as of right. The relevant statutory elections and designation mechanisms are: (i) GST/HST group registration election (Excise Tax Act ss. 240, 226.1) permitting related Canadian corporations to file as a single registrant; (ii) closely related corporation election (ETA s. 156) treating taxable supplies between Canadian affiliates as made for nil consideration (with no credit eligibility for the recipient); (iii) functional currency election (Income Tax Act s. 261) allowing a Canadian corporation to compute Canadian tax results in U.S. dollars where its functional currency is USD; (iv) non-calendar taxation year election permitted for Canadian corporations; (v) for a non-resident parent, election under ITA s. 216 to report Canadian-source rental or royalty income on a net basis; (vi) associated-corporations allocation of the Small Business Deduction (ITA s. 256); (vii) hybrid mismatch rules (ITA ss. 18.2, 18.3, 18.4 and 94.2) operate as mandatory disallowance and recharacterisation rules for structured cross-border financing — no elective hybrid-mismatch opt-in is available for a standard Canadian corporation.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — the GST/HST 'small supplier' registration threshold, computed on worldwide taxable supplies (excluding capital property) in the calendar quarter and the preceding four calendar quarters; for non-resident digital-economy suppliers of specified digital goods or services (including SaaS and email-API platforms), mandatory registration is triggered once revenues from supplies to Canadian recipients exceed CAD 30,000 over any 12-month period; voluntary registration is available to non-residents to claim input tax credits, provided the registrant is engaged in a commercial activity in Canada.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods: a GST/HST registrant may zero-rate tangible personal property where the supplier exports the goods and delivers them to a destination outside Canada (Schedule VI, Part I, s. 17) within the statutory delivery window, with the supply documented as an export in the registrant's books. Services (including SaaS, email-API, and newsletter-hosting infrastructure supplied to foreign clients): Schedule VI, Part V, s. 7 zero-rates a supply of a service to a non-resident who is not a GST/HST registrant, provided the service is not rendered to the non-resident while in Canada, does not relate to real property situated in Canada, and is not performed on tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time of performance; Schedule VI, Part V, s. 10 zero-rates supplies of intangible personal property (software, platforms, digital services) to non-resident non-registered recipients where the intangible is not for use in Canada. For the target email-newsletter infrastructure platform serving foreign B2B customers, the supply is generally a zero-rated offshore service/intangible supply; the CRA's place-of-supply rules (Place of Supply (GST/HST) Regulations, SOR/2001-330) also confirm that supplies made to Canadian-resident recipients alone attract HST, while exports are zero-rated. Eligibility requires contemporaneous documentary evidence of the non-resident recipient status and the overseas character of performance, together with normal business records (contracts, IP-usage metrics, server-side platform logs) demonstrating that the service is not consumed in Canada.

  • CBSA Customs Export Declaration — Form B13A (or the electronic Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) filing) for physical goods exports
  • Commercial Invoice and sales contract evidencing the sale to the non-resident party
  • Bill of Lading (ocean/air/truck) or courier waybill showing delivery to the foreign destination
  • Carrier or freight-forwarder certificate of export / dated proof of inter-jurisdictional delivery
  • Non-resident status evidence of the offshore recipient (signed declaration, foreign tax registration certificate, business registry extract, or passport copy for individuals)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (or equivalent SWIFT/MT103 bank credit advice) evidencing receipt of export consideration from the foreign buyer
  • Email-newsletter infrastructure contract, service-level agreement, and platform usage records corroborating the supply of SaaS/email-API services to the offshore client

No real-time fiscal clearance, government API clearance, or digital-stamping mandate is currently in force in Canada. The CRA accepts electronic invoices and retains electronic record-keeping as fully valid; the Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing initiative (CRA participation with Peppol BIS 3.0 and CIUS specifications, operative in pilot/voluntary phases) is an interoperability framework, not a statutory clearance gate. GST/HST registrants must file returns electronically via GST/HST NETFILE, and all books and records must be retained in English or French at the entity's Canadian place of business (or made available to the CRA on demand) for at least six years under ETA s. 286(1); foreign-origin invoice data used for zero-rating must be listed in the registrant's audit trail and be readily convertible to the CRA's electronic records requirements.

Canada imposes no codified export-proceeds repatriation deadline, but the 180-day modelled settlement window reflects CRA audit expectations for foreign-currency instruments (inward remittance evidence) supporting a zero-rating claim. The CRA aggressively audits fraudulent or poorly documented export zero-rating claims. Consequences: (i) clawback — CRA reassesses the supply as taxable at 13% HST (Ontario) where the export documentation bundle is incomplete, late, or internally inconsistent, with arrears interest and late-filing penalties; (ii) false statement penalty — ETA s. 285(1): the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the net tax at issue where a false statement is made knowingly or in circumstances amounting to gross negligence; (iii) tax evasion offence — ETA s. 327: on summary conviction, a fine of up to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and/or imprisonment up to 2 years; on indictment, a fine of up to 200% of the tax and/or imprisonment up to 5 years; (iv) unlimited reassessment window — the normal 4-year GST/HST reassessment period is extended without time limit where a misrepresentation is attributable to neglect, carelessness, wilful default, or fraud (ETA s. 298); (v) integrity measures — the CRA cross-matches CBSA export declarations, bank FX records, and platform usage logs against the registrant's invoices; deliberately fabricated export claims additionally expose directors and officers to prosecution for tax evasion and common-law fraud.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a free-floating, fully convertible currency for both current and capital account transactions. The Canadian dollar (CAD) is freely convertible without exchange controls or prior administrative approval. Profit remittances, dividends, interest, royalties, and service fees may be repatriated freely, subject only to standard corporate law solvency tests, Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) tax withholding compliance (e.g., Part XIII for non-resident recipients), and anti-money-laundering reporting thresholds. No statutory distinction between current and capital account convertibility; however, transactions exceeding CAD 10,000 must be reported to FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. For an email newsletter infrastructure entity, recurring subscription revenue and cross-border API payments are treated as ordinary business revenue and may be cleared through any authorized financial institution.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada does not impose any mandatory surrender or forced conversion of export proceeds. There is no statutory timeline for repatriation of export receipts, and no minimum percentage of foreign currency earnings must be converted into CAD. Exporters are free to retain foreign currency in offshore accounts, provided such holdings are properly disclosed for tax purposes and reported under Canadian tax law, including the Foreign Income Verification Statement (T1135) where aggregate foreign property cost exceeds CAD 100,000. Payments for digital services rendered (e.g., email infrastructure subscription fees) fall under the export of services under the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) Services Trade Restrictiveness Index; no exchange control approval is required.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) as prudential regulator; foreign exchange administration is decentralized and conducted under the Currency Act and the Financial Administration Act, with no dedicated central FX control agency.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility: Medium with structural limitations. Electronic money institutions (EMIs) or international digital banking platforms (e.g., Wise, Airwallex, Currenxie, Relay) are not chartered banks under the Bank Act (Canada) and cannot provide primary deposit-taking services in Canada. They may be used as ancillary payment gateways and currency conversion utilities, but not as the principal operating bank account for a Canadian corporation due to inability to directly clear through the Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) and absence of Canadian deposit insurance (CDIC). The Canada Business Corporations Act and the Ontario Business Corporations Act do not prohibit the use of foreign EMIs, but Canada's anti-money-laundering regime requires reporting of foreign-held accounts and imposes stricter due diligence for cross-border transaction flows. Legal constraint: EMIs cannot issue cheques drawn on Canadian clearing routing numbers, cannot direct debit through the Automated Payment Settlement System (ACSS), and may not provide eligible CDIC coverage. For the ISIC_6201_22 email infrastructure business, a hybrid structure (domestic bank account plus fintech EMI for low-value disbursements to global developers) is viable but the primary treasury account should be at a Schedule I bank.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant wire transfer protocols: Inbound equity capital should be injected from the parent or international shareholder via SWIFT MT103 wire to the Canadian corporate operating or capital escrow account, using the ISO 20022-cleared payment format. The reference field must state the purpose as 'Equity contribution' with the Canadian corporate entity name and the internal share subscription number/promise to contribute reference. No exchange-control approval is required in Canada, but the bank's Anti-Money Laundering and Anti-Terrorist Financing (AML/ATF) compliance will require: (a) the remitter name and full banking details, (b) a source-of-funds declaration on the bank's 'Purpose and Source of Funds' form, and (c) evidence of the corporate resolution authorizing the share subscription. For amounts exceeding CAD 10,000, the bank files a Large Cash Transaction Report (LCTR) only if cash is involved; for wire transfers, the bank performs SWIFT transaction screening against OSFI sanctions and CRA/Global Affairs Canada trade control lists. For funding from a foreign shareholder, the issuer must file the appropriate corporate actions under the OBCA/CBCA and issue share certificates concurrently with the wire settlement. Dividends or loans subsequently distributed to the foreign shareholder are subject to Canadian withholding tax under Part XIII (unless a tax treaty reduces the rate). For cross-border trade settlement, commercial letters of credit (L/C) are permissible but are rarely required for pure digital infrastructure services due to the absence of physical goods. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility: Medium with structural limitations. Electronic money institutions (EMIs) or international digital banking platforms (e.g., Wise, Airwallex, Currenxie, Relay) are not chartered banks under the Bank Act (Canada) and cannot provide primary deposit-taking services in Canada. They may be used as ancillary payment gateways and currency conversion utilities, but not as the principal operating bank account for a Canadian corporation due to inability to directly clear through the Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) and absence of Canadian deposit insurance (CDIC). The Canada Business Corporations Act and the Ontario Business Corporations Act do not prohibit the use of foreign EMIs, but Canada's anti-money-laundering regime requires reporting of foreign-held accounts and imposes stricter due diligence for cross-border transaction flows. Legal constraint: EMIs cannot issue cheques drawn on Canadian clearing routing numbers, cannot direct debit through the Automated Payment Settlement System (ACSS), and may not provide eligible CDIC coverage. For the ISIC_6201_22 email infrastructure business, a hybrid structure (domestic bank account plus fintech EMI for low-value disbursements to global developers) is viable but the primary treasury account should be at a Schedule I bank.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Combined federal and Ontario general corporate rate 26.5% (federal 15% + Ontario 11.5%); reduced to 12.2% on qualifying active business income up to CAD 500,000 (federal 9% + Ontario 3.2%) for Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPCs).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding under ITA subsection 212(2) on dividends paid by a Canadian-resident corporation to non-residents, subject to downward treaty relief (e.g., 5%/15% under the Canada-U.S. Tax Convention for qualified corporate and portfolio shareholders).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate annual value of cross-border non-arm's-length transactions triggering mandatory contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation under ITA subsection 247(4); CRA requires Local File–style functional and comparability data and Master File–style group organization data only for high-value audit cases, but every intercompany agreement must be in writing.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not apply the UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations (ESR), but economic substance is embedded in the OECD-aligned transfer pricing framework, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA s.245), controlled foreign company (FAPI) rules, and the place-of-effective-management (POEM) residence test. A pure shell interposed offshore between Canada and the newsletter platform is vulnerable to GAAR reassessment and CRA disregard.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act subsection 239(1): conviction for tax evasion carries a fine of 50% to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment up to 5 years. CRA civil penalty under subsection 163(2) is the greater of 50% of understated tax or misrepresentations; plus prescribed interest.

Director personal liability: Directors lose the corporate shield for unremitted Part XIII and source-withholding liabilities via ITA subsection 227.1(1), including dividend WHT and payroll; CRA may assess directors personally, place liens on personal assets, enforce travel bans via court-ordered civil writs, and refer fraud to the Public Prosecution Service for criminal charges.
Shell Company and Artificial Interposition

Statutory sanction: GAAR (ITA s.245) allows CRA to disregard the arrangement and re-characterize income in Canada. Penalty under s.245(4) is 10% of the avoided tax amount plus full reassessments; transfer pricing penalty under s.247(10) is 10% of the transfer-pricing adjustment, with no buffer. Foreign reporting penalties under s.162(7) impose CAD 2,500 per year per failure, escalating for repeated non-reporting.

Director personal liability: Directors may be held jointly and severally liable for resulting corporate tax liabilities if they authorized the artificial arrangement, particularly where the corporate vehicle is used as a sham; civil restitution and disgorgement, director travel bars under inheritance? More precisely, CRA can enforce director liability under s.227.1 for unremitted source and dividend WHT, while the Criminal Code s.380 financier liability and fraud provisions extend to key management.
AML and Compliance Misstatement

Statutory sanction: Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act imposes FINTRAC penalties of up to CAD 1,000,000 for administrative violations and CAD 2,000,000 for repeat violations; Criminal Code s.462.31 (money laundering) carries imprisonment up to 10 years. A false T2 declaration is an offense under s.238(1) carrying summary fines and jail.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorize or knowingly participate in generating false documents face personal criminal liability, civil monetary penalties from FINTRAC/CRA, travel bans under judicial supervision, asset freezing orders, and under Ontario corporate statutes (OBCA s.131) loss of the limited-liability protection where fraudulent or dishonest conduct is proven.
