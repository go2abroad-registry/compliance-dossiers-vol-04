Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

No check-the-box election exists in Canadian tax law; a limited partnership is fiscally transparent and income or loss flows through to partners under Income Tax Act s. 96. Partners may choose partnership fiscal periods on a business-purpose basis and may adopt reasonable allocation methods. No hybrid-mismatch election is available; the CRA and Revenu Québec apply domestic anti-avoidance and anti-hybrid rules to deny mismatched tax outcomes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies in a single calendar quarter or over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters; a separate but substantively equivalent registration threshold applies for QST. Non-residents carrying on business in Canada may be required to register even below the small-supplier threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Tangible personal property exported from Canada qualifies for zero-rating under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act where the supplier exports the goods or delivers them to a common carrier, consignee, or customs broker for immediate export. Basic groceries sold domestically are normally zero-rated under Part III, but imported condiments and specialty non-basic items are taxable unless physically exported and documented. Ancillary services supplied with exported goods may also be zero-rated if supplied to a non-resident and all statutory conditions are satisfied.

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (CAED / ACI eManifest export report)
  • Bill of Lading (ocean, air, or rail waybill and carrier terminal receipt)
  • Commercial invoice, sales contract, and purchase order
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / bank foreign-exchange settlement and payment confirmation

No real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or government pre-approval of individual invoices applies in Canada or Quebec. Registrants above the filing threshold must submit GST/HST and QST returns electronically through CRA GST/HST NETFILE and Revenu Québec Cl@R EDI. Canadian and Quebec record-keeping rules require that electronic invoices and retailer point-of-sale records be preserved in a legible and retrievable form, but there is no continuous transaction-control clearance regime.

If the CRA or Revenu Québec disallows export zero-rating, the full GST/HST and QST become payable, with compound interest and reassessment. For fraudulent or grossly negligent claims, the normal reassessment limitation period is suspended and civil penalties apply, including penalties under Excise Tax Act s. 285.1 for false statements, with gross-negligence penalties of up to 50% of the net tax in aggravated cases. Criminal prosecution for tax evasion may also be pursued under the Criminal Code, and partners as well as the partnership can be jointly and severally liable for the reassessed tax.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a fully convertible currency regime for current account transactions under the Bank of Canada Act. Capital account transactions are liberalized with mandatory FINTRAC reporting for inbound/outbound flows exceeding CAD 10,000. Profit remittance for Canadian-resident LPs faces no convertibility barriers; dividends, interest, and capital distributions may be freely repatriated subject only to 25% withholding tax on non-resident recipients and full CRS/FATCA disclosure.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender or mandatory conversion of export proceeds required under Canadian law. Exporters retain foreign currency at will and convert to CAD on market terms. All export receipts exceeding CAD 10,000 trigger FINTRAC suspicious transaction reporting; large foreign currency balances may prompt enhanced due diligence by the bank.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

The limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and is not itself subject to Canadian or Quebec income tax on allocated partnership income. For a taxable Canadian corporation, the combined general corporate income tax rate in Quebec is generally 26.5%: 15% federal and 11.5% Quebec. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may access a combined small-business rate of approximately 12.2% on eligible active business income within the applicable business limit, subject to federal and Quebec reductions and limitations.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The Canadian statutory withholding rate on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is generally 25%. Under the Canada-U.S. treaty, the rate is generally reduced to 15%, or 5% where the corporate beneficial owner satisfies the required direct ownership threshold. Other treaties commonly reduce the rate to 5%, 10%, or 15%, depending on ownership and recipient status. A limited partnership does not ordinarily pay dividends; distributions retain their partnership character and must be analyzed as partnership allocations, returns of capital, interest, royalties, or other amounts.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

Federal Form T5013 is generally due within five months after the end of the partnership fiscal period. Quebec Form TP-600-V is generally due within five months after the end of the partnership fiscal period. Partner slips must be prepared and delivered within the applicable statutory period. A corporate partner generally files its T2 within six months after its taxation year-end. A Canadian-resident individual partner generally files by April 30, or June 15 where the individual or spouse carries on business, although any balance owing is generally payable by April 30.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada requires contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions. Form T106 generally applies where reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions meet the applicable reporting conditions, including the commonly relevant CAD 1 million aggregate threshold, subject to statutory exclusions. Canada does not generally impose a separate mandatory Local File or Master File threshold identical to all OECD jurisdictions. A Canadian entity in a multinational enterprise group may have Country-by-Country reporting obligations where consolidated group revenue is at least EUR 750 million and the relevant filing conditions apply. Quebec has no separate comprehensive transfer-pricing code overriding the federal regime.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone general Economic Substance Regulation regime equivalent to the UAE or certain other jurisdictions. Substance remains relevant under the Income Tax Act through the general anti-avoidance rule, sham and window-dressing principles, beneficial-ownership analysis, treaty anti-abuse rules, transfer-pricing rules, interest limitation rules, foreign-affiliate rules, and the principal-purpose test in applicable treaties. The partnership should maintain real Canadian operations, inventory, employees or contracted operational capacity, premises, decision-making records, banking controls, point-of-sale records, supplier agreements, and commercially supportable functions. A non-resident partner or related service entity should not receive fees or allocations unsupported by personnel, functions, assets, risks, and decision-making.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Unreported Sales and Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Assessment of unpaid income tax, GST/HST, QST, interest, gross-negligence penalties, and possible criminal prosecution. Tax evasion convictions may result in fines of up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment of up to five years under the federal Income Tax Act, with separate GST/HST and Quebec consequences.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, general partners, and persons directing the business may face criminal prosecution, fines, restitution, and imprisonment. Directors may be personally assessed for unremitted source deductions and certain GST/HST amounts where statutory due-diligence defenses fail. Travel restrictions are not an automatic tax penalty but may arise from criminal process, bail conditions, or enforcement orders.
Failure to Remit Payroll Withholdings

Statutory sanction: Employer assessments, arrears interest, late-remittance penalties, gross-negligence penalties, director liability, collection action, and potential prosecution. Repeated or deliberate non-remittance may result in criminal fines and imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for source deductions that remain unpaid during their tenure unless they establish statutory due diligence. The general partner may also face personal exposure under partnership law and agency principles.
GST/HST and QST Fraud

Statutory sanction: Denial of credits, tax reassessment, interest, civil penalties, gross-negligence penalties, and criminal charges. GST/HST and QST fraud can produce significant fines and imprisonment in addition to repayment and collection costs.

Director personal liability: Directors and controlling persons may be assessed for unremitted GST/HST and may face criminal liability where conduct is knowing, wilful, or fraudulent. A limited partner is not automatically liable solely by reason of status, but protection may be compromised by management conduct, guarantees, agency, fraud, or statutory liability.
False Invoices and Non-Genuine Expenses

Statutory sanction: Denial of deductions, reassessment of partnership income, withholding-tax assessments, transfer-pricing adjustments, penalties, interest, and potential Criminal Code or Income Tax Act prosecution for fraud or tax evasion.

Director personal liability: Individuals who authorize, benefit from, or knowingly participate in false invoicing may face fines, restitution, asset seizure, director liability, and imprisonment. Corporate separateness will not protect fraudulent actors.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: Income adjustments to an arm's-length amount, possible transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where statutory conditions are met, denial of deductions, withholding assessments, interest, T106 penalties, and documentation orders.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face civil liability to the entity for approving unsupported arrangements and criminal exposure if records are knowingly falsified. Ordinary transfer-pricing errors do not automatically create imprisonment or travel bans.
Beneficial-Ownership and Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: Reassessment of withholding at 25%, payer liability for shortfalls, interest, penalties, denial of treaty relief, treaty anti-abuse application, and potential prosecution for false statements.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and authorized signatories may incur civil and criminal exposure for knowingly false certifications. Personal liability is fact-dependent and is not created merely by a failed treaty claim made in good faith.
Shell Company and Economic Substance Failure

Statutory sanction: Denial of deductions or treaty benefits, application of the general anti-avoidance rule, beneficial-ownership challenges, transfer-pricing adjustments, withholding assessments, audit escalation, and possible money-laundering or tax-fraud prosecution where concealment or proceeds of crime are involved.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, beneficial owners, and professional facilitators may face fines, restitution, forfeiture, director disqualification, and imprisonment. Travel bans may be imposed only through applicable criminal, bail, immigration, or court processes.
AML, Sanctions, and Beneficial-Ownership Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, suspicious-transaction reporting failures, seizure or forfeiture, sanctions penalties, account restrictions, and criminal prosecution under applicable anti-money-laundering, sanctions, and Criminal Code legislation.

Director personal liability: Responsible directors, officers, compliance personnel, and beneficial owners may face personal fines, forfeiture, criminal records, and imprisonment. A travel ban is not automatic but may result from criminal proceedings or court orders.
Cash-Flow and Profit-Repatriation Misclassification

Statutory sanction: Recharacterization as taxable income, dividend, interest, benefit, or partnership allocation; withholding-tax assessments; interest; penalties; shareholder-benefit or appropriation adjustments; and creditor recovery actions.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners may face claims for improper distributions, breach of fiduciary duty, oppression, fraudulent conveyance, or unpaid trust amounts. Personal exposure can include restitution and asset recovery; imprisonment requires separate criminal conduct.
Partnership Classification and Limited-Liability Failure

Statutory sanction: Reclassification, denial of intended tax treatment, partner-level reassessments, loss of limited-liability protection, civil claims, filing penalties, and potential dissolution or court remedies under Quebec partnership law.

Director personal liability: A limited partnership has a general partner rather than directors as its primary managing person. The general partner generally bears unlimited liability for partnership obligations. Limited partners may incur broader liability if they act as managers, give misleading representations, guarantee obligations, commit fraud, or fall within a statutory exception.
Record Retention and Books-and-Records Failure

Statutory sanction: Extended audit exposure, denial of unsupported deductions or credits, compliance orders, administrative penalties, adverse evidentiary inferences, and prosecution where destruction or falsification is deliberate.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, general partners, and designated records custodians may face penalties or criminal liability for knowingly destroying, falsifying, or concealing records. Personal liability is not normally imposed for an innocent clerical deficiency.
