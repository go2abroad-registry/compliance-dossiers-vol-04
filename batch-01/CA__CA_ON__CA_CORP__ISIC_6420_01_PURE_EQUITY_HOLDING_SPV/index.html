Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) is the single federal tax authority for corporate income tax, GST/HST, and payroll; self-service portal: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/e-services-businesses/business-account.html) and Business Registration Online (BRO). Ontario Ministry of Finance and the Ontario Business Registry (https://www.ontario.ca/page/ontario-business-registry) are the provincial counterpart for Ontario incorporation records, Ontario corporate tax (administered federally by the CRA under a single-administration tax collection agreement), and Employer Health Tax (EHT). Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) governs customs/export reporting infrastructure (Canadian Export Reporting System, CERS) relevant to export substantiation.

E-government filing portal

Procedure for a foreign-owned Business Corporation (federal or Ontario) to obtain its statutory tax identity: (1) Incorporate under the Canada Business Corporations Act or Ontario Business Corporations Act; obtain the Ontario/federal corporate registration number from the Ontario Business Registry or Corporations Canada. (2) Register with the CRA through Business Registration Online (BRO) to obtain the 9-digit Business Number (BN), the universal tax identifier under which the CRA assigns account-type suffixes: corporate income tax account (RP0001) is mandatory for every resident corporation; GST/HST account (RT0001) is obligatory only if taxable supplies exceed the CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold, otherwise voluntary; payroll account (RP0001) is required if the SPV compensates directors/employees; import/export account (RM0001) is required if the SPV imports or exports commercial goods. (3) For a non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada or earning Canadian-source income, register via Form RC113 (being a non-resident) and obtain a CRA non-resident account; consider a Section 116 income tax clearance certificate for future dispositions of taxable Canadian property. (4) For Ontario, the CRA collects Ontario corporate income tax through the combined federal-provincial registration; separately register for EHT with the Ontario Ministry of Finance only if the SPV has payroll in Ontario. (5) A pure equity holding SPV with only passive dividend inflows and no taxable supplies has minimal registration obligations: the mandatory account is the corporate income tax account; GST/HST registration is optional and typically inadvisable as it restricts input tax credit recovery on exempt financial services; no provincial sales tax registration applies in Ontario because HST is fully federally administered.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation / Territorial_Scope – A Canadian-incorporated Business Corporation is a separate legal taxpayer subject to federal Part I income tax and Ontario corporate income tax at the entity level on its worldwide income if resident in Canada (taxation on worldwide income with foreign tax credits under section 126); a non-resident corporation is taxable only on Canadian-source income (section 115) and may elect to file on a Canadian branch basis. No pass-through or check-the-box election exists in Canadian law; the pure equity holding SPV is a taxable entity whose dividends received from taxable Canadian corporations are fully deductible under the intercorporate dividend deduction (section 112) and dividends from foreign affiliates may be deductible under section 113 (exempt-surplus basket), making the HoldCo a conduit for tax-effective dividend repatriation. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation / Territorial_Scope – A Canadian-incorporated Business Corporation is a separate legal taxpayer subject to federal Part I income tax and Ontario corporate income tax at the entity level on its worldwide income if resident in Canada (taxation on worldwide income with foreign tax credits under section 126); a non-resident corporation is taxable only on Canadian-source income (section 115) and may elect to file on a Canadian branch basis. No pass-through or check-the-box election exists in Canadian law; the pure equity holding SPV is a taxable entity whose dividends received from taxable Canadian corporations are fully deductible under the intercorporate dividend deduction (section 112) and dividends from foreign affiliates may be deductible under section 113 (exempt-surplus basket), making the HoldCo a conduit for tax-effective dividend repatriation. Canada applies no tax-exempt zones; the SPV cannot be a tax-exempt entity except as a non-profit or government-owned corporation, which is not available for a commercial holding structure.

Available election forms

Canada does not recognize U.S.-style check-the-box entity classification; classification is determined by corporate law and tax treaty residence. Statutory elections available to an equity-holding SPV include: (1) section 85 rollover election to transfer property (including shares) into the corporation on a tax-deferred basis at elected amounts between cost and fair market value; (2) section 88(2) plan-of-arrangement or wind-up rollover election for subsidiary amalgamation/wind-up; (3) section 93 foreign affiliate election to deem additional dividends on a disposition of foreign affiliate shares, converting capital gains into deductible dividends; (4) section 83(2) capital dividend account election to distribute tax-free capital dividends (relevant if the SPV receives life insurance proceeds or capital gains eligible to the capital dividend account); (5) section 114/115 election regimes for non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada; (6) voluntary GST/HST registration election. Hybrid mismatch: Canada enacted BEPS Action 2 hybrid mismatch denial rules effective for payments made after July 1, 2022 (Income Tax Act sections 18.4 and 18.5), disallowing deductions on hybrid instruments, hybrid transfers, deductible hybrid distributions and dual-inclusion outcomes; a master holding SPV should documentation-test intra-group financing, preferred share structures and back-to-back arrangements to avoid result-8 deduction disallowances and to preserve the intercorporate dividend deduction integrity.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

Small-supplier threshold: CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies (excluding exempt supplies, incidental supplies and certain financial services) per calendar quarter and cumulatively over the preceding four calendar quarters triggers mandatory registration. A pure equity holding SPV receives dividends and capital gains, which are not consideration for a supply (exempt financial services or non-supplies), so no compulsory GST/HST registration arises unless the SPV makes taxable supplies exceeding CAD 30,000 (e.g., management fees, administrative recharge services to subsidiaries, interest on intercompany loan facilities if not an exempt financial service). Non-resident suppliers of specified digital services and short-term accommodation have no registration threshold under the simplified non-resident GST/HST regime, which is not typical for an equity holding SPV but is noted for completeness.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A registered pure equity holding SPV may claim 0% GST/HST on: (a) goods exported from Canada by the supplier, provided the goods are removed from Canada as soon as is reasonable (normally within 60 days) after the sale, are not further processed in Canada, and the supplier retains documentary proof of export under Schedule VI, Part V of the Excise Tax Act; (b) services supplied to a non-resident person that are not rendered in respect of real or tangible personal property situated in Canada and not for consumption/use in Canada (Schedule VI, Part V, section 7), e.g., group management and administrative services recharged to foreign subsidiaries; (c) financial services are NOT zero-rated – dividends, interest and capital gains from shareholding are exempt supplies or non-supplies and never invoke the zero-rating mechanism toward input tax credit recovery. The zero-rating eligibility is conditioned on the registrant having a GST/HST account, issuing a zero-rated invoice with the BN/RT prefix, receiving consideration, and maintaining contemporaneous export documentation. Pure passive dividend receipt cannot itself produce an input tax credit or refund.

  • Commercial Export Declaration (Form B13A or e-customs B13A) transmitted through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) to the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA), including 10-digit HS tariff classification, customs entry number, export/import client identifier and original receipt indicator
  • Bill of Lading / air waybill / truck carrier manifest / courier waybill evidencing physical movement of goods from Canada to a foreign destination, with shipper, consignee, port of exit, and dated carrier signature
  • Sales contract and commercial invoice bearing the zero-rating reference (Excise Tax Act Schedule VI, Part V) and the SPV's GST/HST registration number
  • Inward Remittance Certificate, foreign exchange brokerage confirmation, or bank transfer advice evidencing receipt of export proceeds in the invoiced currency and amount
  • ETA section 286 records: purchase orders, insurance certificates, customs correspondence, packing lists, and foreign non-resident status evidence (if claiming zero-rated services)

Canada does not impose a mandatory real-time electronic fiscal clearance, digital stamping, invoice pre-authorization, or government-side e-invoicing approval system comparable to Latin American or southern European regimes. The CRA permits electronic invoices in any reliable format provided the registrant retains records satisfying Excise Tax Act section 286 (auditability, retrievability, six-year retention). The CRA supports voluntary business-to-government e-invoicing through the Pan-Canadian Trust Framework and publishes PEPPOL PINT/UBL technical specifications for B2B interoperability. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically through GST/HST NETFILE or approved third-party EDI/API software; CRA My Business Account is the transactional portal for assessments, adjustments and instalments. Ontario has no separate provincial e-invoice clearance mechanism because HST is federally collected. Selected listed financial institutions (SLFIs) – relevant only if the SPV is, or holds 10% or more of, a bank, trust, insurer or other listed financial institution under the SLFI attribution regime effective July 1, 2021 (and the December 2023/2024 draft expansion of the financial-institution definition to certain significantly-holding corporations) – must file annual GST/HST returns computing interprovincial HST attribution using the special attribution method (SAM) formula, file by the SLFI due dates, and maintain attribution data in electronic format; a non-financial pure equity HoldCo is outside this regime.

CRA may reassess GST/HST returns within four years (extended to seven years for misrepresentation or neglect; unlimited in fraud cases) and claw back zero-rating benefits and input tax credits where export documentation is incomplete or inconsistent. Penalties under Excise Tax Act subsection 285(1) for false statements or omissions: CAD 250 plus 25% of the net tax understated or refund overstated where the person acted knowingly or under circumstances amounting to gross negligence; concurrent Income Tax Act subsection 163(2) gross-negligence penalties of 50% of understated tax may apply on related adjustments to intercompany income. Willful evasion is a criminal offense under ETA section 327 punishable on summary conviction by a fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and/or up to two years' imprisonment, and on indictment by a fine of 50%–200% of the tax sought to be evaded and/or up to five years' imprisonment. Arrears interest compounds daily at the CRA prescribed rate plus any applicable provincial-FRIL component; retroactive clawbacks may also trigger Part IV tax reassessment and transfer-pricing audits under ITA section 247(2) if export proceeds are not repatriated within commercially reasonable terms (CRA generally applies a 180-day benchmark for related-party export payment cycles, with documented extension only where transfer-pricing terms are arm's length). CRA/CBSA may conduct verifications through the post-audit verification program, demand proof of physical export, and issue Form GST/RC 163 letters; failure to substantiate results in reversal to 13% HST (Ontario) or 5% GST plus interest and penalties.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility for current and capital account items. No exchange-control approvals are required for inward equity, intra-group loans, dividend repatriation, or liquidation proceeds. Profit remittance hurdles are fiscal and corporate only: (1) OBCA s. 130 solvency test for dividend declarations; (2) Part XIII withholding under the Income Tax Act (25% statutory, treaty-reduced, e.g., Canada-US treaty permits 5% for a non-resident parent owning at least 10% of voting shares); (3) sanctions screening under the Special Economic Measures Act and the United Nations Act.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory surrender timeline, mandatory conversion ratio, or repatriation window for export receipts. A pure equity holding SPV has no export proceeds; residual compliance consists of FINTRAC EFT reporting for wire transfers of CAD 10,000 or more and Statistics Canada balance-of-payments surveys for non-resident direct investment.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); no separate foreign-exchange administration exists. The Foreign Exchange Control Act was suspended in 1951 and repealed in 1992. Functional oversight is split: OSFI supervises banks offering FX products, FINTRAC enforces AML/EFT reporting, and the Department of Finance Canada administers economic sanctions.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium. FINTRAC-registered corporate EMIs are lawful money-service businesses under the PCMLTFA but cannot take redeemable deposits or conduct 'banking business' without a Schedule I bank licence under the Bank Act (s. 541). They are viable as secondary pre-funding corridors for small recurring disbursements but unacceptable as the sole capital-escrow rail due to absence of CDIC deposit insurance, higher counterparty event risk, and narrower acceptance of source-of-wealth documentation for a passive holdco.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: There is no formal CAD-denominated Local File/Master File threshold in Canadian domestic law; however, section 247 of the Income Tax Act and the CRA's Information Circular IC87-2R require contemporaneous documentation for all non-arm's-length transactions with related non-residents where the aggregate fair market value of the reportable transactions exceeds CAD 1,000,000 for a single related party in a fiscal year; additionally, Form T106 must be filed where the aggregate value of all specified transactions with all non-arm's-length non-residents surpasses CAD 1,000,000 annually, and the larger of the CRA 'reasonable efforts' standard requires documentation irrespective of size where the taxpayer claims transfer-pricing relief.

Economic substance scope: Canada has not adopted a standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime equivalent to the UAE or offshore financial centres. Instead, a pure equity holding SPV claiming treaty or domestic law advantages is assessed under the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (section 245 of the Income Tax Act), the 'substance versus form' doctrine, and CRA's administrative practice under the OECD Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS) Action 6. Indicators of unacceptable economic substance include: absence of Canadian resident directors, meetings held entirely outside Canada, management functions exercised abroad, lack of local bank accounts or accounting records, conduits used to round-trip funds to an offshore parent, and no commercial rationale other than the realization of a tax benefit. A transaction found to be an 'avoidance transaction' can trigger full reassessment and a 50% penalty on the net tax benefit.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Corporate Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Under subsection 163(2) of the Income Tax Act, a 50% civil penalty of the tax attempted to be evaded; under section 239 of the Income Tax Act, a conviction for tax evasion is punishable by a fine of not more than 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for up to five years; restitution and court-ordered forfeiture of the proceeds of crime available to the Crown.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorize or participate in tax evasion may be personally assessed for the corporate tax shortfall under the criminal restitution doctrine, face disqualification from acting as directors under the Canada Business Corporations Act, and may be subject to extradition and travel bans where the evasion involves treaty fraud, tax flight, or money laundering; subsection 227.1 of the Income Tax Act makes directors personally liable for unremitted employee source deductions and GST/HST.
Shell Company / Artificial Conduit

Statutory sanction: CRA may apply the GAAR under subsection 245(2), deeming the SPV's separate legal personality ineffective; tax benefit is denied, and a 50% penalty may be assessed on the benefit at the time of the assessment; CRA may also impose transfer-pricing reassessment and withhold the non-resident's income under the treaty abuse provisions (e.g., section 145.1 of the Income Tax Act for hybrid mismatch arrangements).

Director personal liability: Directors of a shell entity may be jointly and severally liable for the corporation's unpaid withholding tax if they knowingly signed remittance returns or directed the diversion of funds; the CRA has authority to issue 'section 160' transfers where assets were distributed to a non-arm's-length individual or corporate shareholder without adequate consideration, making the recipient personally liable for the tax balance; criminal courts may impose director travel bans and personal bankruptcy where the evasion exceeds CAD 10 million.
Anti-Money Laundering (AML) and Know-Your-Client (KYC)

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties assessed by FINTRAC of up to CAD 500,000 for violations (and up to CAD 2,000,000 for repeat offenders); the Payment Card Network (PCN) guidelines do not exempt medium-risk holdco structures; failure to file a Suspicious Transaction Report or Electronic Funds Transfer Report may trigger a separate conviction with a fine of up to CAD 500,000 and imprisonment for up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers can receive personal penalties for AML non-compliance where they deliberately or recklessly omit record-keeping and reporting obligations; under PCMLTFA section 75.1, personal liability reaches CAD 100,000 per individual; where the company is used to commit fraud or criminal financing, the individual can be prosecuted as a principal and, if convicted, prohibited from re-entering Canada and subject to search-and-seizure of personal assets under a restraining order.
Transfer Pricing and Information Return Violations

Statutory sanction: Adjustment of net income to arm's-length amount plus a transfer-pricing penalty of 10% of the net adjustment (minimum CAD 1,000, maximum CAD 100,000 amount per assessment? actually 10% statutory minimum applies), plus interest; where the taxpayer fails to file T106, the daily penalty is CAD 24 per day up to CAD 12,500; if the pricing is considered gross negligence or willful misrepresentation, the 50% civil penalty under s.163.2 may be assessed instead.

Director personal liability: Directors who attest to the accuracy of T106 filings or sign T2 returns can be held liable for the civil penalty where they intentionally misrepresent the arm's-length nature of the transactions; transfer-pricing penalties are normally assessed against the corporation, but under the 'ordinary business care' doctrine, directors who knew or should have known of the false pricing may be jointly liable for the unremitted tax, and CRA may seek an order prohibiting international travel and requiring the posting of security for unpaid assessments.
Failure to Maintain Canadian Corporate Residence and Statutory Recourse

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act, the corporation may be deemed non-resident, losing access to the 112(1) dividend deduction and becoming subject to a 25% withholding tax on every distribution made to its shareholders; Ontario corporate statute filings may be administratively dissolved or struck from the register after repeated default, ceasing the corporation's legal capacity to sue or defend and rendering all contracts unenforceable.

Director personal liability: Directors of a dissolved or non-resident-deemed corporation are personally liable for the corporation's debts incurred after dissolution without their knowledge; recovery under the Ontario Business Corporations Act section 248 for defective filings may subject directors to restitution of assets and statutory fines; in severe cases, personal travel bans and revocation of Canadian eTA or business visitor status may be ordered by the courts as a condition of dealing with the corporation's tax liabilities.
