Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) for federal income tax and GST/HST, portal: canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html; Revenu Québec (RQ) for Quebec corporate tax and Quebec Sales Tax (QST), portal: www.revenuquebec.ca/en/businesses/

E-government filing portal

A foreign shipbuilding entity must first obtain a federal Business Number (BN) from CRA by submitting Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) or registering online via 'Represent a Client'; the 9-digit BN with program accounts (e.g., RC0001 income tax, RT0001 GST/HST, RM0001 payroll, RP0001 customs) constitutes the statutory TIN. Concurrently, the entity must incorporate federally under the Canada Business Corporations Act (CBCA) or provincially under the Quebec Business Corporations Act (QBCA), or complete extra-provincial registration, with a registered office in Quebec and a Canadian resident director or authorized representative, then register with Revenu Québec through the 'clients' portal (Form LM-1) to obtain the Quebec registration number and QST account. No minimum capital is required; the process is completed before the first taxable supply or contractual bid is executed.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation; Territorial_Scope: a resident Canadian business corporation is subject to federal Part I tax on worldwide income (general rate 15% after provincial abatement for 2025) plus Quebec corporate income tax at 11.5% under the Taxation Act (Quebec) where a permanent establishment exists in Quebec; a non-resident-owned shipyard carrying on business in Canada is taxable on Canadian-source income and is not a pass-through entity. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation; Territorial_Scope: a resident Canadian business corporation is subject to federal Part I tax on worldwide income (general rate 15% after provincial abatement for 2025) plus Quebec corporate income tax at 11.5% under the Taxation Act (Quebec) where a permanent establishment exists in Quebec; a non-resident-owned shipyard carrying on business in Canada is taxable on Canadian-source income and is not a pass-through entity. The corporation is not eligible for US-style check-the-box classification; capital cost allowance (CCA), scientific research and experimental development (SR&ED) credits, and qualified shipbuilding investment tax credits are available.

Available election forms

Available elections include: (i) ITA s. 85 rollover election on qualifying property transfers (Form T2057); (ii) ITA s. 216 election for a non-resident to compute Canadian property income on a net basis; (iii) GST/HST closely related corporation election to treat certain supplies as nil consideration (Form RC4616); (iv) GST/HST Quick Method election (Form GST74) for small registrants; (v) Canada's hybrid mismatch rules (ITA s. 12.7, 18.6, 80.01(8)) override treaty benefits for deduction/non-inclusion or double-deduction outcomes, with no elective carve-out; (vi) for a foreign parent, an election under the Canada-Quebec harmonized administration agreement to align federal and provincial tax characterization, and submission of a treaty-based waiver or reduction of Part XIII withholding on dividends.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual worldwide taxable supplies (GST/HST under Excise Tax Act s. 240; QST under QSTA s. 33); a non-resident shipyard making taxable supplies in Canada or Quebec must register once the threshold is exceeded unless a small supplier; exporters of zero-rated commercial vessels may register voluntarily to claim input tax credits (ITCs); registration is mandatory where the shipyard is engaged in a commercial activity in Canada and annual revenue exceeds CAD 30,000.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Excise Tax Act Schedule VI: (i) a supply of a vessel used or to be used principally in the transportation of passengers or goods in international commercial trade is zero-rated under Part I, Heading 5 (including ocean container vessels and their essential shipyard equipment); (ii) a supply of goods (vessels, cranes, ship components) exported by or on behalf of the supplier is zero-rated under Part V, s. 1 where the goods are delivered outside Canada, delivered to a common carrier or customs broker for export, or exported via a marine freight forwarder with proof of export retained for two years; (iii) services provided solely for the benefit of a non-resident purchaser (ship design, engineering, outfitting, project management) are zero-rated under Part V, s. 7 where no element of the service is rendered in Canada for local consumption; (iv) the shipyard must be a GST/HST registrant and obtain a matching QST zero-rating treatment under the QSTA regulations; the builds must be destined for non-resident owners in international trade to qualify.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA Form B13A / Canadian Export Declaration or Canada Customs Invoice) evidencing export from Canada
  • Bill of Lading (ocean or multimodal transport document issued to the non-resident purchaser)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank foreign-exchange contract evidencing receipt of foreign currency payment
  • Commercial invoice (certified) with vessel hull identification number and HS code 8901.20 issued to the non-resident buyer
  • Carrier's certificate or freight forwarder's proof of physical movement of the vessel out of Canadian waters
  • GST/HST and QST zero-rated supply record: purchase contract, delivery protocol, and CRA-validated export ledger

Canada does not impose a real-time fiscal clearance, API-based pre-approval, or digital stamping mandate at the federal level; however, CRA requires electronic record retention under ITA s. 230(4.1) and GST/HST records for six years. The Pan-Canadian e-invoice standard (Peppol BIS / EN 16931) is endorsed by CRA and GS1 Canada as the voluntary national B2B interoperability framework; Revenu Québec mandates electronic filing of QST returns and remittances through its online 'clients' service. A shipyard must issue compliant GST/QST invoices containing its BN and QST registration number, vessel description, and zero-rating status, and retain a fiscal digital record (FDR) for audit; ANSI X12 EDI or XML B2B invoices are acceptable in lieu of statutory e-invoicing.

CRA may reassess within the normal four-year period, extended to seven years for misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, or willful default, and without time limit where fraud or misrepresentation is established (ITA s. 152(4); ETA s. 298(1)); a false or fraudulent export zero-rating claim triggers the ETA s. 280 penalty of CAD 250 or 25% of net tax, and the ITA s. 163(2) gross-negligence penalty of 50% of tax understated; CRA and Revenu Québec may retroactively claw back input tax credits (ITCs), deny refunds and assessment, and impose arrears interest and penalities of 50% on the amount of QST evaded when supporting export documentation is not retained for six years or where the 180-day repatriation benchmark is not met; criminal prosecution is possible under the Criminal Code for aiding false or deceptive statements in customs and export documentation.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a floating exchange rate regime with unrestricted capital and current account convertibility. Resident corporations face no prior approval requirements for profit remittances, dividend distributions, or capital repatriation. FX conversions are permitted without restriction, subject solely to ongoing AML/CTF compliance and KYC obligations administered by FINTRAC.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No mandatory surrender or conversion of export proceeds is required. Receipts may be retained in foreign currency accounts or freely converted to CAD. Exporters must report all cash transactions exceeding CAD 10,000 to FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act; no statutory conversion ratios or timelines apply.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined standard general corporate income tax rate for a Quebec corporation: 15% federal and 11.5% Quebec. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may access a federal small-business rate of 9% and, subject to Quebec eligibility rules, a Quebec small-business rate of 3.2%, producing a potential combined rate of 12.2% on eligible income.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Canadian withholding tax on taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident, subject to treaty reduction. Under the Canada-United States treaty, the rate is generally 5% for a qualifying corporate beneficial owner holding at least 10% of the voting power and 15% in other cases. Other treaties commonly reduce the rate to 5%, 10%, or 15%, depending on ownership and treaty conditions.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 and CO-17 are generally due within six months after the corporation's fiscal year-end. Any balance of tax is generally payable two months after year-end, or three months after year-end for qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporations meeting the statutory conditions. Instalments are due monthly or, where permitted, quarterly.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate value of non-arm's-length non-resident transactions generally triggers Form T106 reporting and contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation requirements. Canadian law does not impose a separate OECD-style mandatory Master File and Local File filing package for every taxpayer; documentation must nevertheless support the arm's-length nature of material transactions. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to a constituent entity of a qualifying multinational enterprise group with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone economic-substance regulation equivalent to the United Kingdom, Cayman Islands, or certain other jurisdictions. Substance is relevant through the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty anti-abuse rules, beneficial-ownership requirements, sham and characterization doctrines, transfer-pricing analysis, thin-capitalization provisions, foreign-affiliate rules, and principal-purpose tests. A Quebec shipyard should maintain real Canadian management, employees, production assets, procurement functions, technical decision-making, inventory and project controls, banking records, and risk-bearing capacity proportionate to the profits claimed in Quebec. Intercompany procurement, design, engineering, financing, warranty, licensing, and management charges require written agreements and evidence of actual services and benefits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Corporate tax evasion and false tax reporting

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act, tax evasion may result in fines ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years. Gross-negligence penalties can generally equal 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit, in addition to tax and interest.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face criminal prosecution where personal participation, authorization, knowledge, or wilful blindness is proven. Ordinary corporate income-tax debt does not automatically create personal liability, but directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions and certain GST/HST amounts, subject to statutory due-diligence defenses. Courts may impose restitution or fines; imprisonment is possible for criminal convictions. Travel restrictions are not an automatic tax consequence but may arise from bail, probation, enforcement, or criminal proceedings.
Failure to remit payroll deductions and indirect taxes

Statutory sanction: The CRA and Revenu Quebec may assess the unremitted amounts, interest, gross-negligence or failure-to-remit penalties, director-liability assessments, garnishment, liens, and legal costs. Deliberate tax fraud may produce criminal fines and imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors can be assessed personally for certain unremitted payroll deductions and GST/HST or QST amounts if statutory conditions are met and due diligence is unavailable. A director should retain cash-flow controls, remittance calendars, reconciliations, approval evidence, and documented corrective action.
Transfer-pricing manipulation and profit shifting

Statutory sanction: The CRA may reassess income under the arm's-length principle, deny deductions, impose transfer-pricing adjustment penalties generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where statutory conditions are met, assess interest, and apply the general anti-avoidance rule or treaty anti-abuse provisions. Quebec may make corresponding reassessments.

Director personal liability: Directors generally face corporate reassessment rather than automatic personal liability. Personal exposure can arise from participation in fraud, knowingly false records, obstruction, criminal tax evasion, or director-liability provisions applicable to source deductions and indirect taxes.
Treaty abuse and improper withholding-tax reduction

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for the unwithheld tax, interest, penalties, and information-return failures. CRA may deny treaty relief, apply the domestic 25% rate, and reassess related deductions where anti-avoidance or characterization rules apply.

Director personal liability: Responsible directors and officers may be exposed for deliberate false certifications or knowing failures to remit. Personal exposure is strongest where the individual controlled the payment process, ignored clear deficiencies, or participated in concealment.
Shell company, beneficial ownership, and economic-substance failure

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include denial of treaty benefits, income reallocation, GAAR reassessment, transfer-pricing adjustments, withholding-tax assessments, penalties, interest, corporate-register consequences, and financial-institution account restrictions.

Director personal liability: Piercing the corporate veil is exceptional and fact-specific. Directors may nevertheless incur direct civil or criminal liability for fraud, misrepresentation, oppressive conduct, breach of fiduciary duty, or statutory remittance failures. Civil restitution, asset preservation, and enforcement orders may be available; travel bans or imprisonment require separate judicial or criminal processes.
Anti-money-laundering, proceeds of crime, and sanctions violations

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties vary by violation and compliance history and may reach significant amounts, including up to CAD 1,000,000 for certain serious violations. Criminal proceeds-of-crime and money-laundering offences can result in substantial fines and imprisonment, including life imprisonment for laundering proceeds of indictable offences in the most serious cases. Sanctions violations may attract prosecution, asset seizure, and penalties under Canadian sanctions legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, compliance personnel, and beneficial owners may be prosecuted where they knowingly participate, direct, counsel, or facilitate the conduct. Potential consequences include fines, imprisonment, forfeiture, director disqualification in appropriate proceedings, asset restraint, and court-imposed bail or travel conditions.
Customs, export-control, and maritime-license non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Consequences may include reassessed duties and taxes, customs penalties, seizure, forfeiture, administrative monetary penalties, permit suspension, procurement exclusion, environmental orders, and criminal prosecution depending on the statute and conduct.

Director personal liability: Corporate officers and directors may be personally prosecuted for knowing or consented violations. Civil damages, remediation costs, forfeiture, fines, and imprisonment are possible for serious or intentional conduct; ordinary corporate liability remains distinct from personal criminal liability.
Records destruction, obstruction, and audit interference

Statutory sanction: Failure-to-provide-information penalties, obstruction offences, adverse reassessments, preservation orders, prosecution, fines, and imprisonment may apply. Record-retention failures may also compromise deductions, credits, treaty relief, and loss claims.

Director personal liability: Individuals who direct, authorize, or knowingly assist obstruction may face personal criminal and civil exposure. Directors should implement litigation holds, tax-audit protocols, document retention, privilege controls, and truthful cooperation procedures.
Corporate insolvency and unlawful profit extraction

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include dividend recovery, oppression remedies, fraudulent-preference or transfer-at-undervalue claims, insolvency proceedings, director compensation orders, tax reassessments, and creditor damages.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal liability for prohibited dividends, unpaid wages or source deductions, breach of fiduciary duty, oppression, fraudulent transfers, and transactions approved without reasonable solvency analysis. The corporate veil does not protect intentional asset stripping or statutory director breaches.
