Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible. A Canadian limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent for Canadian income-tax purposes: the partnership generally computes income and allocates it to partners under the Income Tax Act, while partners are generally taxed on their allocated shares. The partnership may nevertheless have filing, withholding, information-reporting, payroll, GST/HST, customs, and other statutory obligations. Limited liability and tax transparency depend on proper provincial formation, limited-partner conduct, and applicable Canadian tax rules. A non-resident partner may be taxable in Canada on allocated Canadian-source income or income attributable to a Canadian permanent establishment, and section 116, Regulation 105, Regulation 105 withholding, Regulation 212 withholding, or other withholding rules may apply depending on the transaction.

Available election forms

Canada has no general United States-style check-the-box election for a Canadian limited partnership. The partnership is normally classified under Canadian legal and tax principles, with income allocated under the partnership agreement and the Income Tax Act. Relevant elections or regimes may include GST/HST joint-venture elections where statutory conditions are satisfied, section 156 closely related-person elections where available, partnership rollover or contribution elections such as section 97(2) where the statutory requirements are met, and elections affecting accounting or income computation. Canadian anti-hybrid mismatch rules can deny or neutralize deductions, exemptions, or foreign tax-credit outcomes involving hybrid entities or arrangements; they do not provide a general elective partnership classification. U.S. tax classification may be made separately under U.S. check-the-box rules, but that classification does not control Canadian treatment.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, calculated under the small-supplier rules, generally measured over the applicable single-quarter and preceding four-quarter periods. The threshold is not a universal exemption for all activities: registration may be required for non-resident commercial importers, registrants making taxable supplies in Canada, and entities that voluntarily register. The CAD 30,000 threshold should be tested at the prescribed person or associated-group level, including applicable partnership and related-person aggregation rules.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods supplied in Canada and exported from Canada may generally be zero-rated at 0% where the supplier ships or causes the goods to be shipped outside Canada, the purchaser exports the goods under the applicable statutory conditions, or another prescribed export rule applies. The supplier must retain contemporaneous evidence linking the invoice, purchaser, goods, shipping route, export date, and actual exportation. Goods sold to a Canadian intermediary are not automatically zero-rated merely because the intermediary intends to export them. Services may be zero-rated only where a specific place-of-supply and export-of-service rule is satisfied; services relating to tangible personal property situated in Canada, or services performed in respect of Canadian business activities, may remain taxable. Exporters may generally recover eligible GST/HST incurred on inputs through input tax credits, subject to registration, documentary, timing, and attribution requirements. Dairy products, including whole and skimmed milk powder, may also be subject to customs tariff classification, tariff-rate quotas, import or export permits, sanitary or food-regulatory requirements, and commodity-specific controls that are separate from GST/HST zero-rating.

Canada has no general nationwide real-time electronic-invoice clearance, mandatory government e-invoicing network, or universal digital tax-stamp requirement for ordinary GST/HST invoices. Registered suppliers must issue invoices or other documentary evidence containing prescribed GST/HST information, retain records, and support input tax credits. Electronic records are generally acceptable if integrity and accessibility requirements are met. Customs declarations and commercial import processes are increasingly administered electronically through the CBSA CARM Client Portal, but CARM is a customs-account and release system rather than a general real-time GST/HST invoice-clearance regime. Provincial, contractual, payment-network, or sector-specific electronic invoicing requirements may apply independently.

Canada has no general statutory 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for GST/HST export zero-rating. Zero-rating is substantiated by the statutory export conditions and reliable documentary evidence, not by a universal foreign-exchange certificate or mandatory repatriation period. If CRA determines that the supply was not exported, that the service did not qualify for zero-rating, or that evidence is insufficient, CRA may assess the applicable GST/HST retroactively, deny related input tax credits or rebates, impose arrears interest, and apply penalties for late remittance, failure to file, false statements, or gross negligence. Gross-negligence penalties can generally be up to 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit or refund, subject to the applicable statutory calculation. Deliberate tax evasion may result in prosecution, fines, and imprisonment. False customs declarations, incorrect tariff treatment, or non-compliance with export reporting may generate CBSA Administrative Monetary Penalty System penalties, reassessment of duties and taxes, seizure or forfeiture exposure, and prosecution. Any refund or input tax credit obtained on the basis of fraudulent or unsupported export evidence may be reassessed and recovered with interest and penalties.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible on both current and capital account. There are no exchange-control permits, no capital-repatriation ceilings, and no CFE-style approval gates for non-resident limited partners of an Ontario limited partnership. Remittance frictions under the Income Tax Act (Canada) are exclusively tax-driven: (i) Part XIII non-resident withholding at the 25% statutory rate on any dividends, interest, management fees, or royalties flowing to non-resident partners (treaty reduction available, e.g., to 15%/0% under the Canada-US Convention), and (ii) section 116 compliance certificates for any disposition of taxable Canadian property such as an interest in a partnership whose assets consist principally of Canadian real or resource property. Capital-account transactions such as partner capital subscriptions, loan advances, and liquidation distributions are unrestricted, but financial institutions must complete Balance of Payments reporting to Statistics Canada for foreign direct investment transactions above reporting thresholds, and must apply OSFI/FINTRAC guidance on beneficial ownership for higher-risk non-resident structures.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement and no mandatory conversion ratio. Exporters of raw whole and skimmed milk powder may retain, hold, or convert export receipts in any currency at commercial banks without Bank of Canada or OSFI approval. The only mandatory obligations are statistical and documentary: a CBSA B13 export declaration for shipments meeting statistical thresholds, Statistics Canada balance-of-payments reporting for large receipts, and dairy-specific export certification under the Safe Food for Canadians Regulations and the Canada Agricultural Products Act to verify sanitary and compositional standards. The Canadian Dairy Commission administers supply-management import controls, but milk powder exports themselves are not subject to surrender, repatriation-timing, or forced-sale rules.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (the central monetary and banking authority), acting alongside the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for prudential supervision of Schedule I and Schedule II banks, the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for AML/KYC and trade-transaction monitoring, and Global Affairs Canada for sanctions administration under the Special Economic Measures Act and the United Nations Act. Canada has no dedicated Foreign Exchange Administration because the Foreign Exchange Control Act was formally repealed in December 1951; therefore no central-bank approval is required for foreign-currency conversion, capital movement, or profit repatriation.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Restricted / Low-Medium and legally unsuitable as a primary banking rail. Canada does not recognise EU or UK electronic money institution (EMI) licences; cross-border digital payment firms operate as FINTRAC-registered Money Service Businesses (MSBs) or payment service providers under the Retail Payment Activities Act, not as deposit-taking banks. An MSB cannot issue documentary letters of credit, hold partner-capital escrow, provide SWIFT MT700 trade finance, or extend regulated overdraft lines, and Ontario treats money-services activities as regulated conduct requiring registration under applicable provincial law. Wise Business, Airwallex, and Convera may be used as supplementary FX execution or low-value expense payment utilities, but the consolidated treasury and all intercompany/inbound capital flows must sit on a Schedule I chartered bank platform to preserve LC issuance capability, FINTRAC reporting integrity, and auditability for the Ontario LP structure.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

No entity-level corporate income tax applies to the Ontario Limited Partnership; the LP is fiscally transparent and all income is allocated to partners. Reference combined rates for a taxable Canadian corporation: federal general rate 15% plus Ontario general rate 11.5% equals 26.5% combined statutory headline. A corporate partner resident in Canada would be subject to this combined rate on its allocated share of the LP dairy-trading income.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% statutory Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder. In the Ontario LP structure, profit distributions to non-resident partners are not technically dividends; the partners are taxed on their allocated share of the LP business income under Part I. Any distribution of interest, rent, royalty or management-fee characterization remains subject to the 25% Part XIII rate unless a tax treaty reduces it.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000: contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation (Local File equivalent) is mandatory when the aggregate annual value of non-arm's length cross-border transactions with non-resident related parties exceeds CAD 1,000,000 (Income Tax Act Section 247(4); T2 Schedule 88). A Master File and Country-by-Country Report are required for multinational groups with consolidated group revenue of at least CAD 750,000,000. The Ontario LP must prepare and retain the documentation within 6 months of the fiscal year-end.

Economic substance scope: Canada has not enacted a stand-alone Economic Substance Regulations regime comparable to the UAE or BVI; however, the underlying principle of economic substance is enforced through: (1) the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) under Section 245 of the Income Tax Act, which denies tax benefits from transactions lacking bona fide business purpose and real economic substance; (2) anti-treaty-shopping jurisprudence (e.g., Alta Energy) denying treaty benefits where the main purpose is treaty shopping; (3) the Principal Purpose Test (PPT) under the OECD Multilateral Instrument as ratified by Canada; and (4) the Ontario Limited Partnerships Act, which strips limited liability from a limited partner that participates in control of the business. To maintain substance, the Ontario LP must maintain a physical office in Canada, employ personnel, keep complete books and records, hold inventory or warehouse contracts, issue commercial invoices, and make binding trading decisions in Canada.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Corporate Returns

Statutory sanction: Summary conviction under Income Tax Act Section 238: a fine of 50% to double the amount of tax evaded and imprisonment of up to 2 years. Indictable conviction under Section 239(1): a fine of 100% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment of up to 5 years. Civil gross-negligence penalty under Section 163(2) of 50% of the understated tax. Daily compound interest at the prescribed CRA rate applies to the entire unpaid balance.

Director personal liability: The directors of the corporate general partner and the individual general partner are jointly and severally liable under Income Tax Act Section 227.1 and Section 323 for unremitted source deductions, GST/HST and Part XIII withholding. CRA may issue a threat-of-personal-liability letter and file a lien on personal real estate. A travel ban may be obtained under Section 226(2) of the Income Tax Act as an injunction to prevent a director or partner from leaving Canada while payment is outstanding. A limited partner who becomes involved in management loses limited-liability protection and becomes a general partner for liability purposes under the Ontario Limited Partnerships Act.
Shell Company / Shallow Substance / Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: The General Anti-Avoidance Rule may nullify the intended tax benefit, causing the CRA to recharacterize the entire transaction; treaty withholding relief is denied retroactively, resulting in 25% Part XIII tax plus interest and penalties; transfer-pricing reassessment with penalty of 10% of the net upward adjustment under Section 247(3); FINTRAC administrative monetary penalty of up to CAD 1,000,000 for unregistered money services business activity if the LP handles third-party payment flows; cancellation of the Ontario extra-provincial registration and the dairy wholesale licence

Director personal liability: Directors, the general partner and any controlling shareholders may be assessed personally under Section 160 of the Income Tax Act for non-arm's length transfers; the court may pierce the corporate veil in the Ontario Superior Court, holding the US or PRC parent directly liable for the LP obligations; CRA collection measures can include freezing of personal bank accounts and a Section 226(2) travel ban.
Offshore Tax Non-Compliance and Foreign Filing Failures

Statutory sanction: T1135 late-filing penalty of CAD 25 per day up to CAD 2,500; repeated failures attract a penalty of 5% of the value of the unreported property; criminal prosecution under Section 239 for wilful failure to report foreign property; the CRA may impose third-party penalties under Section 163.2 of up to CAD 24,000 plus the gross compensation of any tax planner who assisted the non-filing

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for the penalties if they were the controlling mind and failed to exercise due diligence; the Section 227.1(3) due-diligence defence requires proof of reasonable care and the absence of knowledge or consent; directors found liable may be subject to travel restrictions and asset seizure in support of the tax collection.
