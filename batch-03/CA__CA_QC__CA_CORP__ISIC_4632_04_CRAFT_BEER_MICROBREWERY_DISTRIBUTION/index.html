Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

No check-the-box pass-through election exists under Canadian law. The Business Corporation is taxed at entity level on worldwide income (federal Part I tax plus Quebec corporate tax), with 'Canadian-controlled private corporation' status potentially qualifying for the federal small business deduction (9% on active business income up to CAD 500,000) and the Quebec reduced rate (3.2% on the same base). Available statutory elections include: (i) GST/HST closely-related corporation joint election (Excise Tax Act s. 156 and QST Act equivalent) treating qualifying related registrants as one registrant; (ii) GST/HST joint venture election (ETA s. 273 / QST equivalent); (iii) Income Tax Act s. 85 rollover election on asset transfers to the corporation; (iv) no elective opt-out for the hybrid mismatch rules — mandated deduction-denial provisions under ITA ss. 12.6–12.93 and related provisions apply automatically to cross-border hybrid arrangements after June 30, 2022.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — small-supplier registration threshold measured by worldwide taxable supplies (including zero-rated exports) over any four consecutive calendar quarters; applies equally to GST/HST and QST. Export-oriented wholesalers typically register voluntarily even below the threshold to recover input tax credits.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods, including craft beer and kegs, qualify for a 0% GST/HST and 0% QST rate where the supply is made in Canada and the goods are transferred to a non-resident recipient or a common carrier for export promptly after sale, per ETA Schedule VI s. 23 and corresponding QST Act provisions. The goods must not be consumed in Canada. Offshore services (e.g., international distribution or logistics consultancy supplied to a non-resident) qualify for zero-rating under ETA Schedule VI s. 7 where the services are performed outside Canada or relate to foreign property. For excise duty paid on beer produced in Canada and later exported, a drawback/rebate is claimable under s. 124 of the Excise Act, 2001 within two years of the duty payment; Quebec's specific tax on alcoholic beverages does not attach to exported product.

  • CBSA Form B13A (Goods to Be Exported) or electronic export declaration filed through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CAED) for non-U.S. destinations; U.S.-bound shipments are ordinarily exempt from B13A unless controlled or permitted goods are involved
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing shipment from Canada to the non-resident consignee
  • Commercial invoice reconciling to the B13A/CAED filing, with HS code 2203.00 for malt beer
  • Proof-of-export evidential package retained per CRA GST/HST Memorandum 4.5 (carrier certificates, customs stamps, container tracking movements, port storage records)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate, SWIFT wire advice, or bank statement demonstrating receipt of foreign currency from the non-resident buyer — a CRA audit documentation benchmark rather than a statutory requirement
  • Excise Act, 2001 drawback application (with supporting production and export records) where excise duty was paid on the exported beer

No real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control (CTC), or mandatory digital stamping regime exists in Canada. E-invoicing is voluntary, with PEPPOL BIS 3.0 / UBL adoption promoted through Canada's 'Business Connect' initiative. Statutory invoice content is prescribed (supplier and recipient identity, GST/HST and QST registration numbers, dates, tax rates and amounts — ETA s. 3 and QST Act equivalents). In Quebec, the Charter of the French Language requires French to be used in commercial documents such as catalogues and contracts; business records must be kept in Canada and made available to CRA and Revenu Québec inspectors, in French or English, upon demand.

CRA and Revenu Québec may deny zero-rating where export evidence is incomplete, contradictory, or the goods return to Canada. The denied exporter must remit the full combined rate of 14.975% (5% GST + 9.975% QST) on the sale value, plus prescribed-rate interest and penalties. Gross-negligence false-statement penalties apply: ETA s. 285 imposes the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the tax sought to be evaded; the Quebec Tax Administration Act mirrors this for QST. The ordinary four-year reassessment window is suspended indefinitely where misrepresentation, neglect, or fraud is established. Criminal tax evasion (ETA s. 327 and equivalent Quebec provisions) carries fines up to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment of up to five years. Export pricing to related non-resident distributors is also scrutinized under OECD-aligned transfer-pricing rules, with CRA's Aggressive Tax Planning unit able to adjust values, deny deductions, and apply transfer-pricing penalties of 10% of the net adjustment.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

CAD is fully convertible under IMF Article VIII for both current-account and capital-account transactions. There is no FX approval, quantitative ceiling, or waiting period for profit remittance, dividend repatriation, or capital outflow. The only statutory frictions are Quebec Business Corporations Act (QBCA) solvency gating for distributions and Part XIII withholding tax (25% statutory; reduced to 5%/15% under the Canada-US treaty) on dividends. Non-resident ownership is unrestricted, dividends may be declared in USD or CAD, and no currency-export permit is required.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no export-proceeds surrender, no mandatory repatriation timeline, and no forced conversion ratio; exporters retain 100% of USD and other foreign-currency receipts in multicurrency accounts. The applicable statutory obligations are: (i) CBSA B13A export declaration for shipments above C$2,000 or controlled goods; (ii) FINTRAC EFT report for every international electronic transfer equal to or greater than C$10,000; (iii) Statistics Canada balance-of-payments (BOP) threshold-reporting for international transactions; and (iv) federal-beneficial-ownership registry filings (Bill C-42) for the reporting entity.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) is the central monetary and Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) licensing authority; OSFI supervises federally chartered banks; FINTRAC enforces cross-border electronic funds transfer (EFT) reporting under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). Canada maintains no separate foreign-exchange administration; the Minister of Finance retains dormant discretionary exchange-control powers under the Currency Act and Foreign Exchange Regulations, which are not invoked for ordinary trade.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Low-to-Medium viability. Foreign digital EMIs (Wise Platform, Airwallex, Corpay, OFX, Payoneer) may operate only as FINTRAC-registered Money Services Businesses (MSBs) and must partner with an approved Payments Canada clearing agent to settle CAD. Bank of Canada RPAA licensing applies from 2024-2025, adding a dual-federal regulatory layer. Legal constraints: EMIs cannot grant deposit insurance, cannot issue Canadian deposit accounts, cannot provide overdraft/credit, and cannot issue standby letters of credit; they are therefore unacceptable for escrowed share capital, LC-backed equipment imports, or primary treasury concentration. Recommended narrow use: sub-C$10,000 operational disbursements to overseas hops/glassware suppliers and fast USD-to-CAD conversions for small invoices only.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 per non-arm's-length transaction series (goods/services); group Master File required where consolidated group revenue exceeds CAD 50,000,000; Local File must be contemporaneous and filed with the income tax return only upon CRA request.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate the offshore Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) in the manner of Caribbean/QFC jurisdictions; however, taxpayers must satisfy business-purpose and economic substance requirements through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA s.245), Quebec's GAAR provision, and the capital equity test; a structure lacking actual management, employees, premises or economic risk may be disregarded or recharacterised.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraudulent Concealment

Statutory sanction: Penalty of 50% to 200% of tax evaded under ITA and Quebec Taxation Act fines; prosecution may impose imprisonment of up to 5 years; interest and gross negligence penalties assessed on entire unreported income.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for corporate withholding tax source deductions and GST/QST remittances (ITA s.227.1; Excise Tax Act s.323); where evasion is directed by an officer, that officer faces personal conviction, travel restrictions, issuance of an arrest warrant, and seizure of personal property.
Shell Company / Artificial Primary Business

Statutory sanction: CRA and Revenu Québec realign the transaction under GAAR; denies treaty relief and reclassifies the income as personal income to owners; penalties equal to 50% of disallowed benefits may be imposed; in Quebec, a 'shelf company' misuse may trigger revocation of registrations and a sanction of CAD 5,000 to CAD 50,000.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil is pierced if the corporation is a sham; nominal directors (who accept fees and are not involved) are held personally liable for tax debts and may be named in consent orders prohibiting departure from Canada until the liability is discharged.
Transfer Pricing / Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: ITA s.247(3) penalties: 10% of the net adjustment (apply to the decline of revenues or profits shifted), minimum CAD 500, plus reassessed tax and interest; for repeat offenders, 20% of the adjustment; Quebec mirrors this in provincial tax law.

Director personal liability: Directors may avoid TP penalty individually, but where they knowingly sign false certificates under the T2 or CO-17, they face disqualification from serving as directors for up to 7 years under the Canada Business Corporations Act; personal liability for unpaid amounts is not assessed unless fraud is proven.
Money Laundering / Terrorist Financing (AML)

Statutory sanction: Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA): administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 500,000 per violation; criminal indictment for each offence imposes a fine of up to CAD 2,000,000 and imprisonment for up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for corporate AML fines if involved in laundering, and may be the subject of freezing orders, asset forfeiture, and travel bans; individuals who fail to supervise compliance may receive prison sentences up to 2 years.
Withholding Tax on Cross-Border Payments

Statutory sanction: Unpaid Part XIII tax is recoverable with interest from the payer; penalties equal to 10% of the amount that should have been withheld if the payer failed to withhold (ITA s.227(9)); in Quebec, provincial penalties under the Tax Administration Act.

Director personal liability: Corporate directors become personally liable under a deemed trust for unremitted withholding taxes (salary, dividends, royalties, interest); the Canada Revenue Agency may register a security interest against personal assets and obtain an international travel ban under the Criminal Code if the amount is wilful.
