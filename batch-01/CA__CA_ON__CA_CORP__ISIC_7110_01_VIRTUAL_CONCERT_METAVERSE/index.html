Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA / Agence du revenu du Canada) is the sole competent national authority for federal corporate income tax, GST/HST, and payroll. Ontario corporate income tax is collected by the CRA under the Federal-Provincial Tax Collection Agreement; the Ontario Ministry of Finance administers only Employer Health Tax (EHT) and residual Retail Sales Tax (RST). Digital portals: CRA My Business Account (canada.ca) and Business Registration Online (BRO); ServiceOntario handles extra-provincial registration under the Ontario Business Corporations Act.

E-government filing portal

1. Incorporate under the Canada Business Corporations Act or the Ontario Business Corporations Act and obtain Articles of Incorporation. 2. If federally incorporated, file extra-provincial registration (Form 1 under the OBCA) with ServiceOntario to obtain an Ontario corporate number. 3. Apply to the CRA for the nine-digit Business Number (BN) through Business Registration Online or Form RC1; non-resident directors must attach certified articles, director identification, and Form RC434 appointing a Canadian representative for service of process and tax administration. 4. On the same application, open program accounts: RC (GST/HST), RT (corporate income tax), and RP (payroll); add RM (customs) only for physical imports/exports. 5. CRA issues the BN and account confirmation immediately for online applications. 6. Register separately with the Ontario Ministry of Finance for EHT once Ontario payroll exceeds the CAD 1,000,000 exemption threshold (effective January 1, 2025).

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: a Canadian business corporation is a separate taxable person with no flow-through or disregarded-entity concept (ITA s. 89(1)). Resident corporations are taxed on worldwide income, with residence determined by place of incorporation or central management and control; a CBCA/OBCA corporation is resident in Canada. Combined 2024-2025 federal plus Ontario general corporate income tax approximates 26.5% (federal 15% plus Ontario 11.5%). Non-resident corporations are taxed only on Canadian-source income (territorial limitation). Pass-through eligibility is not available.

Available election forms

Canada has no US-style check-the-box election and no consolidated-group election. Available mechanics: (a) first taxation-year end election (ITA s. 249); (b) functional-currency election to report in US dollars or another CRA-recognized currency (ITA s. 261); (c) capital dividend account election on tax-free dividends (ITA s. 83(2)); (d) section 85(1) tax-deferred rollover on property contributed to the corporation; (e) GST/HST group registration election (ETA s. 156) and joint-venture election (ETA s. 273); (f) hybrid-mismatch measures under ITA ss. 18.2, 18.3, and 212.3 apply automatically with no elective override; the Canada-US Treaty Art. IV(6) transparency rules govern classification of any US LLC shareholder making check-the-box elections.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of taxable supplies made in Canada over any trailing four consecutive calendar quarters under the ETA small-supplier rule; registration is mandatory once the threshold is exceeded and voluntary below it. A virtual concert admission or metaverse ticket is a taxable supply of intangible personal property, ordinarily subject to 13% HST unless zero-rated as a supply to a non-resident consumed outside Canada (see export zero-rating criteria).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A metaverse/virtual concert ticket is an intangible personal property right or digital service. It is eligible for zero-rating at 0% GST/HST under ETA Schedule VI Part V when: (i) the recipient is a non-resident of Canada, not a GST/HST registrant, and consumes or uses the performance outside Canada; (ii) the supply does not relate to real property or tangible personal property situated in Canada; and (iii) delivery is evidenced by geo-IP logs, tokenized-ticket/blockchain ledgers, and invoice records. Supplies to GST/HST registrants carrying on commercial activity in Canada, or to individuals physically present in Canada, remain taxable at 13% HST. Where physical merchandise is bundled and exported, zero-rating requires shipment out of Canada within 60 days of the earlier of payment or the date consideration becomes due, evidenced by a CBSA Form B13A export report. Canada imposes no separate statutory FX repatriation deadline; the 60-day window is the documentary export-evidence period for GST/HST zero-rating purposes.

  • CBSA export report (Form B13A) or ACI e-manifest where tangible merchandise is physically exported from Canada
  • Commercial invoice or ticket contract identifying the non-resident buyer, the destination country, and a 'GST/HST zero-rated export' notation
  • Digital-delivery evidence: geo-IP logs, metaverse session access records, and tokenized-ticket or blockchain ledger showing delivery and use outside Canada
  • Inward Remittance Certificate, SWIFT MT103, or Stripe payout reconciliation and USDC transfer record evidencing settlement from abroad, converted to CAD at transaction-date fair market value
  • Non-resident self-declaration or ETA export substantiation memorandum documenting the end-user country and confirming no Canadian consumption

No real-time fiscal-clearance or digital-stamping mandate exists in Canada or Ontario. CRA requires electronic filing of GST/HST returns (NETFILE) for registrants with annual taxable supplies exceeding CAD 1.5 million; other registrants may file electronically. Electronic invoicing is permitted and is being promoted through the voluntary Pan-Canadian Peppol framework. Every GST/HST invoice must contain the supplier's name and BN, invoice date, description of the supply, consideration, tax rate, and tax amount, or a clear 'zero-rated export' notation (ETA ss. 169 and 223). Registration records and invoices must be retained for six years.

CRA closely audits zero-rated export claims, cryptographic payment trails, and related ITC refunds. Failure to substantiate export triggers reassessment of 13% HST plus daily compound interest (ETA s. 296). Civil penalties under ETA s. 280 for false or grossly negligent statements equal the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the net tax understated; fraudulent evasion under ETA s. 285 and ITA s. 239 attracts fines of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to five years. GST/HST and corporate income tax reassessment windows (normally four years) remain open indefinitely in the case of misrepresentation or fraud. Claimed input tax credits are clawed back with interest, and circular USDC remittances, backdated geo-IP logs, or sham foreign intermediary structures are recharacterized under common-law sham doctrine, GAAR (ITA s. 245), and ETA s. 274 tax-avoidance rules.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is freely convertible under a fully liberalized capital account. Exchange controls were abolished in 1951; neither the Bank of Canada Act nor the Currency Act imposes restrictions on current-account or capital-account conversion. Profit remittance, dividend distribution, and shareholder loan repayments to non-residents are unrestricted, subject solely to Part XIII withholding taxes (generally 25% statutory, reduced by applicable tax treaty) and to Canadian transfer-pricing rules under section 247 of the Income Tax Act. No prior administrative approval or central-bank permit is required for capital inflows or outflows; only standardized statistical reporting (Statistics Canada balance-of-payments surveys) and FINTRAC reporting for international EFTs of C$10,000 or more apply.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory surrender timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no repatriation window for export or virtual-services receipts. The entity may retain cross-border earnings in foreign currency (USD, USDC-converted fiat) in a foreign-currency account at any Schedule I bank and convert to CAD at prevailing market rates. Registration obligations are limited to statistical surveys; no approval is required from the Bank of Canada or OSFI.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as monetary and currency-issuance authority, in coordination with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for bank prudential regulation and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for AML/ATF and cross-border electronic funds transfer surveillance under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Moderate feasibility with material regulatory constraints. Canada does not operate a distinct electronic-money-institution (EMI) licensing regime; foreign EMI platforms must either (a) obtain FINTRAC Money Services Business (MSB) registration and, where applicable, the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) registration administered by the Bank of Canada, or (b) operate through a sponsored arrangement with a regulated Schedule I bank. Offshore EMIs lacking FINTRAC registration cannot lawfully access Canadian clearing rails or hold client funds in pooled Canadian settlement accounts. For USDC-based revenue conversion, the entity should contract with a registered Canadian crypto-asset trading platform (VASP) compliant with Ontario Securities Commission (OSC) and CSA crypto-asset guidance, or use a banking partner with an approved stablecoin settlement gateway.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Form T106 mandatory when aggregate non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents exceed C$1,000,000 per taxation year; contemporaneous Master File and Local File documentation required at the same threshold; Country-by-Country Report filing triggers at consolidated group revenue of C$750,000,000.

Economic substance scope: Canada applies GAAR (section 245 ITA) to deny avoidance transactions lacking real economic substance; treaty benefits are subject to the Principal Purpose Test under the MLI; a corporation must maintain actual commercial operations, decision-making office, personnel, and physical premises to withstand a residence or treaty-shopping challenge. No standalone ESR regime exists in Canada.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Concealed Canadian-Source Revenue

Statutory sanction: Gross negligence penalty equal to 50% of understated tax under subsection 163(2); criminal conviction under section 239 ITA attracting fines to 200% of evaded tax and imprisonment up to 5 years; jeopardy assessment and asset freezing; seizure of crypto wallets and bank accounts.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorize false returns are jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions and transferred corporate assets under section 160 and subsection 227.1(1); civil restitution orders; travel bans and court-ordered prohibitions; imprisonment on fraud or evasion convictions.
Shell Company Arrangement / Impermissible Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: GAAR reassessment under section 245 ITA, denial of treaty benefits, retroactive Part XIII withholding tax, interest and penalties up to 50%; possible mandatory disclosure penalties under section 163.2; reassessment and adjustment of transfer prices.

Director personal liability: Director knowingly signing a treaty relief certificate without factual residence commits false statement, carrying personal tax liability and criminal exposure; corporate veil can be pierced when the Canadian corporation is a mere conduit, exposing directors to full unpaid Part XIII and Part I taxes; CRA departure bans under section 226.1 apply.
Anti-Money-Laundering / FINTRAC Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties up to C$1,000,000 per violation for corporations; criminal fines and imprisonment under the PCMLTFA; freezing of corporate wallets and bank accounts; asset forfeiture; potential revocation of operating licenses.

Director personal liability: Senior directors and officers are personally liable for knowingly directing unreported virtual-currency transactions; fines up to C$1,000,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years; travel restrictions and extradition may be ordered; civil liability to victims of money laundering or investor fraud.
