Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – Federal administration of income tax, GST/HST, and Business Number registration through the My Business Account web portal; Ontario Ministry of Finance and Ontario Business Registry – provincial registration for Ontario limited partnerships and Ontario provincial tax accounts. The CRA administers the Ontario HST component under the federal HST framework, so the LP's GST/HST account serves as the primary provincial indirect tax identifier.

E-government filing portal

For a foreign-owned Ontario Limited Partnership (CA_LP), the statutory TIN/VAT ID procurement procedure is: (1) register the limited partnership under Ontario's Limited Partnerships Act through the Ontario Business Registry (ServiceOntario), obtaining an Ontario registration number; (2) file CRA Form RC1 ('Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts') with the partnership as the applicant, requesting a Business Number (BN) and a GST/HST account (RT program account); (3) attach the partnership agreement and, for non-resident partners, complete the applicable non-resident identification schedules; (4) receive the CRA-assigned 9-digit Business Number, which functions as the federal TIN and contains the RT program account as the GST/HST identifier; (5) appoint an authorized representative through Form RC59 for the foreign head office to manage filings; (6) integrate Ontario provincial registration through the Ontario Business Registry portal, which is mirrored in the CRA BN record. The resulting BN/RT is the statutory VAT/tax identifier for all Canadian federal and Ontario HST purposes.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible – The Ontario limited partnership is a fiscally transparent pass-through entity: no entity-level federal income tax under the Income Tax Act; each partner, resident or non-resident, is taxed on their share of the partnership's income or loss. Non-resident partners are taxable only on Canadian-source allocations, subject to Part I filing obligations or Part XIII withholding. For GST/HST, the partnership itself is treated as a person and may register as a GST/HST registrant; the LP is not a tax-exempt entity and remains within the territorial scope of Canadian federal and Ontario provincial taxation.

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a US-style check-the-box election. The CA_LP may, however, exercise the following statutory elections: (i) select the partnership's fiscal period, with mandatory calendar year-end for partnerships having Canadian-resident partners unless a non-calendar fiscal year can be justified; (ii) elect to register for GST/HST on Form RC1 even if taxable supplies are below CAD 30,000, thereby allowing zero-rating of exports; (iii) elect the Quick Method of GST/HST accounting where eligible; (iv) make joint partnership elections under the partnership agreement for income allocation, including retroactive allocations if all partners concur; (v) use the GST/HST 'non-resident simplified registration' mechanism for non-resident partners; (vi) comply with hybrid mismatch rules (ITA s.18.4) which operate automatically to deny deductions on certain hybrid mismatched arrangements rather than offering an elective shelter.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD $30,000 – the LP must register for GST/HST once total worldwide taxable supplies of the partnership (including associated entities' supplies) made in Canada equal or exceed CAD 30,000 in the current calendar year or in any immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters. For pure export zero-rating, voluntary registration is generally obtained in order to claim input tax credits and secure the 0% rate on export services.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating of exported goods applies under s.6 of Part V of Schedule VI of the Excise Tax Act; zero-rating of services to non-residents applies under s.7 of the same Schedule. For programmatic real-time ad exchange / DSP / SSP services, the supply to a non-resident advertiser, publisher, or platform is generally zero-rated provided: (a) the recipient is not resident in Canada; (b) the service is not in respect of real property situated in Canada; (c) the service is not supplied to a non-resident individual while that individual is physically present in Canada; and (d) the LP has adequate evidence of the recipient's non-resident status. When the recipient is resident in Canada or present in Canada, the supply is taxable at 13% (where the place of supply is Ontario) and subject to ordinary HST remittance.

  • Bill of Lading (or courier / air waybill evidencing international shipment) for any physical component of the campaign deployment

Canada does not maintain a real-time clearance, continuous transaction controls (CTC), or digital stamping regime for GST/HST invoices. Electronic invoicing must be issued through CRA-compliant means such as My Business Account, payroll/tax e-services or recognized EDI/XML channels; there is no API-based fiscal authorisation step. Ontario does not impose an additional provincial electronic clearance mandate beyond the federal GST/HST rules. Registered LPs must retain electronic records in a legible CRA-accessible format and are subject to CRA's electronic audit inspection rights.

The CRA may reassess the LP beyond the normal four-year limitation period where zero-rating has been misstated or where misrepresentation or wilful default exists. Civil penalties: ITA s.163(2) gross negligence penalty is 50% of the tax understated; ETA s.285.1 imposes a penalty of 5% of the tax improperly claimed plus arrears interest. Clawbacks can include retroactive cancellation of the GST/HST registration, disallowance of all input tax credits, and loss of export zero-rating status. In cases of deliberate evasion, criminal prosecution under ETA s.327 or ITA s.239 may result in fines up to double the evaded amount and imprisonment. For non-arm's-length export arrangements, the CRA may also apply the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA s.245) and impose transfer-pricing adjustments under s.247.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is a fully convertible currency under a floating exchange rate regime. Canada maintains no general foreign exchange controls under the Currency Act or the Bank of Canada Act. Both current account transactions (e.g., trade in goods and services, dividends, interest, royalties) and capital account transactions (e.g., foreign direct investment, portfolio investment, intercompany loans) are freely permitted. There are no statutory hurdles to profit remittance, no prior approval requirements for dividends or distributions to non-residents, and no withholding tax is imposed by Ontario at the provincial level on profit distributions by a limited partnership. However, payments to non-residents may be subject to Part XIII Canadian federal withholding tax if characterized as interest, rent, royalties, or management fees; treaty relief under the applicable income tax treaty (e.g., Canada-US Tax Convention) may reduce or eliminate such withholding. LP distributions of operating income to non-resident partners generally are made gross, with the partnership itself not withholding unless the partner is a non-resident of Canada and the distribution constitutes a Canadian-source payment subject to Part XIII rules.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There are no statutory export proceeds surrender requirements in Canada. Exporters are not obliged to convert export receipts into CAD, repatriate such receipts, or hold them in a specified account within a prescribed timeline. The Bank of Canada does not mandate any conversion ratio. Gross proceeds from export transactions may be retained in foreign currency, converted freely, or used directly to settle foreign-currency trade obligations. For customs and cross-border payment transparency, exporters must ensure that wire transfer purpose codes and commercial invoicing are properly documented, but no regulatory surrender time or amount applies.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); foreign exchange oversight is coordinated through the Department of Finance Canada and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for anti-money laundering purposes

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium. Offshore digital Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) generally do not hold a Canadian banking licence and cannot offer domestic Canadian-currency deposit-taking services unless licensed as a bank, trust company, or provincially regulated loan company. A purely offshore EMI may provide borderless multi-currency accounts and virtual IBANs to the LP for e-commerce receipts and cross-border settlements, but those arrangements are legally treated as unregulated payment services and carry higher counterparty, operational, and regulatory risk. To operate as a money services business (MSB) in Canada, any EMI entity with a Canadian nexus must be registered with FINTRAC and comply with the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). Recommended structure: use a FINTRAC-registered Canadian MSB/fintech (e.g., Wealthsimple, Koho, or a Payments Canada participant) for domestic CAD clearing, while using a top-tier Schedule I bank for primary treasury operations. The LP should not rely on an offshore EMI as the principal liquidity rail for trade settlements or capital escrow, as such rails lack Canadian deposit insurance and direct access to the Large Value Transfer System (LVTS)/Real-Time Rail (RTR).

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

The Ontario limited partnership may receive capital contributions from non-resident partners without exchange control approval because Canada imposes no foreign investment restrictions on an LP. For a compliant SWIFT remittance, the sending bank must use the appropriate cross-border wire message format (MT103 for single beneficiary; MT202 COV when a cover payment is provided). The beneficiary bank in Canada must be identified with the full legal name of the LP, the partnership's Canadian routing/bank account number, and the LP's registered Ontario address. Purpose code should be 'Capital contribution to a limited partnership' under the OECD/OECD classification; for MT103 field 70 (Remittance Information), include the reference 'CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION—LP_CA_ON—ISIC/NAICS 7310/541810' and, where required, the external Legal Entity Identifier (LEI) of the LP. For tax transparency, the capital contribution must be recorded in the partnership's capital account; if made by a foreign partner by way of transfer of assets or IP rather than cash, a formal contribution-in-kind deed and a Canadian valuation report are required to protect arm's length treatment. The wire should include the beneficiary bank's BIC/SWIFT code and the receiving branch's institution number, but the most robust routing is through a US clearing intermediary (correspondent) as Canadian banks are not direct members of the Federal Reserve wire system. For USD capital contributions, use ABA routing via a US correspondent (e.g., RBC's New York agency, BMO Harris N.A. Chicago, TD Bank N.A. Portland, Maine) and then book via CAD TT via the Canadian domestic LVTS. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium. Offshore digital Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) generally do not hold a Canadian banking licence and cannot offer domestic Canadian-currency deposit-taking services unless licensed as a bank, trust company, or provincially regulated loan company. A purely offshore EMI may provide borderless multi-currency accounts and virtual IBANs to the LP for e-commerce receipts and cross-border settlements, but those arrangements are legally treated as unregulated payment services and carry higher counterparty, operational, and regulatory risk. To operate as a money services business (MSB) in Canada, any EMI entity with a Canadian nexus must be registered with FINTRAC and comply with the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). Recommended structure: use a FINTRAC-registered Canadian MSB/fintech (e.g., Wealthsimple, Koho, or a Payments Canada participant) for domestic CAD clearing, while using a top-tier Schedule I bank for primary treasury operations. The LP should not rely on an offshore EMI as the principal liquidity rail for trade settlements or capital escrow, as such rails lack Canadian deposit insurance and direct access to the Large Value Transfer System (LVTS)/Real-Time Rail (RTR).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1 million (goods) / CAD 500,000 (services) / CAD 250,000 (intangibles) for cross-border transactions with non-arm's length parties

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have specific Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) like offshore jurisdictions; however, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) and substance-over-form principles apply. CRA may scrutinize transactions lacking economic substance.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Criminal penalties under the Income Tax Act: fines up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors and partners may be personally liable for unpaid taxes and penalties if they knowingly participated in the evasion; travel bans may be imposed.
Shell Company

Statutory sanction: CRA may apply GAAR and impose a 50% penalty on the tax benefit; reassessment of taxes with interest.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for corporate taxes if they signed returns or directed the scheme; civil restitution may be ordered.
AML Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1 million for individuals and CAD 2 million for entities; criminal penalties for willful non-compliance.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for penalties if they failed to implement compliance measures; criminal liability if they knowingly participated.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Penalty of 10% of the net adjustment to tax, plus interest on the reassessment.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for penalties if they knowingly participated in the non-compliance; CRA may also impose gross negligence penalties.
