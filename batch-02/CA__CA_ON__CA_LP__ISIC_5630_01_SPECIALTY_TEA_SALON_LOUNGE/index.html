Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) - GST/HST Registration via Business Registration Online (BRO) portal (https://www.canada.ca/en/services/taxes/gst-hst.html); Ontario Ministry of Finance for provincial tax filings and ONT-TAX-WEB portal; Ontario Business Registry for extra-provincial registration of the limited partnership.

E-government filing portal

Non-resident general partner must appoint an Ontario-resident attorney for service and file Form 1 (Registration of an Extra-Provincial Limited Partnership) with the Ontario Business Registry. For GST/HST, the partnership must register through the CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) portal using Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) or online incorporation/registration; the Business Number (BN) with the RT (GST/HST) program account is issued immediately for online filings. The partnership's general partner (resident or non-resident) executes a partnership declaration; if no Canadian-resident partner exists, a nominee resident agent is required. Once the BN is issued, the partnership must file GST/HST returns on a monthly, quarterly, or annual basis depending on total annual worldwide taxable revenues. Identity verification requires: partnership agreement, certificate of limited partnership from home jurisdiction, proof of Ontario attorney for service, and identification of all partners (name, address, residency status, tax ID).

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a US-style check-the-box regime; federal classification of a limited partnership is governed by the Income Tax Act (subdivision j of Division B). Available statutory elections include: (1) partnership fiscal period election under s. 249.1(4)-(5) selecting a non-calendar fiscal period for Canadian GST/HST and income tax filings; (2) optional CRA Form GST25 (Election to have a fiscal year that is not a calendar year) for GST/HST purposes; (3) joint election under s. 152(4) Income Tax Act to waive or extend the normal reassessment period (waiver election); (4) for partnerships with non-resident partners, election under s. 216 to file a special return on deemed taxable Canadian income from certain property to avoid Part XIII withholding on income from Canadian real property; (5) GST/HST partnership attribution election under ETA s. 273 allowing the partnership to be registered as a single entity without separate legal personality recognition; (6) election to have the GST/HST net tax calculation use the quick method (GST Form GST74) if annual taxable supplies do not exceed the CRA threshold.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies (including those of associated persons) in any single calendar quarter or over the prior four consecutive calendar quarters; if exceeded, GST/HST registration is mandatory. For a limited partnership the threshold applies to the partnership aggregate. Zero-rated export supplies count toward the threshold; financial services and most real property sales are excluded from the registration threshold calculation.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies to: (i) goods exported from Canada by the supplier or a subsequent purchaser under ETA s. 6 and Schedule VI Part V; (ii) services supplied to a non-resident provided the service is for a business or consumption outside Canada and, where the non-resident is not registered, the 'outside Canada' test and 'not in the course of a business carried on in Canada' test are satisfied (ETA s. 9, Schedule VI Part V); (iii) tea products physically exported and accompanied by commercial shipping documents, provided title passes to a non-resident purchaser outside Canada; (iv) intangible property (e.g., franchising intellectual property for international tea salon branding) supplied to a non-resident. The supplier must retain evidence that the goods were exported within 60 days of the sale, or that the services were performed and delivered outside Canada. Domestic retail sales in the Ontario lounge are taxable at 13% HST; zero-rating applies only to export orders and qualifying offshore services (such as international tea consultancy, cultural workshop licensing to non-residents).

  • Bill of Lading (through bill of lading / air waybill showing consignor and consignee, full shipping route, and export destination)
  • Commercial invoice describing the loose-leaf tea products, quantities, unit prices, HST treatment, and exporter's Business Number

  • Inward Remittance Certificate / bank transfer or SWIFT confirmation evidencing that the non-resident purchaser remitted payment in foreign currency to the partnership's Canadian bank account
  • For services: executed service agreement with non-resident client, delivery records, and written confirmation that the service recipient consumes the benefit outside Canada

Canada mandates no real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or government pre-approval of invoices; GST/HST invoices may be issued electronically in any format, subject to CRA record-keeping requirements (6 years). Registrants whose annual taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 must provide on each invoice: supplier's Business Number, invoice date, vendor name, purchaser name, and tax amount. The Pan-Canadian PEPPOL e-invoicing standard is mandatory only for suppliers transacting with the Federal Government of Canada (Shared Services Canada / Public Services and Procurement Canada) for procurement above certain thresholds; it is voluntary for B2B transactions. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically via CRA GST/HST NETFILE, My Business Account, or approved third-party software; payments are remitted electronically. No digital stamp or fiscal API clearance is required for retail point-of-sale tea salon operations, although Ontario requires POS receipts to display the HST amount separately.

The CRA applies an export audit profile for food-and-beverage venues claiming zero-rating; auditors scrutinize whether goods actually left Canada and whether the 180-day payment-receipt benchmark is met; uncollected or repatriated foreign currency within 180 days raises a presumption that the supply was for domestic consumption. Penalties: (1) ETA s. 285 false-statement penalty of 25% of any tax assessed on zero-rated exports found to be domestic taxable supplies, increased to 50% for repeat violations; (2) ETA s. 280 failure-to-file penalty of 1% of net tax due plus 0.25% per full month, up to 12 months; (3) ETA s. 281 failure to remit penalty; (4) gross negligence penalties under ETA s. 285.1 of up to 50% of the understated net tax where the exporter knowingly or under circumstances of gross negligence made false statements; (5) clawback of refunds or input tax credits previously claimed, with compound interest (CRA prescribed rate, revised quarterly) running from the date of the original refund; (6) for fraudulent zero-rating claims, s. 327(2) prosecution liability with fines of up to 200% of the tax evaded, plus imprisonment up to 5 years; (7) s. 239 Income Tax Act penalties for tax evasion where the partnership or partners deliberately misstate export documentation, including monetary penalties and criminal record; (8) directors' and general partners' personal civil liability for unremitted or incorrectly refunded GST/HST under ETA s. 323 where the general partner corporation is deemed negligent; (9) six-year record-keeping mandate (s. 286 ETA), and failure to produce export evidence results in automatic reassessment of the full HST plus interest and penalties.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada has a freely convertible Canadian dollar and generally no exchange controls, capital controls, mandatory repatriation regime, or general approval requirement for inbound or outbound corporate foreign-exchange transactions. Current-account payments, capital contributions, intercompany loans, dividends, interest, royalties, service fees, and trade payments may generally be settled through Canadian banks subject to sanctions, anti-money-laundering, tax, transfer-pricing, beneficial-ownership, and source-of-funds controls. Capital account transactions are generally unrestricted, but non-resident investment structures may be reviewed under the Investment Canada Act, competition legislation, sanctions law, tax rules, and lender-specific compliance procedures. Profit remittances from an Ontario limited partnership are generally not subject to an exchange-control approval; tax consequences may include Canadian withholding tax on certain payments to non-residents, partnership allocation and filing obligations, and documentation requirements under applicable income-tax treaties.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada does not impose a statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement, mandatory conversion ratio, or general deadline for converting export receipts into Canadian dollars. Export receipts may remain in foreign-currency accounts or be converted at the entity's discretion. Exporters must retain commercially credible invoices, customs records, shipping evidence, contracts, and payment records and must comply with Canadian sanctions, customs, tax, anti-money-laundering, and reporting obligations. Financial institutions may request transaction-purpose information and supporting documents under their risk-based compliance programs.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada, with foreign-exchange policy and financial-sector oversight responsibilities supplemented by the Department of Finance Canada, the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), the Canada Border Services Agency, and applicable provincial securities regulators including the Ontario Securities Commission

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for supplementary payment functionality and Low to Medium as a sole banking substitute. Canada does not provide a general European-style electronic-money-institution passport. Offshore digital banks or electronic-money institutions cannot automatically provide Canadian deposit-taking or payment services. The provider must rely on an appropriately authorized Canadian bank or payment-service arrangement, and the customer must verify whether the provider is registered under the Retail Payment Activities Act framework, supervised as a foreign money services business where applicable, or otherwise permitted to conduct the relevant activity. Offshore wallets may create increased risk involving client-money segregation, settlement finality, Canadian-dollar access, sanctions screening, beneficial-ownership verification, tax reporting, data residency, insolvency protection, and restrictions on accepting deposits or holding funds for Canadian residents. The Ontario LP should maintain a Canadian bank as its primary operating and payroll account and use an offshore platform only after confirming licensing, contractual safeguarding, withdrawal rights, correspondent-bank identity, and Canadian regulatory perimeter.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Route capital contributions and partner loans through an account held in the exact legal name of the Ontario limited partnership or its authorized general partner, using SWIFT MT103 or an equivalent bank wire with complete originator, beneficiary, purpose, and intermediary-bank data. Canada has no universal statutory FX purpose-code regime for ordinary corporate wires; the bank may nevertheless require a transaction-purpose classification such as partner capital contribution, shareholder loan, intercompany loan, or operating funding. The remitter should use consistent legal-name and account details and avoid personal or unrelated third-party accounts. Maintain the executed contribution or loan instrument, partner approval, source-of-funds evidence, remitter ownership information, bank advice, and ledger entry. Reconcile the incoming wire to the partnership books and preserve records for tax, AML, audit, and bank-review purposes. For material or unusual flows, obtain pre-clearance from the relationship bank and confirm sanctions, beneficial-ownership, transfer-pricing, thin-capitalization, interest-withholding, and partnership-allocation implications.Use domestic EFT, wire transfer, or bank bill-payment rails for Canadian suppliers and SWIFT MT103 for international suppliers. For higher-value or documentary transactions, use a commercial letter of credit, documentary collection, standby letter of credit, or bank guarantee subject to bank credit approval and applicable International Chamber of Commerce rules such as UCP 600, ISP98, or URC 522. Match each payment to an approved purchase order, invoice, contract, customs or shipping record where relevant, and supplier due-diligence file. For card and point-of-sale receipts, settle through a Canadian-acquiring bank or regulated payment processor into the operating account, with chargeback, refund, reserve, and merchant-dispute controls. Separate supplier payments, payroll, taxes, partner distributions, and intercompany transfers in the accounting system.
