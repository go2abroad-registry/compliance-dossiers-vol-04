Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Bifurcated dual authority: (1) Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) for Part I federal corporate income tax, GST/HST, and customs liaison, accessed via My Business Account and Business Registration Online; (2) Revenu Québec for Québec Sales Tax (QST/TVQ) and Québec corporate income tax, accessed via Revenu Québec 'Clic SÉcurisé'. The corporate identity registry is the Registraire des entreprises du Québec, which issues the Québec Enterprise Number (NEQ); all CRA tax accounts are consolidated under the 9-digit Business Number (BN).

E-government filing portal

Step-sequential SOP for a foreign-owned Quebec Business Corporation: (i) incorporate or continue a share corporation under the Canada Business Corporations Act or Quebec Business Corporations Act, obtain the NEQ from the Registraire des entreprises, and obtain the 9-digit CRA Business Number automatically via Business Registration Online (BRO); (ii) register as a GST/HST registrant with CRA using Form RC1 and as a QST registrant with Revenu Québec using Form LM-1-V / Clic SÉcurisé once taxable worldwide supplies exceed the C$30,000 small-supplier threshold (mandatory for an export grain-trading concern); (iii) open program accounts (RC for T2 corporate tax, RP for GST/HST, RM for QST) and add the CBSA Import/Export program account in the CARM portal for HS 1201.90 (soybeans) and HS 1005.90 (corn) cross-border shipments; (iv) file the T2 (federal) and CO-17 (Quebec) income tax returns annually, maintaining a Canadian registered office and the Quebec resident-director requirement (at least 25% of directors must be resident Canadians); (v) appoint a resident representative/mandataire for non-resident shareholders and file applicable treaty or Part XIV branch-remittance elections; (vi) maintain a contemporaneous compliance file evidencing NEQ, BN, RQ registration certificates, and bank-beneficiary records for USD/MXN-denominated grain settlements.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation. A CBCA/BCA business corporation is a separate statutory taxpayer resident in Canada, subject to Part I federal income tax and Quebec income tax on worldwide income, with foreign tax credits for foreign-source business income. Grain brokerage commissions and export-resale profits are ordinary active business income taxed at entity level; there is no pass-through of income or credits to shareholders. Distributed profits are separately subject to Part XIII non-resident dividend withholding tax where applicable. The corporation is also an independent GST/HST/QST registrant; no look-through treatment applies.

Available election forms

Canada has no US-style check-the-box or hybrid entity classification election; every Canadian corporation is automatically an entity-level taxpayer. Available statutory elections relevant to a grain-trading corporation include: (i) functional-currency reporting election under s. 261 Income Tax Act (ITA) for a USD-based export trader; (ii) GST/HST closely-related-corporations election under s. 156 Excise Tax Act (ETA) to file consolidated group returns for grain-handling subsidiaries; (iii) non-calendar fiscal-period election under s. 249.1(1)(d) ITA; (iv) capital cost allowance and straight-line depreciation elections under s. 13(29) ITA and the CCA regulations; (v) eligible-dividend designation enabling the enhanced Quebec dividend tax credit; (vi) s. 83(2) capital dividend account election; (vii) s. 216 election for non-residents with Canadian rental or timber income (rare in this trade); (viii) controlled-foreign-affiliate designations under s. 95(2) ITA and foreign-affiliate 'dumping' disallowance elections under s. 212.3 ITA, which do not change entity status but contain hybrid-mismatch implications; and (ix) Quebec mirror elections under ss. 306-309 of the Taxation Act, filed with Revenu Québec.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

C$30,000. The GST/HST small-supplier threshold under s. 148 ETA and the identical QST threshold under s. 25 of the Act respecting the Québec sales tax are both C$30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies (including supplies of associated persons) in a single calendar quarter or in the four preceding calendar quarters. Bulk-grain export brokers and buyers/resellers virtually always cross this threshold. Even below the threshold, voluntary registration permits the recovery of GST/QST paid on freight, demurrage, inspection, and brokerage inputs. Commissions earned for arranging export vessel sales count as taxable brokerage services for threshold purposes unless zero-rated as services supplied to non-residents in respect of exported goods.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A Quebec grain-trading corporation may apply the 0% rate to its export supplies under two distinct statutory routes: (i) supply of bulk agricultural commodities (soybeans, yellow corn, other cereal grains) is zero-rated as a basic grocery under Part III of Schedule VI to the ETA and under the parallel QST basic-foodstuff provisions, provided the goods are food for human consumption; and (ii) irrespective of classification, tangible personal property exported by or on behalf of a registrant is zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI / s. 142 ETA where the goods leave Canada as part of the supply and are not consumed or delivered in Canada. A disclosed vessel-brokerage or commission agency service may additionally be zero-rated under s. 7, Part V, Schedule VI (ETA) where the recipient is a non-resident and the service is supplied for the benefit of that non-resident, and is in respect of tangible personal property exported from Canada. The exporter or service provider must hold contemporaneous documentary proof of export: ownership transfer to a non-resident, physical shipment via ocean or cross-border carrier within a commercially reasonable time, foreign-currency settlement from the non-resident buyer, and no subsequent diversion back into Canada. Commercial grain shipments ordinarily require a Canadian Grain Commission official inspection and weighing certificate for bulk vessels, though such certificate is an industry quality/quantity instrument rather than a tax-condition to zero-rating.

  • Commercial invoice in triplicate showing supplier BN, Revenu Québec QST number, HS 1201.90 / 1005.90 commodity description, explicit 'Zero-rated export supply' notation, quantity, gross/net weight, and destination to the non-resident buyer
  • CBSA B13A Export Declaration (or equivalent electronic data transmitted through the Canadian Export Reporting System / CARM portal, filed for all commercial shipments exceeding C$2,000 or 10 metric tonnes)
  • Bill of Lading (ocean or through-bill) naming the Quebec exporter as shipper, a non-resident consignee, port of loading, port of discharge, and vessel name/IMO number, endorsed to evidence transfer of title outside Canada
  • Charter party or freight contract confirming the international fixture and the foreign destination of the bulk cargo
  • Canadian Grain Commission Official Inspection Certificate and Official Weight Certificate for the export parcel, evidencing grade, moisture, and quantity loaded
  • Inward remittance evidence: SWIFT MT-103 credit advice, irrevocable transferable letter of credit advising, or bank payment confirmation demonstrating USD or other foreign-currency settlement by the foreign buyer
  • Where applicable, US import clearance documentation (CBP Form 7501) or the Canada-US-Mexico Agreement entry papers for cross-border rail/overland grain movements to the United States

Canada and Quebec impose no real-time fiscal clearance, no continuous transaction control (CTC), no mandatory API pre-clearance, and no digital stamping regime as of the current date. CRA is developing a voluntary PEPPOL-standard federal e-invoicing service for B2B transactions, with adoption anticipated no earlier than 2026; Revenu Québec permits fully electronic QST invoicing without prior authorization. Prescribed invoice-content rules apply: supplier BN and QST registration number, buyer name and address, date, description, quantity, consideration, applicable GST/QST rate or explicit zero-rated indication, and a clear export-documentation cross-reference. Foreign-currency invoices for export grain are acceptable if converted at the Bank of Canada noon exchange rate. Books, records, bills of lading, and export documents must be retained in Canada or be accessible electronically to CRA and Revenu Québec on demand.

CRA and Revenu Québec may reassess within the ordinary limitation periods (4 years federally for income tax and GST/HST; 3 years for Quebec income tax under the Taxation Act; 4 years for QST), extended to 10 years (and unlimited for fraud or wilful misconduct) where misrepresentation, neglect, carelessness, or wilful default is present. Substantiation failures trigger: (i) clawback of the zero-rating and reassessment of the full 14.975% GST/QST on the gross invoice amount; (ii) denial or reversal of input tax credits and input tax refunds claimed on freight and brokerage inputs; (iii) gross-negligence penalties of 25% of understated tax under federal s. 163(2) ITA and Quebec s. 1049 Taxation Act; (iv) GST/QST false-statement penalties under s. 327 ETA of 50% of evaded tax (minimum C$100) and QST equivalents; (v) daily compounded prescribed-rate interest on unremitted amounts from the date the return was due; (vi) director and agent joint-and-several liability for unremitted GST/QST under s. 323 ETA; and (vii) criminal prosecution for wilful evasion under s. 239 ITA / s. 327.1 ETA, with fines up to 200% of the tax evaded. Canadian law imposes no statutory 180-day export-proceeds repatriation deadline; the 180-day benchmark adopted in this dossier represents the documentary-event deadline for LC negotiation and the transfer-pricing comparability buffer that CRA and Revenu Québec use when testing intercompany grain-export payment terms; a longer settlement period requires contemporaneous commercial justification and appropriate interest imputation. Both tax authorities cross-check exporter zero-rating claims against CBSA export declarations, Canadian Grain Commission cargo data, carrier manifests, and foreign-bank remittance advices.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility on both current and capital account. Canada abolished foreign exchange controls in 1951 and imposes no central bank approval or license requirement for foreign exchange transactions, profit repatriation, or dividend remittance. A Quebec business corporation may freely pay dividends abroad, subject only to Part XIII withholding tax of 25% (statutory) reduced to 5%–15% under income tax treaties (e.g., Canada–U.S. treaty dividends), and to corporate solvency tests under the Canada Business Corporations Act / Québec Business Corporations Act. No 'convertibility gap' exists for CAD–USD flows; bank market rates apply.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canadian law imposes no mandatory surrender, conversion, or repatriation timeline for export proceeds or brokerage commission receipts in foreign currency. Exporters and brokers may retain USD/CAD balances in Canadian or offshore accounts. However, every cross-border EFT of CAD 10,000 or more must be reported to FINTRAC by the financial institution (Cross-Border Wire Transfer Report), and Statistics Canada's BOP reporting may apply to large transactions. For grain brokerage, the Canadian Grain Commission does not require surrender of proceeds.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) — monetary authority; FX regulatory administration is distributed among the Department of Finance Canada, the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for chartered bank prudential supervision, and FINTRAC for AML/CFT wire-reporting. Canada has no dedicated exchange-control authority.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Medium — viable as a secondary settlement rail only. Offshore and Canadian fintech EMIs (Wise, Airwallex, Payoneer) operate in Canada as money services businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC; they are not CDIC-insured banks, cannot access Lynx (LVTS) in their own name, and typically clear CAD through partner chartered banks. Multi-currency virtual IBAN solutions are useful for collecting small brokerage commissions and paying lighter vendor counterparties, but the primary corporate treasury and LC issuance must remain with a Schedule I chartered bank. Legal constraint: an EMI cannot issue commercial LCs or true foreign exchange forwards requiring a banking counterparty credit line.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined general corporate rate: 15% federal plus 11.5% Quebec. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access a combined rate of approximately 12.2% on eligible active business income within the applicable small-business limit, subject to federal and Quebec eligibility rules and business-limit reductions.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Canadian domestic law on taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident. Under the Canada-China income tax treaty, the rate is generally reduced to 10% where the beneficial owner is a company owning at least 10% of the voting power, and 15% in other treaty cases, subject to treaty conditions and anti-abuse rules. Other treaties may provide 5%, 10%, or 15% rates depending on ownership and treaty provisions.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general monetary threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is automatically unnecessary. Under section 247 of the Income Tax Act, documentation must support the arm's-length nature of non-arm's-length cross-border transactions and must generally be prepared by the documentation deadline. Form T106 reporting is generally triggered where the aggregate reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents exceed CAD 1,000,000, subject to statutory exclusions and filing rules. Canada does not impose a separate universal statutory Master File or Local File threshold equivalent to some jurisdictions; OECD-style local-file and master-file documentation is prudent for material controlled transactions, and Country-by-Country reporting applies to qualifying multinational groups with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a standalone Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to the United Arab Emirates or certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance is assessed through Canadian transfer-pricing rules, the principal-purpose and treaty anti-abuse rules, the general anti-avoidance rule, beneficial-ownership principles, specific anti-avoidance provisions, and judicial analysis of the actual commercial relationship. A grain-trading corporation should maintain Canadian personnel, decision-making records, inventory or title-risk evidence where it acts as principal, customer and supplier contracts, financing records, market-risk controls, banking substance, and contemporaneous evidence supporting its functions, assets, and risks.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Corporate income tax evasion

Statutory sanction: Civil reassessment of tax, arrears interest, gross-negligence penalty generally equal to 50% of the understated tax or improperly claimed benefit, and possible prosecution. Tax evasion convictions may result in fines ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to five years under the Income Tax Act.

Director personal liability: Directors may face criminal liability for participation, authorization, acquiescence, or wilful blindness; personal exposure can include fines, imprisonment, director liability for unremitted payroll or source deductions, and court-ordered restitution. Ordinary corporate tax debts do not automatically pierce the corporate veil.
False statements and gross negligence

Statutory sanction: Civil penalties may apply for false statements or omissions, including the greater of a statutory minimum and 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit, together with interest and reassessment.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may incur personal criminal exposure where they knowingly participate in the false filing. Professional advisers may also face penalties where statutory requirements are met.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: Primary or secondary transfer-pricing adjustments, denial of deductions, withholding-tax reassessments, interest, and a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where reasonable efforts and documentation requirements are not satisfied.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure for knowingly participating in false records or tax evasion. The corporation remains primarily liable for reassessed tax and penalties.
Treaty abuse and beneficial-ownership failure

Statutory sanction: Withholding at 25%, reassessment of underwithheld tax, payer liability, interest, penalties, and denial of treaty relief under beneficial-ownership and anti-abuse principles.

Director personal liability: Responsible directors and officers may be assessed for source deductions in cases involving failure to remit or statutory director liability, and may face criminal sanctions for deliberate false certifications.
Failure to remit payroll or non-resident withholding

Statutory sanction: The payer may remain liable for the unremitted amount, plus interest and late-remittance penalties. Repeated or deliberate failures may result in enhanced penalties and prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions under the Income Tax Act where the statutory conditions are met. Travel restrictions are not automatic but may follow enforcement proceedings or criminal orders.
GST/HST fraud and input-tax-credit abuse

Statutory sanction: Assessment of unpaid GST/HST, denial of input tax credits, interest, gross-negligence penalties, criminal prosecution, and potential seizure or collection action.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for certain unremitted GST/HST amounts and may face criminal liability for fraud or knowingly false claims.
Shell company and sham transaction

Statutory sanction: Denial of deductions and treaty benefits, GAAR reassessment, transfer-pricing adjustments, withholding-tax liability, penalties, interest, and potential criminal fraud or tax-evasion charges.

Director personal liability: Civil restitution, asset tracing, director and officer liability for knowing participation, criminal fines, imprisonment, and possible prohibition from acting as a corporate director. Travel bans are not an automatic tax penalty and generally require judicial or enforcement authority.
Anti-money-laundering and proceeds-of-crime risk

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, seizure and forfeiture of proceeds, account restrictions, and criminal prosecution under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and Criminal Code.

Director personal liability: Individuals may face substantial fines, imprisonment, asset forfeiture, director disqualification, and court-imposed restrictions. Travel bans may arise from criminal bail, sentencing, or forfeiture proceedings but are not automatic.
Sanctions, export-control, and trade-compliance breach

Statutory sanction: Asset freezes, transaction prohibitions, administrative or criminal penalties under Canadian sanctions legislation, forfeiture, and imprisonment for serious violations.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and compliance personnel may be prosecuted for authorization, consent, participation, or wilful blindness. Personal fines, imprisonment, asset seizure, and judicial travel restrictions may apply.
Corporate record and beneficial-ownership non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Corporate-law offences, regulatory penalties, loss of good standing, audit escalation, and potential criminal charges where records are deliberately falsified.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be fined or prosecuted and may face personal liability for false records or obstruction. Corporate veil piercing requires exceptional facts such as fraud, sham conduct, or improper use of the corporation.
