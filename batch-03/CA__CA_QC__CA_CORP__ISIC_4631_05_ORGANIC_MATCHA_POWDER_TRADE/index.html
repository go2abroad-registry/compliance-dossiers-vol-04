Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html; Revenu Québec – https://www.revenuquebec.ca/en/

E-government filing portal

Non-resident entities carrying on business in Canada must apply for a CRA Business Number (BN) via the Business Registration Online (BRO) platform or by completing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number). For GST/HST registration, a non-resident may register voluntarily under Division V of the Excise Tax Act. For QST, a non-resident must also register with Revenu Québec (Form LM-1 or online via Revenu Québec’s Business Registration Service), typically requiring a Quebec establishment or an agent, and must obtain a QST registration number. Prior to registration, the entity must have a Canadian-representative appointment for service of process if no place of business in Canada.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation (Canadian Business Corporation is a resident taxpayer if incorporated in Canada or managed/controlled in Canada; subject to Part I federal income tax and Quebec corporate tax under the Taxation Act (Quebec)).

Available election forms

No check-the-box election exists for Canadian federal tax purposes. A corporation may elect under subsection 216(1) of the Income Tax Act to be taxed on net Canadian-source employment income, but this is irrelevant for a trading corporation. Hybrid mismatch elections under OECD BEPS Action 2 are not applicable as domestic law follows the nexus approach. For GST/HST, a non-resident may elect to use the simplified 'quick method' for small suppliers; for QST, an election for the 'general rate' may apply to certain taxable benefits, but no deemed classification election analogous to the U.S. check-the-box is available.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (GST/HST and QST small-supplier thresholds); once exceeded, registration is mandatory.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of tangible personal property (including organic matcha powder) are zero-rated under section 39 of the Excise Tax Act (GST/HST) and equivalent provisions of Quebec’s QST legislation, provided the goods are exported within a reasonable time, usually within 60 days of sale, by the supplier or the purchaser, and the supplier retains documentary proof of export. Services are zero-rated if performed for a non-resident who is not registered for GST/HST/QST and the service is not rendered to a recipient physically present in Canada.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada Border Services Agency Form B13A / Goods to Follow list)
  • Bill of Lading (or air waybill) showing Canadian origin and foreign destination
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (or bank statement evidencing receipt of foreign currency payment)

No real-time fiscal clearance or digital stamping mandate currently applies federally or in Quebec. However, GST/HST registrants with annual taxable supplies above CAD 10 million must file GST/HST returns electronically; Quebec imposes mandatory electronic filing for QST returns for registrants with annual taxable supplies exceeding CAD 1,500,000. Standard electronic data interchange (EDI) or XML-based invoicing is accepted, but no authorized clearance portal, API submission, or Hash-based stamping regime exists for the ordinary sale of goods.

CRA and Revenu Québec may deny zero-rating and reassess GST/HST and QST along with penalties and interest. The Excise Tax Act imposes a penalty of 10% of the net tax owing on failure to remit, plus interest; false statements or omissions may trigger a $8,000 or 50% of the tax sought to be evaded penalty under subsection 285.1(1) ETA. Quebec’s Tax Administration Act similarly authorizes penalties of 10%–25% of the amount evaded and retroactive assessments up to four years (seven years in cases of negligence, wilful default, or fraud). Evidence of fraudulent export documentation or sham repatriation also leads to criminal prosecution under the Income Tax Act and Criminal Code, and clawback of refunds received under section 296 ETA.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible on both current and capital accounts. There are no exchange-control licences, no prior approvals for capital injections, no restrictions on dividend or profit remittance to non-resident shareholders, and no requirement to justify foreign-currency inflows to the central bank. Legal obligations on remittance are limited to tax at source under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (generally 25% on dividends paid to non-residents, subject to treaty reduction), Quebec-specific corporate formalities, and FINTRAC Large Cash Transaction or Suspicious Transaction Reporting above prescribed thresholds.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory timeline for repatriation, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no surrender of export receipts. A Quebec matcha distribution corporation may retain foreign currency in a permissible foreign-currency operating account, convert it at negotiated bank rates, or net positions subject to general tax accounting. Export proceeds remain taxable in Canada and foreign exchange gains or losses must be recorded according to IFRS or ASPE in Canadian-dollar functional statements.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as the central monetary authority; Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) as prudential regulator of federally chartered deposit-taking institutions; Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) as the financial intelligence unit and AML authority. Canada does not operate a dedicated foreign exchange control bureau; the Canadian dollar trades on a free float and there is no central exchange-control approval regime.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium. A Quebec corporation may use fintech electronic money institution (EMI) interfaces, but in Canada such providers must operate either as a FINTRAC-registered money services business (MSB), a payment service provider under an regulated Canadian bank partnership, or a non-bank financial institution appropriately authorized by provincial law. Unregulated offshore EMIs are not viable as a primary treasury host for SWIFT and trade settlements because Canadian banks will still require a direct relationship for clearing, trade documentation, and FX risk management. The optimal strategy is to use a Schedule I bank as the settlement spine and optionally connect a FINTRAC-compliant fintech to initiate payment requests or operational dashboards.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Route inbound shareholder capital or related-party loans via SWIFT MT103 to the Quebec corporation's operating or capital account at a Canadian Schedule I bank. The wire should state the beneficiary account number and SWIFT BIC, and Field 70 or the payment narrative must include the reference 'Capital injection – ISIC 4631 organic matcha distribution' or similar clear purpose, because Canada does not require a government-issued exchange-control code but banks and FINTRAC rely on the narrative to assess legitimacy. If the inbound remittance exceeds CAD 10,000 in cash equivalents or triggers suspicion, the bank is obliged to file a Large Cash Transaction Report or Suspicious Transaction Report. No prior central-bank approval is required. All contributions should be recorded in corporate accounts and reflected in the Quebec enterprise register as either paid-in share capital or an intercompany liability.Supplier payments for matcha procurement should be settled through SWIFT MT103 customer credit transfers for standard commercial invoices and open-account terms, or through a commercial documentary Letter of Credit (LC) under UCP 600 and ISBP 745 for larger, recurring purchase programs. A Canadian bank will issue or confirm the LC after reviewing the trade contract, LC mandate, and credit limit. For domestic or North American settlements, Payments Canada provides clearing in Canadian dollars; for USD, the Canadian bank uses its U.S. correspondent through Fedwire or CHIPS. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Form T106 (Information Return for Non-Arm's Length Transactions with Non-Residents) is mandatory if the aggregate value of reportable non-arm's-length cross-border transactions with each non-resident is greater than C$1,000,000 per fiscal year. Contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation (Local File/Master File, OECD-aligned under CRA administrative practice) must be prepared and retained by taxpayers with total group revenue above C$50,000,000 and should be updated before the T2 filing deadline.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no formal Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) statute of the type used in offshore financial centres; however, substance is enforced through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR), the concept of central management and control for corporate residence, and treaty anti-abuse clauses. A Quebec corporation importing and distributing matcha powder must maintain real decision-making, staff, premises and risk functions in Canada; a passive shell with management and books outside Canada may be deemed to be a non-resident or a sham and denied treaty relief.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Wilful Misstatement

Statutory sanction: Gross-negligence penalty under Income Tax Act s.163(2): 50% of the federal tax understated; criminal tax evasion under s.239(1) carries a fine of up to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment up to five years. Quebec administrative penalties mirror federal treatment and add provincial interest.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly direct or assist the misstatement are parties to the offence under s.242 of the Income Tax Act; they face personal criminal liability, fines, restitution orders, and imprisonment without corporate-veil protection. Travel bans can be imposed as bail/court conditions during prosecution.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA may re-determine corporate residence under the central-management-and-control test and GAAR, deny treaty/Limited Liability benefits, reassess income in the hands of the true beneficial owner, and levy Part XIII withholding on deemed dividends. Persistent non-compliance leads to administrative dissolution under the Quebec Business Corporations Act and loss of registration with the Registraire des entreprises.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false statutory declarations or permit unlawful dissolution are jointly liable for unpaid corporate income tax, GST/QST and source deductions; courts may impose asset-freezing orders, travel bans and orders requiring personal restitution before the corporate veil can be lifted.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing

Statutory sanction: CRA adjusts the transaction to arm's-length under Income Tax Act s.247(2) and imposes a transfer-pricing penalty under s.247(3) equal to 10% of the gross adjustment. The taxpayer also faces T106 late/incomplete information-return penalties up to C$2,500 per year plus interest.

Director personal liability: Directors may be assessed personally for tax liabilities arising from deliberate transfer-pricing misrepresentation when they signed the T2/CO-17 or directed the pricing; criminal culpability is possible if the understatement is intentional, with fines and imprisonment.
AML / Financial Transaction Reporting

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, FINTRAC may impose administrative monetary penalties up to C$1,000,000 per noncompliance for a corporation; criminal money-laundering under Criminal Code s.462.31 carries imprisonment up to ten years.

Director personal liability: A director designated as a compliance officer can be personally fined for failure to implement an adequate anti-money-laundering program; courts may order travel bans and passport surrender where there is a flight risk, plus confiscation of illicit assets under the Criminal Code.
