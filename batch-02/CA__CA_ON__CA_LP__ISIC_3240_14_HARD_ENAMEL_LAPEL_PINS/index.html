Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); digital portal: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-businesses/business-account.html)

E-government filing portal

A non-resident Limited Partnership (LP) without a permanent establishment in Canada is not required to obtain a Canadian tax identification number for Canadian income tax purposes unless it carries on business in Canada. If the LP carries on business in Canada, it must register with the CRA through the Business Registration Online (BRO) service to obtain a 15-character Business Number (BN). A BN is required to register for GST/HST, payroll, and import/export accounts. For GST/HST purposes, the LP must register if it is a non-resident that carries on business in Canada and has gross annual worldwide taxable supplies exceeding CAD 30,000 (the threshold for non-residents, as opposed to CAD 30,000 for residents). The LP must file Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts) via BRO, CRA mail, or fax, providing the partnership's legal name, country of formation, Canadian agent details (if any), and fiscal period. The CRA will issue the BN and separate program accounts (e.g., RT for GST/HST, RP for payroll, RM for import/export). The LP must apply for a separate GST/HST account only if taxable supplies are made in Canada; a non-resident LP making zero-rated exports of goods from Canada must register to claim input tax credits, and a non-resident LP making taxable supplies outside Canada is not required to register. For Ontario provincial taxes, the LP must register with the Ontario Ministry of Finance under the Retail Sales Tax (RST) only if it is a vendor of taxable goods in Ontario under the Retail Sales Tax Act (RST is not applicable to the sale of tangible personal property by a non-resident where delivery occurs outside Ontario). The LP may also need a CRA Import/Export (RM) account to export goods; this is obtained simultaneously with the BN.

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible; territorial scope: Canadian-source business income of a non-resident LP is subject to Part I tax under the Income Tax Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. 1 (5th Supp.)) if the LP carries on business in Canada. A limited partnership is fiscally transparent for Canadian income tax purposes; income is allocated to partners based on the partnership agreement, and each partner is taxed on their share. Non-resident partners are subject to Canadian tax on Canadian-source business income (under section 115) at graduated rates; if the LP has a permanent establishment in Canada, the non-resident partner may also be subject to Part I tax on business income under section 2(3). A non-resident LP without a permanent establishment may still be taxable if it is deemed to carry on a business in Canada. No check-the-box election exists under Canadian domestic law (unlike U.S. check-the-box); entity classification is determined by statute and jurisprudence. The LP is not eligible for entity-level taxation; it is a pass-through entity under the Income Tax Act (subsection 96(1)). Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible; territorial scope: Canadian-source business income of a non-resident LP is subject to Part I tax under the Income Tax Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. 1 (5th Supp.)) if the LP carries on business in Canada. A limited partnership is fiscally transparent for Canadian income tax purposes; income is allocated to partners based on the partnership agreement, and each partner is taxed on their share. Non-resident partners are subject to Canadian tax on Canadian-source business income (under section 115) at graduated rates; if the LP has a permanent establishment in Canada, the non-resident partner may also be subject to Part I tax on business income under section 2(3). A non-resident LP without a permanent establishment may still be taxable if it is deemed to carry on a business in Canada. No check-the-box election exists under Canadian domestic law (unlike U.S. check-the-box); entity classification is determined by statute and jurisprudence. The LP is not eligible for entity-level taxation; it is a pass-through entity under the Income Tax Act (subsection 96(1)). There is no tax-exempt zone applicable for standard commercial manufacturing/export operations.

Available election forms

No check-the-box election mechanism exists in Canada. However, the following elections/mechanisms are relevant: (1) Section 216 election: A non-resident may elect to be taxed on net rental income from real property in Canada instead of gross withholding; not applicable to commercial sales income. (2) Section 219 election: Available to non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada through a branch, allowing payment of branch tax instead of Part I tax; not applicable to LPs as pass-through entities, but a non-resident corporate partner may use the branch tax mechanism if the LP is deemed to have a Canadian business. (3) GST/HST election under section 156 of the Excise Tax Act (ETA): A non-resident LP may elect to have quarterly filing instead of annual filing if it is a 'non-resident registrant' and its taxable supplies in Canada are less than CAD 1,000,000. (4) Election under subsection 150(2.1): An LP may elect to have a fiscal period other than calendar year, but must file an election with CRA. (5) Hybrid mismatch: Canada has not fully adopted OECD BEPS Action 2 hybrid mismatch rules; however, under section 96(2.1) of the Income Tax Act, a non-resident investor's share of income from a Canadian-resident LP is deemed to be income from carrying on business in Canada, and anti-avoidance rules in section 94B may apply to offshore investment fund interests; also, the 'foreign affiliate' rules (sections 90-95) may recharacterize certain hybrid instruments. No specific statutory election for hybrid mismatch treatment exists in Canadian law.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of annual worldwide gross taxable supplies (including supplies of goods and services made in Canada, and zero-rated exports) for non-resident persons; a non-resident that does not carry on business in Canada is deemed to be carrying on business if it solicits orders via an agent or through a permanent establishment. A non-resident LP that is a 'small supplier' (taxable supplies of CAD 30,000 or less) is not required to register, but may voluntarily register. A non-resident LP that makes taxable supplies in Canada and is not registered must charge and collect GST/HST when its supplies exceed the threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under section 1 of Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act, supplies of goods made by a registrant to a non-resident are zero-rated if the goods are exported by the vendor directly or are delivered to a common carrier for export, and the goods are exported as soon as reasonably possible. The LP must be a GST/HST registrant to claim zero-rating. For enamel lapel pins (HS 7326.20 or 7117.19; NAICS 339999), zero-rating applies to a supply of tangible personal property made to a non-resident purchaser if the property is exported in accordance with the conditions in sections 1-15 of Part V, Schedule VI: the vendor must maintain evidence that the goods were exported (bill of lading, customs export declaration, carrier receipt). For services related to offshore supplies (e.g., design services provided to a foreign customer), zero-rating applies under section 7 of Part V, Schedule VI if the service is supplied to a non-resident and is not in respect of real property located in Canada or tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time the service is performed. As the business scope is manufacturing/manufacture of enamel pins for export (Etsy sales to U.S./international customers), the LP should zero-rate all sales where delivery is to a foreign address and the goods leave Canada. However, if the LP sells to Canadian customers, HST at 13% applies. If the LP sells digital goods or services of a non-tangible nature via Etsy, the place of supply rules in section 142 of the ETA apply: for intangible personal property supplied to a consumer, the supply is deemed made in Canada if the consumer is a Canadian resident. The LP must obtain proof of export within the required period.

  • Canadian Export Declaration (CERS) – required for exports with a value exceeding CAD 2,000; for low-value shipments under CAD 2,000, provincially exempt, but the exporter must retain commercial invoice and shipping documents
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill (issued by Canada Post, UPS, FedEx, DHL, or a freight forwarder; must show exporter's name, consignee, destination, and description of goods)
  • Commercial Invoice (showing description, quantity, unit value, and statement 'GST/HST zero-rated export' with reference to Part V, Schedule VI, ETA)
  • Customs Entry Document (Canada Customs Invoice / Form B3, or CERS reporting acknowledgment) for shipments over CAD 2,000
  • Proof of export – includes carrier tracking history, customs stamp, warehouse receipt from a bonded facility, or Canadian Customs Coding Form (CA2) for supplies to non-residents
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (IRC) / Foreign Exchange Purchase Record – to evidence receipt of payment from a non-resident customer; in Canada an IRC is a bank-issued statement of funds received from abroad, used by CRA to verify the export transaction
  • Packing List
  • Certificate of Origin (for NAFTA/USMCA eligibility, if claiming preferential tariff treatment; not mandatory for zero-rating but recommended for U.S. export evidence)

Canada does NOT mandate real-time electronic invoicing or fiscal clearance for B2B/B2C transactions. The CRA does not require digital stamping, API-based clearance, or government pre-authorization of invoices. However, the CRA mandates that GST/HST registrants retain electronic records and issue invoices containing specific information: for a supply exceeding CAD 30, an invoice must show: (1) vendor's name and GST/HST registration number; (2) date of invoice; (3) description of goods/services; (4) consideration (total amount); and (5) the amount of tax or a statement that the tax is included. Electronic invoicing is permitted but must be in a readable and accessible format. There is no mandatory Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) or PEPPOL/BIS Billing network. For exports, the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) requires a Canadian Export Declaration (via the Canadian Export Reporting System, CERS) for all exports of goods with a value exceeding CAD 2,000; this is a customs clearance document, not an indirect tax clearance. No digital stamping or real-time fiscal clearance is applicable. Ontario's RST does not require electronic invoicing certification; Ontario RST applies to the sale of tangible personal property in Ontario, but the sale of enamel lapel pins by a non-resident to Ontario consumers is generally subject to HST, not RST, unless the vendor is an Ontario-based seller. For online marketplace transactions (e.g., Etsy), the platform may be deemed the supplier for HST purposes under the 'platform rules' effective July 1, 2021; a non-resident LP selling through Etsy to Canadian customers may rely on Etsy to collect and remit GST/HST if Etsy is the deemed supplier; however, the LP must still review if it has any direct sales in Canada.

Under the Excise Tax Act, a registrant that incorrectly claims zero-rating on an export may be assessed for the uncollected GST/HST. If the vendor fails to maintain sufficient evidence of export, CRA may, within a normal reassessment period (4 years from the date of the notice of assessment; 7 years in case of misrepresentation or fraud), reassess and collect the GST/HST at 13% (or 5% federal rate) that should have been charged, plus interest (section 280). Penalties include: (1) A penalty under section 280 of ETA for failure to collect tax, equal to 20% of the amount assessed; (2) A penalty under section 285 for knowingly making a false statement, equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 25% of the amount of tax sought to be evaded; (3) For conviction on indictment, a fine of up to CAD 200,000 and/or imprisonment of up to 5 years (section 327); (4) Third-party civil penalties under section 290. With respect to export documentation, subsection 169(3.1) and section 3 of Part V, Schedule VI require that the vendor obtain and retain an 'official proof of export' within 2 years of the supply. If the proof is not obtained within 2 years, CRA may disallow the zero-rating and assess tax. Clawback risk is high for E-commerce sales where shipping documentation is lost or incomplete; CRA frequently audits online sellers. Additionally, if the LP's Etsy sales are made via a marketplace (Etsy), the marketplace may be deemed to make the supply to the Canadian consumer, and Etsy will collect HST; in such case the LP must not also charge HST, to avoid double-remittance. Failure to file GST/HST returns (including nil returns) within the deadline triggers a late filing penalty under section 280.1: 1% of the amount due plus 25% of the amount, increasing by 1% per full month of delay (maximum 12%). The LP is also exposed to joint and several liability for penalties if it participates in a fraudulent zero-rating claim. CRA may audit the LP's records under section 275, and the taxpayer must retain books and records for 6 years. Ontario does not impose a separate provincial penalty for export zero-rating as HST is harmonized.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current-account and capital-account convertibility and has accepted IMF Article VIII obligations since 1952. The CAD is freely floating and is not subject to exchange controls, surrender quotas, or prior approval for remittances. For the Ontario LP, inbound capital contributions from non-resident limited partners are unrestricted; outbound dividends, distributions, and trade disbursements are unrestricted. Tax entry barriers are out of FX scope: non-resident partners may be subject to Canadian Part I income tax on Canadian-source active business income if the LP carries on business through a permanent establishment, and a non-resident partner may also attract Part XIV branch tax on unremitted Canadian business earnings, mitigated under applicable treaties such as the Canada-US tax convention.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No surrender, repatriation deadline, or mandatory conversion ratio applies to export proceeds in Canada. Goods valued at CAD 2,000 or more that are exported must be reported to the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) via the Canadian Export Reporting System (ERS), generally using a carrier-generated electronic message or the B13A export declaration, with the exporter's CRA-issued Export Customs Code. Export proceeds may be retained in CAD or foreign currency; however, financial institutions must file an Electronic Funds Transfer Report (EFTR) with FINTRAC for any single or 24-hour aggregate non-cash wire transfer of CAD 10,000 or more, and a Large Cash Transaction Report (LCTR) for cash equivalents at the same threshold.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) - monetary authority and sole banknote issuer under the Bank of Canada Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. B-2). Financial intelligence and AML/ATF oversight rests with FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (S.C. 2000, c. 17); prudential supervision of Schedule I banks rests with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) under the Bank Act (S.C. 1991, c. 46). No separate foreign exchange control administration exists in Canada.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% (federal 15% + Ontario 11.5% general corporate rate; the Ontario Limited Partnership itself is a flow-through entity and is not subject to entity-level income tax; corporate partners are taxed on their allocated share at the applicable corporate rate)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid by Canadian resident corporations to non-resident shareholders; not directly applicable to a limited partnership profit allocation, as partnership distributions are not dividends. Non-resident corporate partners are instead subject to Part I tax on their share of Canadian-source business income plus potential Part I.14 branch tax of 25% on the after-tax amount repatriated or deemed distributed.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: T106 Reporting threshold: CAD 1 million aggregate value of reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents; contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation is recommended when the aggregate adjusted amount exceeds CAD 5 million to secure protection against the 10% transfer-pricing penalty; Country-by-Country reporting applies to multinational groups with consolidated group revenue of CAD 750 million or more.

Economic substance scope: Ontario does not maintain separate Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) akin to OFC jurisdictions; however, Canadian tax law applies the substance-over-form doctrine and the Income Tax Act s.245 General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR). For partnership validity and treaty access, the Ontario LP must demonstrate real management, substantive business operations in Ontario, and economic substance; limited partners cannot participate in management without losing limited liability, and general partner activities must have commercial authenticity.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Statements

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s.239(1): imprisonment up to 5 years and a fine of up to 200% of the evaded tax; CRA may reassess beyond the normal three-year limitation period; provincial Ontario offences carry parallel fines and imprisonment terms.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate general partner are jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions and GST/HST under ITA s.227.1 and ETA s.323; civil fraud and intentional evasion can lead to piercing the corporate veil, personal restitution, travel restrictions, and imprisonment.
Shell Company / Lack of Commercial Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA may invoke the GAAR under ITA s.245 to deny the tax benefit and impose a 50% gross negligence penalty; treaty benefits may be denied under the OECD Multilateral Instrument's Principal Purpose Test, leading to reassessment, foreign reporting penalties, and potential criminal prosecution if intent is established.

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner may be personally liable for the disallowed tax as a director under provincial corporate law and federal tax statutes; a court may pierce the LP veil if it is a facade, allowing creditors to seize personal assets; travel bans and asset freezing orders can be issued in tax recovery proceedings.
AML / Money Laundering (FINTRAC)

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 500,000 for corporations and CAD 250,000 for individuals per violation under PCMLTFA; criminal charges for laundering proceeds of crime under Criminal Code s.462.31 can result in up to 10 years imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly participate, authorize, or fail to take reasonable measures to stop money laundering may face personal fines, imprisonment, exclusion from serving as directors, and personal forfeiture of assets under Criminal Code asset-forfeiture provisions.
