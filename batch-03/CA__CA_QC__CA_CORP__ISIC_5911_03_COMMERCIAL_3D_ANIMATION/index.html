Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

No statutory minimum paid-up capital under the QBCA; the articles may declare nominal common shares (e.g., nil or C$0.01 par value). Subscribed consideration must be fully paid into the corporation's own account at a Canadian financial institution before certificate issuance; the REQ does not require an escrow or blocked account. Deferred or unpaid share subscriptions are permitted contractually but shares cannot be issued until the consideration is actually received.

Occupational clearance: register the establishment with CNESST under the OHS Act (CQLR c S-2.1) and maintain first-aid supplies, unencumbered exit paths, and ventilation maintenance logs; a mandatory health and safety program applies once 20 or more workers are employed.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) - federal portal: CRA My Business Account and Business Registration Online (BRO) at canada.ca; Revenu Quebec - provincial portal: CLIC at revenuquebec.ca administering QST, Quebec corporate income tax, QPIP, and QHSF accounts.

E-government filing portal

Step 1 - Corporate tax residency: a corporation incorporated under the Canada Business Corporations Act or the Quebec Business Corporations Act is deemed resident in Canada under ITA s. 250(4). Step 2 - Federal BN/TIN: register via CRA Business Registration Online (BRO) for the 9-digit Business Number (BN), or submit Form RC71 for corporations; simultaneously open program accounts RC0001 (Part I income tax), RT0001 (GST/HST), and RP0001 (payroll, if staff are hired). Step 3 - Quebec registration: register with Revenu Quebec via CLIC to obtain the NEQ (Quebec Enterprise Number) and QST account (TQ/TVQ number). Step 4 - Authority: execute a CRA Form RC59 authorized representative mandate and a Revenu Quebec electronic-filing mandate for the Canadian accountant-of-record. Step 5 - Effective dates: GST/HST and QST registrations become mandatory immediately after the small-supplier threshold is exceeded; voluntary registration is available earlier. Step 6 - Non-resident ownership: if controlled by non-residents, the corporation is not a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) and cannot claim the federal small business deduction; dividend remittances to the foreign parent are subject to Part XIII withholding subject to treaty relief.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation; Territorial_Scope: resident corporations taxed on worldwide income; non-resident corporations taxed only on Canadian-source business, employment, and capital gains; no pass-through eligibility; no Quebec tax-exempt or special economic zone applies to commercial animation production.

Available election forms

Canada has no US-style check-the-box election. Mandatory hybrid-mismatch rules (ITA ss. 18.2-18.21) apply without elective opt-out. Available elections: (i) ETA s. 156 and parallel QST election for closely related corporations to treat intercompany taxable supplies as made for nil consideration; (ii) ITA s. 85(1) rollover election for tax-deferred transfer of property to the corporation on incorporation; (iii) ITA s. 256(2) associated-corporation agreement for small business deduction allocation; (iv) ITA s. 261 functional currency election, relevant for USD-denominated VFX receivables; (v) ITA s. 216 election is available only for non-resident rental income and is not applicable to animation services.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold computed on worldwide taxable supplies (including associated persons) over the preceding four calendar quarters for both GST/HST (ETA s. 148) and QST; mandatory registration applies immediately once the threshold is exceeded; voluntary registration is permitted below the threshold and is commonly adopted to claim input tax credits and to zero-rate export supplies.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Commercial 3D animation and VFX services exported to non-resident clients qualify for GST/QST zero-rating under ETA Schedule VI Part V s. 7 and QST Act s. 54, provided: (a) the recipient is a non-resident person not registered under the ETA/QST for the supply; (b) the service is not supplied to an individual present in Canada; (c) the service does not relate to real property situated in Canada or tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time of performance; and (d) the service is not part of a Canadian-market production (e.g., for a Canadian broadcaster). Licensing of animation copyright and intangible intellectual property to non-residents is zero-rated under ETA Schedule VI Part V s. 10, subject to the foreign-use limitation. The invoice must explicitly state both GST and QST at 0% and cite the zero-rating provision.

  • Executed master services / VFX-animation production agreement with the non-resident client, identifying the client's foreign tax residence, scope of deliverables, and payment terms
  • Zero-rated commercial invoice marked 'GST/HST 0%' and 'QST 0%' with prescribed ETA and QST invoice fields and the relevant zero-rating statutory reference
  • Electronic delivery and acceptance certificate covering completed shots, render manifests, or digital master hand-off
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (IRC) or SWIFT MT103 / Wise B2B payment receipt evidencing crediting of foreign funds to the corporation's Canadian bank account
  • Foreign bank statement or accounts receivable ledger corroborating receipt in CAD within the 180-day repatriation window
  • Non-residency evidence for the client: foreign VAT/GST registration number, tax residence certificate, or foreign corporate registry extract

No real-time fiscal clearance, e-invoicing pre-authorization, or digital stamping regime exists in Canada or Quebec. Invoices must carry the prescribed GST/HST and QST information fields; electronic invoices are valid with recipient consent. Mandatory electronic filing applies: GST/HST returns via CRA GST/HST NETFILE for registrants with annual taxable supplies above CAD 1.5 million; QST returns via Revenu Quebec CLIC/EDI above the same CAD 1.5 million turnover; T2 corporate income tax returns must be filed electronically. Transaction records and fiscal data must be retained in Canada for six years and produced in machine-readable format upon demand.

CRA and Revenu Quebec may reassess within four years of the assessment date, extended to ten years where misrepresentation, neglect, or fraud is established (ETA s. 298 and QST equivalent). If export zero-rating is overturned, the corporation is liable for unremitted GST/QST even if not collected from the foreign client, plus compounded daily arrears interest and clawback of related input tax credits. Civil penalties: 25% of net tax for culpable conduct or false statements (ETA s. 285.1; QST equivalent); the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of tax for false statements (ETA s. 280); 50% of the amount of tax sought to be evaded for wilful evasion (ETA s. 281); 50% of understated Part I income tax for gross negligence (ITA s. 163(2)). Criminal exposure: fines up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to five years (ITA s. 239; ETA s. 327), with Revenu Quebec pursuing parallel penal proceedings on the QST portion. Directors and officers may incur personal liability for unremitted GST/QST (ETA s. 323 and QST equivalent).

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canadian dollar (CAD) is a freely floating, fully convertible currency under Article VIII of the IMF Articles of Agreement. Both current-account payments (VFX service fees, animation royalties, intercompany disbursements) and capital-account flows (equity subscriptions, shareholder loans, dividend repatriation) are unrestricted and require no central-bank license. Profit remittance by dividend is lawful but triggers Part XIII non-resident withholding tax under the Income Tax Act (statutory 25%, typically reduced to 5%-15% under treaty), plus ordinary CRS/FATCA declarations; intercompany shareholder debt must respect the thin-capitalization ratio (1.5:1 debt to equity) or excess interest is denied under ITA s.18(4) to 18(6). The only material hurdles are documentary: tax-residency certificates, share subscription records, and evidence of paid-up capital.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no export-proceeds surrender obligation and no mandatory conversion ratio. All export receipts — including CGI/VFX service income, animation production advances, and licensing royalties — may be retained in CAD or any foreign currency, onshore or offshore, and are available for immediate disposition. The only compliance layer is FINTRAC cross-border EFT/large-cash reporting above CAD 10,000, standard IT-format tax filings, and Statistics Canada/Financial Department balance-of-payments surveys. Statutory surrender timeline: N/A; there is no forced repatriation or conversion window.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), acting jointly with the Minister of Finance under the Currency Act; chartered banks are prudentially supervised by OSFI (Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions), and cross-border monetary flows are reported to FINTRAC. Canada operates a fully liberal exchange administration rather than a SAFE-style foreign exchange control agency: no registration, approval, or licensing is required for ordinary commercial FX transactions, capital subscriptions, or profit remittances.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Wise Business (Wise Payments Canada, a FINTRAC-registered money services business) — Feasibility: High for low-value disbursements and foreign-client collections, but only as a complementary accrual rail. Legal constraints: Wise is not a Schedule I bank; balances are not CDIC-insured and are held in safeguarded/omnibus accounts with chartered banks; Wise cannot issue commercial letters of credit or structured FX hedging lines. For a Low_Risk_Standard 3D animation corporation, use Wise B2B for vendor payments, contractor royalties, and small remittances below CAD 10,000, while maintaining core operating liquidity and all capital escrow balances at an OSFI-regulated Tier 1 bank.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined headline rate, composed of 15% federal Part I corporate income tax and 11.5% Quebec provincial corporate income tax on general active business income.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Canadian Part XIII withholding tax is imposed on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder who is not resident in Canada. Quebec does not levy a separate dividend withholding tax.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Form T106 (Information Return for Non-Arm's Length Transactions with Non-Residents) must be filed by a Canadian corporate entity when the aggregate value of all reportable transactions with a particular non-resident exceeds C$1 million in the taxation year. Under the OECD-aligned Canadian transfer pricing rules, contemporaneous Local File documentation and Master File information are expected for multinational groups with Canadian operations where the controlled transactions are material; Country-by-Country reporting is mandatory for Canadian parent entities or constituent entities of MNE groups with consolidated group revenue of at least C$750 million. Revenu Québec expects the same documentation to be available for the CO-17 return.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain a standalone Caribbean-style Economic Substance Regulations regime, but the Canada Revenue Agency and Revenu Québec vigorously apply the General Anti-Avoidance Rule and a substance-over-form doctrine. A Quebec 3D animation corporation must maintain real employees, actual production and office infrastructure, complete contracts with foreign parent or affiliate studios, genuine bank accounts, IP ownership records, and revenue allocation consistent with the allocation of function, asset, and risk that would prevail at arm's length. A shell Canadian entity created solely to convert foreign animation revenue into dividend stream, or to obtain tax credits, is exposed to reassessment, denial of tax benefits, and criminal sanction.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Knowingly False Corporate Returns

Statutory sanction: Under subsection 239(1) of the Income Tax Act, a person who evades tax is guilty of an indictable offence and liable to a fine of at least C$100,000 and not exceeding 200% of the tax evaded, in addition to imprisonment for up to five years. Under Quebec's Taxation Act and Tax Administration Act, similar fines and imprisonment can be imposed.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly participate in or authorize the false filing are personally liable in civil restitution for the taxes evaded and applicable penalties. Courts may pierce the corporate veil to recover assets from personal bank accounts. Criminal conviction can trigger travel restrictions, including denial of bail or limitations on passport issuance, and a record permanently barring public contracts.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement and Artificial Intercompany Charges

Statutory sanction: The Income Tax Act imposes a transfer pricing penalty of 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment where the taxpayer fails to make reasonable efforts to determine and use an arm's length price. CRA may also apply interest on the reassessed tax. Revenu Québec can impose a parallel assessment and penalties. GAAR can deny deductions and rebate tax credits.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly assessed for the corporation's unpaid Canadian tax under the director liability provisions, including the 10% transfer pricing penalty and interest. Where the arrangement is an intentional sham, directors face criminal fraud liability, imprisonment, and potential asset-freezing orders.
Unreported Profit Repatriation and Unremitted Part XIII Withholding

Statutory sanction: The payer corporation is liable for the entire amount that should have been withheld and remitted to the CRA, plus daily compound interest and penalties for failure to withhold. Section 227.1 provides for director liability for unremitted source deductions; a 10% penalty applies to each remittance failure and a 20% penalty for repeated failure.

Director personal liability: Directors of the Canadian payer are personally and severally liable for unremitted Part XIII withholding, even after resignation if the liability arose during tenure. Enforcement can include garnishment of personal assets, travel-banning court orders where criminal charges are laid, and repossession of the corporation's IP assets where insolvency is induced by the improper dividend.
Shell Company and Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA and Revenu Québec may reassess the shell under the general anti-avoidance rule, deny the claimed tax benefit, reallocate the income to the beneficial owner, and impose penalties of up to 50% of the tax understated. The entity can also be dissolved under the Quebec Business Corporations Act for continued non-compliance with annual registry filings.

Director personal liability: Directors of the shell corporation are personally liable for unlawful dividends paid while the corporation was insolvent, and for unremitted wage and withholding obligations. Courts can disregard corporate separation to attach the assets of the foreign sole shareholder, impose civil restitution in favour of CRA, and in egregious cases issue sentences of imprisonment for fraud.
AML, Beneficial Ownership Registry and Annual Return Failures

Statutory sanction: Under the Canada Business Corporations Act, a corporation that fails to comply with the individuals-with-significant-control register requirement is liable to a fine of up to C$200,000 and a director/officer is liable to a fine of up to C$20,000. Under the Quebec Business Corporations Act and the Taxation Act, failure to produce annual declarations can lead to certificate of revocation or extra-provincial strike-off, placement of the corporation under tax collection measures, and penalties on top of unpaid taxes.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly cause a false beneficial ownership declaration or who obstruct a court-ordered accounting can be personally fined, ordered to pay restitution, and, in cases involving money laundering, have their bank accounts frozen. Travel bans are uncommon for civil registry failures, but criminal AML prosecutions can result in bail restrictions and imprisonment for senior managers.
