Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Swiss Federal Tax Administration (FTA / Eidgenössische Steuerverwaltung ESTV / Administration fédérale des contributions AFC / Amministrazione federale delle contribuzioni AFC), portal https://www.estv.admin.ch. For VAT and indirect taxes, the FTA is the sole competent federal authority; corporate income tax is administered by cantonal tax authorities, but the FTA administers federal direct tax, withholding tax, and stamp duties.

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity deriving Swiss VAT-liable turnover (generally exceeding CHF 100,000 in annual worldwide gross turnover, or CHF 100,000 from Swiss customers for e-services) must register with the FTA through its e-services portal (estv.admin.ch) or by submitting the official Swiss VAT registration form. Non-resident entities must appoint a Swiss fiscal representative accepted by the FTA (Art. 68 VAT Act). A Swiss domestic stock company (AG) automatically receives a UID (Unternehmens-Identifikationsnummer) upon commercial register entry; the UID serves as the VAT identification number after VAT registration, and the corresponding VAT certificate (MWST-Nr.) is issued by the FTA.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation — A foreign entity deriving Swiss VAT-liable turnover (generally exceeding CHF 100,000 in annual worldwide gross turnover, or CHF 100,000 from Swiss customers for e-services) must register with the FTA through its e-services portal (estv.admin.ch) or by submitting the official Swiss VAT registration form. Non-resident entities must appoint a Swiss fiscal representative accepted by the FTA (Art. 68 VAT Act). A Swiss domestic stock company (AG) automatically receives a UID (Unternehmens-Identifikationsnummer) upon commercial register entry; the UID serves as the VAT identification number after VAT registration, and the corresponding VAT certificate (MWST-Nr.) is issued by the FTA.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box election exists under Swiss law. Statutory elections include: (i) cantonal/communal tax group consolidation elections under certain cantonal tax acts (e.g., Art. 13 of the Tax Harmonization Act and cantonal implementation, such as in Zug or Zurich); (ii) VAT option to tax qualifying real estate lease transactions; (iii) election for capital contribution repayment treatment to avoid deemed liquidation; and (iv) application of hybrid mismatch rules (implemented effective 1 January 2020) under Swiss domestic law, including the optional disallowance deduction for hybrid financial instrument payments to the extent not subject to income tax in the recipient jurisdiction.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

8.1%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CHF 100,000 of annual worldwide gross turnover (Art. 10 para. 1 Swiss VAT Act); for foreign suppliers of digital services or e-commerce to Swiss consumers, the same CHF 100,000 threshold applies to turnover generated from Swiss customers. Registration is optional below threshold but required once exceeded.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods (including fashion jewelry and fine timepieces) qualify for the 0% Swiss VAT rate where (i) the goods are physically transported out of Switzerland by the supplier, the purchaser, or a third party; (ii) the export is evidenced by a valid customs declaration in accordance with Art. 23 Swiss VAT Act; and (iii) the supplier retains sufficient documentary proof (export declaration, transport documents, commercial invoices) in its records for at least 10 years. Certain services directly facilitating export and certain offshore services meeting the use-and-enjoyment outside Switzerland test may also be zero-rated. The zero-rating claim is subject to substantiation at audit; incorrect or fraudulent claims trigger reassessment and penalties.

  • Swiss customs export declaration (electronic via EZV/NCTS or validated customs entry, e.g., AUS/ZELV record)
  • Bill of Lading or airway bill (or CMR for road transport) evidencing physical departure from Switzerland
  • Commercial invoice of the exported goods, including full description, quantity, and export value
  • Proof of delivery to the foreign consignee (e.g., signed delivery note, foreign customs entry record, courier confirmation)
  • Inward remittance certificate or bank/FX settlement record showing receipt of payment from the overseas buyer in the original currency

Switzerland does not currently mandate real-time electronic fiscal clearance or digital stamping. Electronic invoices are legally accepted provided the authenticity, integrity, and readability of the invoice are maintained (Art. 37 VAT Act). VAT returns and relevant reporting must be filed electronically through the FTA's e-services portal. No government clearance API or stamp is required; however, the Swiss QR-bill standard facilitates electronic processing for B2C payments, but it is not a tax clearance mandate.

Fraudulent, abusive, or insufficiently substantiated zero-rating claims are subject to retrospective VAT assessment, late payment interest (statutory rate generally 4-5% per annum), and administrative fines of up to three times the evaded tax (Art. 86 Swiss VAT Act). Willful tax evasion or organized fraudulent activity may trigger criminal prosecution with imprisonment or additional monetary penalties. Records must be retained for 10 years; the burden of proof lies on the supplier to demonstrate entitlement to the export exemption.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Switzerland maintains unrestricted current-account and capital-account convertibility under the Swiss Constitution and Art. 3 of the Federal Act on Currency and Payment Means. There are no exchange controls, no mandatory prior licensing for cross-border payments, and no restrictions on invoicing in foreign currency, holding foreign-currency accounts, or repatriating profits. Dividends, interest, royalties, management fees, and loan repayments may be freely remitted internationally, subject only to the 35% Swiss anticipatory withholding tax on dividends/distributions (reduction or refund available under double-taxation treaties or for Swiss-resident beneficial owners) and to the Swiss bank's standard documentary due-diligence requirements. E-commerce proceeds from fashion jewelry and fine timepieces may be collected in CHF, EUR, USD, GBP, or other freely convertible currencies with no central bank conversion requirement.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Switzerland imposes no statutory surrender, repatriation, or mandatory conversion quota for export receipts. Export proceeds may be retained abroad or converted freely. However, all Swiss banks must, under the Anti-Money Laundering Act (AMLA) and the CDB 20 Due Diligence Agreement, identify the economic purpose and origin of funds for cross-border commercial transactions and report statistical payment particulars to the SNB where applicable (e.g., Swiss International Financial Reporting guidelines). No specific percentage of export proceeds must be converted to CHF.

Central bank supervision: Swiss National Bank (SNB) – Schweizerische Nationalbank / Banque nationale suisse / Banca nazionale svizzera; foreign exchange and cross-border payment statistics are collected under the National Bank Act, the Foreign Exchange Ordinance, and the Swiss Code of Obligations. Ancillary regulatory authorities include the Swiss Financial Market Supervisory Authority (FINMA) for banking/AML conduct, the State Secretariat for International Finance (SIF) for international financial-market policy, and SECO for export controls and sanctions.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Conditional (peripheral collection channel, not a primary treasury rail). Swiss fintech companies holding a FINMA fintech license (Art. 1b Banking Act) may accept client deposits up to CHF 100 million on a non-interest-bearing basis and provide e-wallet accounts and FX conversion, subject to a valid banking relationship with a settlement bank. Offshore digital EMIs (e.g., EEA EMI licenses) are legally usable to collect card payments and process online marketplaces, but they cannot replace a Swiss resident bank account for statutory capital and tax purposes. A Swiss AG should maintain a licensed Swiss bank account as the principal treasury anchor, use the EMI only for card acquiring / split-settlement routes, and ensure the EMI has applicable payment institution authorization plus LSW/FATF status, otherwise Swiss banks may reject downstream transfers as high-risk.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Equity capital injections into the Swiss AG must be wired via SWIFT MT103 or MT202 to the bank's blocked account in the name of the company, identified with the purpose code for direct investment equity (e.g., 'Direct Investment – Equity Capital' under the Swiss balance-of-payments reporting classification). The payment reference must include the company name, commercial register number, and the notarial file reference. A bank confirmation of a blocked account in the name of the company is issued to the notary/registry before registration of the capital increase. Shareholder loans are routed similarly as 'Direct Investment – Loan Capital' with a signed subordination and interest-rate document. No SNB prior authorization is required because Switzerland has no exchange controls. For foreign-currency conversions, the bank executes the conversion to CHF at the prevailing market rate and no notification to the central bank is required unless the payment exceeds the SNB's monthly statistical reporting threshold for banks.Trade settlements with overseas suppliers (e.g., watch manufacturers, jewelry workshops, packaging vendors) use the following rails: (1) SWIFT MT103 customer credit transfer for CHF/USD/GBP and any non-SEPA currency via correspondent banking; (2) SEPA Credit Transfer (ISO 20022 pain.001) for EUR payments to beneficiaries within SEPA including EU and EEA countries; (3) CH-Interbank Clearing (SIC) and EURO SIC for same-day CHF/EUR domestic settlements; (4) Commercial Letters of Credit (documentary credits under UCP600) via SWIFT MT700/MT707 for high-value purchases from manufacturers, with confirmed/irrevocable LCs when required; and (5) mass payout files (pain.008) for recurring royalty/artist payments to designers or small workshops. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

8.5% federal corporate income tax computed on post-tax net profit (equivalent to approximately 7.83% on a pre-tax basis); the combined Zug cantonal and communal statutory burden amounts to approximately 14.6% for a resident Aktiengesellschaft, yielding a jurisdictionally blended headline rate of 8.5% federal plus cantonal/communal component of about 6.1% inclusive of all surcharges.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

35% statutory Swiss federal withholding tax (Verrechnungssteuer) is levied on taxable profit distributions, including dividends, bonus shares with reserve capitalization, and deemed distributions (verdeckte Gewinnausschüttung) to shareholders, imposed at source on the gross amount paid or credited to persons resident outside the Swiss banking system under Art. 1 ff. VStG.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

31 March of the calendar year following the fiscal year-end for calendar-year taxpayers; cantonal authorities routinely grant an automatic extension to 31 May upon request, and further extensions up to six months are obtainable for professionally prepared returns. Non-calendar-year fiscal periods are accepted where the commercial year coincides with a permitted accounting reference date and the return is due within six months of the fiscal year-end, subject to cantonal practice.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: A Swiss-resident AG is mandatorily required to prepare and maintain a Transfer Pricing Documentation File (Local File) under Art. 58 DBG and ESTV Circular No. 49 when its aggregate intercompany transactions with any related party, whether Swiss or foreign, exceed CHF 600,000 in a fiscal year, or where related-party loans exceed CHF 600,000 outstanding at any balance sheet date. A Master File (group documentation) must be furnished upon specific request of the tax authority where the multinational group has consolidated turnover above CHF 1 billion or where the Swiss entity's intragroup transactions exceed CHF 6 million; documentation must be presented within 60 days of an official request. The documentation must demonstrate the selected transfer pricing methodology, comparable analysis, contractual terms, functional profile, and arm's length margins.

Economic substance scope: Switzerland does not apply a standalone ESR filing statute comparable to the UAE ESR framework; nevertheless, economic substance is a mandatory condition for the recognition of the AG as the beneficial owner of treaty income and for the acceptance of intercompany allocations. The FTA scrutinizes e-commerce structures where the Swiss entity acts merely as an agent or title-holding conduit; in those cases the tax authority may attribute the e-commerce revenue to the entity where the actual functions, risks, and assets are located and may reassess the Swiss AG on a deemed arm's length profit. For the CH_AG active in online luxury watch and jewelry retail, substance is evidenced by: physical Swiss inventory, Swiss customer support, Swiss bank accounts used for PSP settlements, and board minutes demonstrating management of pricing, fraud prevention, and return logistics in Switzerland.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion via Suppressed E-Commerce Revenue

Statutory sanction: Art. 175 DBG imposes a fine equal to one to three times the amount of federal tax evaded, plus default interest on the underpaid amount; inaggravated cases under Art. 176 DBG the multiplier rises to two and a half times the evaded tax or more. Where false commercial documentation is used to conceal income (tax fraud under Art. 186 DBG), the responsible officers face imprisonment of up to three years and a monetary penalty of up to CHF 30,000; any evaded VAT under the Swiss Value Added Tax Act triggers an additional fine equal to the tax evaded (min. CHF 2,000) and reversal of input tax deductions.

Director personal liability: Directors and the authorized signatory who sign a false tax return become personally liable for the penal sanctions and are jointly and severally liable for the amount of tax unpaid, including interest and penalties, under Art. 754 and 755 CO. Cantonal tax authorities issue travel bans (Ausreisesperre) and asset attachment orders against directors concealing revenue abroad; conviction for tax fraud results in automatic loss of capacity to serve as a board member and extended reporting obligations under anti-money laundering rules.
Withholding Tax Evasion on Dividends and Royalties

Statutory sanction: Under Art. 77 VStG the AG is strictly liable for the withholding tax that was not deducted, together with default interest at the statutory rate; the FTA additionally levies a fine of up to the amount of the unpaid WHT for each infringement. Criminal prosecution under Art. 80 VStG applies where the concealment was intentional, carrying imprisonment of up to three years and a monetary penalty; the withholding agent's license to act in a professional capacity may be suspended.

Director personal liability: Each director is personally, jointly, and severally liable for the WHT amount to the extent the company has not remitted it and the director caused or tolerated the omission; the unpaid WHT claim is treated as a privileged claim in insolvency. Persistent violations give rise to personal travel restrictions and asset freezes for the management, and criminal conviction triggers imprisonment of up to three years.
Shell Company / Treaty Shopping and Absence of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: The FTA denies all treaty benefits retroactively under Circular No. 42 and the domestic anti-abuse jurisprudence, so that the full 35% withholding tax becomes final on all outbound profit distributions; the cantonal and federal tax administrations reallocate the e-commerce profit to the entity with actual substance and reassess the Swiss AG on deemed distributions, including penalties and interest from the initial tax period. The commercial register may strike the company for lack of a lawful registered office under Swiss law.

Director personal liability: Directors who organize the sham structure are personally liable for restitution of the unpaid taxes, social security contributions, and withholding amounts; they may face criminal charges for fraudulent bankruptcy and mismanagement (Art. 165/166 CO) and for inciting tax evasion in a foreign jurisdiction. In cross-border practice, the authorities of the parent's residence state will likely assert CFC taxation, transactional penalties, and travel bans against the directors, and the Swiss directors may be disqualified from holding any directorship for a period of years.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement and Hidden Profit Distribution

Statutory sanction: Art. 58 DBG empowers the tax authority to make a retrospective upward adjustment of taxable income to the arm's length standard; the adjustment is re-characterized as a hidden dividend distribution and subjected to the 35% withholding tax at the corporate level. The company additionally faces a procedural penalty under Art. 174 DBG of up to CHF 10,000 for the non-production of documentation, plus statutory default interest on all corrected assessments; significant adjustments trigger criminal inquiries for attempted tax evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly approve or sign non-compliant transfer pricing agreements are jointly and severally liable for the additional assessments and WHT amounts; in severe cases the directors are prosecuted for intentional evasion, exposing them to fines of up to three times the evaded tax and imprisonment of up to three years. The personal assets of directors may be reached where the company is insolvent and the prejudice arises from the directors' unlawful intercompany decisions.
AML and Counterfeit Luxury Goods Payment Trail

Statutory sanction: Although the e-commerce merchant itself is not a licensed financial intermediary, the use of payment rails to launder criminal proceeds invokes the Swiss Anti-Money Laundering Act (AMLA); upon FINMA determination of systematic violation the company faces a fine of up to CHF 500,000 and confiscation of the criminal proceeds under Art. 70 of the Swiss Criminal Code. Individual directors who knowingly facilitate money laundering are liable under Art. 305bis of the Swiss Criminal Code with imprisonment of up to five years and a monetary penalty; conviction triggers a professional ban (Berufsverbot) applicable to any activity involving payments or fiduciary management.

Director personal liability: The directors are personally subject to imprisonment, confiscation of proceeds traced into their personal assets, and travel bans imposed by investigating magistrates. Where the company has insufficient assets to satisfy imposed fines and confiscation orders, the directors are held civilly liable for the shortfall under tort law if their negligence or intent caused the loss; additionally FINMA may issue an order prohibiting the directors from managing any regulated or commercial entity in Switzerland.
