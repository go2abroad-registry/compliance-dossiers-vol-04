Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation; Swiss AG is a resident corporation subject to unlimited tax liability at federal, cantonal and communal levels. Federal corporate income tax applies at a flat rate of 8.5% (computed on profit after tax) plus cantonal/communal income and capital taxes (combined effective rate approx. 11-22% depending on canton); foreign permanent establishments and foreign real estate are exempt with progression; no passthrough election exists for an AG.

Available election forms

Swiss law does not recognise a US-style check-the-box or transparent entity election; entity-level taxation is mandatory. Available elections under Swiss law: (1) VAT group taxation election (Art. 21 MWSTG) for financially, economically and organisationally integrated entities to be treated as a single VAT taxpayer; (2) flat-rate 'saldo' VAT method election (Art. 37 MWSTG) permitting simplified input VAT deduction by sector-specific rates; (3) voluntary VAT registration below the CHF 100,000 turnover threshold (Art. 11 MWSTG); (4) option to tax an otherwise exempt real-estate supply (Art. 22 MWSTG); (5) hybrid mismatch rules (Art. 61a/62a DBG) are mandatory and cannot be neutralised by election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

8.1%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CHF 100,000 of annual worldwide turnover from taxable supplies (excluding exempt supplies and certain real-estate proceeds) for businesses established in Switzerland; non-established foreign suppliers register at CHF 100,000 of annual turnover from taxable supplies with a Swiss nexus (Art. 10 MWSTG). Licence and royalty turnover from the Malatang self-serve franchise master licensing counts as taxable turnover for threshold purposes.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods: 0% VAT with full input VAT deduction applies where goods are dispatched or transported by the supplier or a third party to a foreign destination (Art. 23 para. 1 no. 1 MWSTG). Services and intangibles: zero rating applies to B2B supplies to entrepreneurial recipients with seat or place of business abroad, services relating to foreign real estate, and transfers of licences, brand rights, know-how and intellectual property used exclusively abroad (Art. 23 para. 2 MWSTG). For the Chinese Malatang self-serve master franchise licence, offshore royalty and franchise fee income is zero rated where the foreign franchisee is an entrepreneur and the licensed exploitation rights are located and used outside Switzerland; domestic on-premise restaurant turnover of the Swiss AG is taxable at 8.1% and take-away food at 2.6%, with no zero rating applicable to domestic food service. Switzerland imposes no statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline.

  • Customs export declaration (e-dec Ausfuhrzollanmeldung) validated by the Swiss Federal Customs Administration, including the export summary document for the exporting AG
  • Transport document evidencing physical export: Bill of Lading (sea), CMR waybill (road) or Air Waybill (air) naming the Swiss exporter as shipper and a foreign destination
  • Commercial invoice stating 'Export / Zero Rated VAT Art. 23 MWSTG' and bearing the Swiss UID/VAT number
  • Inward remittance certificate or banking records evidencing receipt of export proceeds in foreign currency
  • For franchise/licence exports: written master franchise contract, royalty schedule, foreign franchisee VAT-registration or tax-residence proof, and a signed declaration that the licensed intellectual property is exploited outside Switzerland

No real-time fiscal clearance, state API or digital stamping regime applies in Switzerland. VAT invoices must contain the mandatory particulars of Art. 26 MWSTG (supplier identity and UID/VAT number, recipient, date, goods/services, consideration, VAT rate and tax amount). Electronic invoicing is lawful; the Swiss QR-bill (QR-Rechnung) and Peppol BIS 3.0 are the adopted standards. From 2026, suppliers to Swiss federal authorities must issue and receive structured e-invoices under the Federal E-Invoicing Ordinance.

The FTA's export-control and VAT audit sections cross-check customs export declarations, transport documents, invoice flows and bank receipts. Where zero rating is not substantiated, the FTA retroactively reassesses VAT at the standard or reduced rate (8.1%/2.6%, or 3.8% for accommodation-related supplies), adds default interest of 4.5-5% per annum, and applies a 10-year reassessment limitation period. Penalties under Arts. 101-103 MWSTG: wilful and deceitful tax fraud carries a fine of up to CHF 250,000 and/or imprisonment; less serious wilful infringements carry a fine of up to CHF 35,000; negligent violations carry a fine of up to CHF 5,000; the evaded tax and interest are collected in addition, and administrative publication or forfeiture of the tax advantage may be ordered in serious cases.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Swiss franc (CHF) is fully convertible on both current and capital account. Switzerland maintains no exchange controls or restrictions on payments, transfers, or profit remittances, subject to general anti-money laundering (AML) and sanctions compliance. Profit repatriation to foreign shareholders is unrestricted; dividends, royalties, and interest may be remitted via any licensed bank without prior SNB approval, though companies must comply with Swiss withholding tax procedures (e.g., 35% withholding tax on dividends, reduced under applicable treaty). No quantitative limits exist on cross-border fund flows.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory requirement exists to surrender export proceeds or convert foreign currency receipts into CHF. Exporters may retain funds in any freely convertible currency. SNB does not mandate conversion ratios or timelines. All proceeds are subject to standard banking transaction reporting for statistics and AML purposes, but there is no mandatory conversion deadline.

Central bank supervision: Swiss National Bank (SNB) in conjunction with the Swiss Financial Market Supervisory Authority (FINMA) for regulatory oversight and the Federal Department of Finance (FDF) for trade reporting.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium-Low for foreign offshore digital EMI-only structures (e.g., UK/EU EMIs) due to FINMA licensing requirements for deposit-taking and payment services. FINMA awards a 'Fintech License' (banking license relaxation) for public deposits up to CHF 100m, but pure payment service providers need a 'PI License' under the Financial Institutions Act (FinIA). Offshore EMIs can partner with Swiss correspondent banks, however, Swiss licensed banks often reject EMIs without a local physical presence. Recommended route: incorporate a Swiss finance subsidiary or obtain FinIA license for higher compliance viability.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Capital injections into the Swiss AG must be routed via SWIFT MT103 (wire transfer) or SEPA (if within EU/EEA and in EUR) using the corporate account IBAN at a licensed Swiss bank. The correct purpose codes must be used to align with Swiss reporting: for equity contributions, use purpose code A10 (equity injection) or B20 (non-export trade; capital under Swiss Payment Statistics). For shareholder loans use C10 (loan repayment/outflow) and C20 (loan disbursement). The bank may require a notarised board resolution authorising the capital injection, proof of funds, and a subscription agreement to evidence compliance with minimum capital requirements (AG minimum CHF 100,000). For foreign direct investment (FDI) from non-EU residents, banking verification includes SNB statistical reporting (R1 form) and the bank will ask for a compliance declaration. Proceeds must be booked in CHF unless otherwise agreed and should be accompanied by a copy of the Commercial Register entry and payment reference to the share capital account.Outbound trade settlements (e.g., royalty payments, equipment procurement, supplier payments for the franchise operation) should use SWIFT MT103 (single funds transfer) or MT202 (bank-to-bank) for USD/EUR transactions. For trade finance where goods constitute a cross-border transaction, a commercial Letter of Credit (LC) issued by the Swiss bank is acceptable, though standard practice for fast-casual franchise payments will be an LC at sight or deferred payment LC. The beneficiary's correspondent bank and account must be fully verified to avoid sanctions screening delays. Clarification of the payment message must include the Swiss purpose code (e.g., B09 for services, B30 for royalties) and an invoice number. SEPA direct debit is limited to B2B within EEA/EU and is not permitted for Swiss law-governed contracts; use SEPA credit transfer (SCT) or SWIFT. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

8.5% federal standard corporate income tax (tax-on-tax; approximately 9.3% pre-tax equivalent) plus cantonal and communal taxes, producing a combined effective tax rate of approximately 11.9%–21.0% depending on canton and activity; Zug and other holding-friendly cantons commonly yield 14%–16% for licensing/franchise companies.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

35% statutory Swiss withholding tax (Verrechnungssteuer) on dividends paid by the Swiss AG to any shareholder, whether resident or non-resident, unless a double tax treaty or Swiss domestic participation privilege reduces the effective burden.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: The Swiss Federal Tax Administration expects contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation, corresponding to a Local File, where annual cross-border related-party transactions involving goods, services, royalties, or financing by the Swiss entity exceed CHF 5 million per year; a Master File is expected when the multinational group has aggregate global turnover exceeding CHF 1 billion. Even below these thresholds, arm's length evidence is required if intercompany transactions exist.

Economic substance scope: Switzerland does not apply Cayman- or Barbados-style Economic Substance Regulations (ESR), but economic substance is a foundational condition for Swiss tax residence, treaty relief, and the arm's length principle. A Swiss AG holding or licensing Chinese Malatang brand rights must demonstrate, in Switzerland, real personnel, decision-making, contract negotiation, royalty management, bank accounts, and IP enforcement functions. If substance is lacking, the Swiss tax authority may reattribute royalty income to the offshore parent or treat the Swiss entity as an agent of the foreign principal; the MLI Principal Purpose Test and Swiss GAAR (Art. 21 DBG / General anti-avoidance rule) can deny treaty benefits where the main purpose is obtaining tax relief. For the fast-casual self-serve franchise model, the Swiss master licensor must retain economic substance commensurate with its contractual functions and IP risks.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Return

Statutory sanction: Under Art. 175 DBG, a fine may be imposed up to three times the amount of tax evaded but not less than CHF 1,000; default interest of 3.5% per annum on evaded tax; exclusion of deductions; and in severe cases criminal imprisonment up to two years under Art. 176 DBG for attempted qualified tax fraud.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign or cause the filing of a false return, or who knowingly conceal income from the franchise/IP model, are personally jointly and severally liable for the underpaid tax and fine if they acted intentionally or negligently; Swiss corporate law (Art. 754 CO) allows creditor and shareholder claims for damages, and tax authorities may seek civil restitution, travel restrictions, and disqualification from board functions.
Failure to Withhold Swiss Withholding Tax on Dividends

Statutory sanction: The Swiss company is liable for the unwithheld tax amount; a late payment penalty and default interest are assessed; if intentional, criminal tax fraud proceedings can impose fines and imprisonment; the recipient loses treaty relief and may suffer double taxation.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for unwithheld tax if they knowingly instructed or failed to ensure the tax deduction under Art. 12 VStG; piercing the veil is permitted when the company is frustrated or knowingly participates; civil restitution claims may cover the full withholding amount and penalties.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement and Shell Substance Abuse

Statutory sanction: Swiss tax authorities may make a transfer pricing adjustment under Art. 58 DBG, recharacterizing income and disallowing excess deductions; additional federal and cantonal tax of up to 21% combined effective rate applies, plus annual default interest of 3.5%; failure to provide the required TP documentation can trigger administrative fines up to CHF 10,000 and the application of penalties for intentional evasion; DTA relief may be denied under the Principal Purpose Test.

Director personal liability: Directors who adopted or approved non-arm's length intercompany royalty rates, failed to maintain contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation, or engaged in a sham structure can be personally liable for resulting tax adjustments and penalties; civil liability includes reimbursement of the company's tax cost and loss to minority shareholders; an administrative investigation may lead to an international travel ban and criminal prosecution for aiding tax evasion.
Shell Company Formation and Economic Substance Failure

Statutory sanction: Swiss GAAR may deem the shell entity not to be the beneficial owner of the royalty and dividend streams, causing the reattribution of income to the foreign principal, denial of DTA benefits, full Swiss-based taxation of the deemed Swiss PE, and fines for violation of correct bookkeeping; the commercial registrar may strike the entity from the register if it is found to have no statutory seat or administrative activity.

Director personal liability: Directors of the shell are exposed to personal liability for DTA relief fraud; civil restitution to the tax authorities for unpaid Swiss tax and interest; criminal sanctions for facilitating tax evasion and money laundering; travel bans, asset freezes, and prohibition from managing legal entities in Switzerland; veil piercing is consented where the company is a mere façade.
Anti-Money Laundering and AML Beneficial Ownership Non-Disclosure

Statutory sanction: Breach of AML obligations can result in a fine of up to CHF 500,000 for serious cases, confiscation of the illicitly gained assets, and imprisonment up to three years under Swiss Criminal Code Art. 305bis; the company may be denied DTA refunds and face enhanced tax audit scrutiny; the regulator can close the Swiss unit for continued non-compliance.

Director personal liability: Directors and AML compliance officers face personal criminal liability if they knowingly accept or process funds of criminal origin; civil liability under AMLA and the Swiss Code of Obligations includes restitution of the assets plus interest; travel bans, extradition, and reputational debarment are common; all directors who fail to report suspicious franchise payment flows may be held jointly liable for consequent tax and criminal sanctions.
