Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — sole federal crown corporation administering the Income Tax Act and the Excise Tax Act (GST/HST). Ontario's 8% provincial HST component is collected by CRA on behalf of the Ontario Ministry of Finance under the federal-provincial Tax Collection Agreement. The Ontario Ministry of Finance (Tax Administration and Appeals Division) remains the competent authority for Ontario Retail Sales Tax (RST), Employer Health Tax (EHT), and Ontario commodity taxes; Ontario corporate income tax is also collected by CRA. The operative self-service portal is CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-business-portal.html).

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Register the Limited Partnership under the Ontario Limited Partnerships Act with the Ontario Ministry of Government and Consumer Services to obtain a statutory partnership existence and Ontario registration number. Step 2: Apply to CRA for a Business Number (BN) using Form RC1A 'Request for a Business Number' or online through 'Register Now' in My Business Account, identifying the entity as a partnership. Step 3: Upon issuance of the 9-digit BN, open the GST/HST account (program identifier 'RT') under s. 240(1) of the Excise Tax Act (ETA); for non-resident partnerships, the general partner must appoint a Canadian agent for service of process (Form RC276 'Canadian Business Return (Non-Resident)') and provide the partnership agreement. Step 4: Open the 'RM' customs account if the LP will file export declarations through the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA). Step 5: Register with the Ontario Employer Health Tax (EHT) account and Workplace Safety and Insurance Board (WSIB) only if the LP maintains Canadian payroll; no provincial trade licence is required for ISIC 4662 wholesale of copper cathodes. Step 6: For treaty-based withholding relief on allocations to non-resident partners, file Form NR3010 and issue T5013 partnership information returns; the LP itself files a T5013 (fiscal transparency return) even though it is not an income taxpayer.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada has no US-style 'check-the-box' election; the Ontario LP is statutorily fiscally transparent under s. 96(1) of the Income Tax Act (ITA), with income, gains, losses and credits computed at partnership level and allocated to partners. Elective mechanisms available: (a) ITA s. 96(3) joint election to compute partnership income and losses separately for each business carried on by the LP; (b) ETA s. 156 election for nil-consideration supplies between closely related registrants (LP and its corporate general partner); (c) ITA s. 21(1)/(2) elections to capitalize interest and foreign-exchange gains into the adjusted cost base of partnership property; (d) Canada-US Treaty Article IV(6) fiscally-transparent entity application requiring partner-level documentation (Form NR3010) rather than an entity-level election; (e) no OECD Model Election (OECM) exists under Canada's hybrid mismatch rules (ITA ss. 18.2/18.3), which apply automatically to disallow deductions for hybrid mismatches involving the LP or its non-resident partners.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — the ETA 'small supplier' threshold (ss. 148(1)(e) and 240(1)) computed on worldwide taxable supplies in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters, including zero-rated export supplies of copper cathodes. An Ontario LP carrying on business in Canada is a mandatory registrant once total taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000; a non-resident LP without a Canadian permanent establishment is generally not required to register unless it solicits orders for sale in Canada, but voluntary registration is permitted to claim input tax credits (ITCs) attributable to export activities; once registered, the LP charges 13% HST on Canadian domestic wholesale supplies and 0% on qualifying exports.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available under ETA Schedule VI, Part V (exports of goods), principally ss. 9–11. The LP may apply 0% GST/HST where Grade A refined copper cathodes are supplied by way of sale in Canada and exported by the LP as a zero-rated supply, provided the goods are delivered to a destination outside Canada and removed from Canada 'as soon as reasonably possible' after delivery. Where the buyer arranges collection (EXW/FCA Incoterms), zero-rating under s. 10 requires the LP to retain evidence of export by the recipient. Supplying copper cathode already located outside Canada at the time of sale falls outside the scope of Canadian GST/HST under s. 142(1) ETA and requires no zero-rating claim; the LP must classify each LME-linked delivery by location of goods at the time of transfer and by Incoterm to avoid an incorrect 13% HST assessment or an unsubstantiated 0% claim.

  • CBSA B3-3 Canada Customs Coding Form or CES electronic Export Declaration (required for shipments valued above CAD 2,000 to non-US destinations; US-bound shipments rely on the proof-of-export exemption provisions)
  • Bill of Lading (ocean combined transport or waybill) evidencing physical export of the copper cathodes from Canada and the named foreign destination
  • Commercial Invoice reflecting LME Grade A copper cathode specification (ASTM B115-00), net weight, unit price, Incoterm, and export value
  • Inward Remittance Certificate — not statutorily required in Canada; CRA-accepted equivalents are the SWIFT MT103 credit advice, documentary letter of credit settlement, or LME warrant settlement record showing receipt of foreign proceeds in a Canadian bank account
  • Packing List, weighbridge/assay weight certificate, and carrier's signed proof of departure (outturn report) substantiating the physical movement from Canadian premises
  • GST/HST return reconciliation schedule evidencing the attribution of input tax credits to the zero-rated export supplies

Canada does not impose real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or continuous-use API mandates at the federal or Ontario level. Electronic invoicing is permitted under ETA ss. 101(1) and 223(2) provided invoices are complete, legible, and retained in a secure readable format for six years. The pan-Canadian e-invoicing framework (announced in the 2024 Federal Budget) is voluntary and PEPPOL BIS 3.0-based; it is not yet mandatory for B2B metal trading. For exports, the CBSA requires pre-departure electronic export declarations via the Canadian Export Reporting System (CES) or eManifest portal for goods valued above CAD 2,000 destined to countries other than the United States; LME warrant transfers alone do not trigger CBSA filing because the physical cathode shipment, not the warrant, constitutes the export nexus.

CRA audit risk centers on the insufficiency of export documents: where export documentation is missing or internally inconsistent, the claimed zero-rated supply is re-assessed as a taxable 13% HST supply, with interest and penalties from the original reporting date. The normal reassessment window for GST/HST is four years (ETA s. 298(1)); the window is unlimited for misrepresentation, neglect, or wilful default. Penalties include: (i) ETA s. 281 false-statement penalty of the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the net tax that would have been payable had the misstatement been accepted; (ii) ETA s. 280 summary conviction fine up to CAD 25,000 and/or imprisonment up to one year, or unlimited fine and imprisonment up to five years on indictment for wilful evasion or 'missing-trader' style zero-rating fraud; (iii) gross negligence penalties under ITA s. 163.2 assessed against partners who knowingly participate in false export claims; (iv) retrospective clawback of the full 13% HST plus compounded daily arrears interest at the prescribed CRA rates; and (v) security requirements imposed under ETA s. 299 for repeat non-compliance. Regarding FX repatriation: Canada imposes no statutory 180-day export-proceeds repatriation deadline for arm's-length trade receivables; the 180-day figure corresponds to the ITA s. 17 deemed-interest horizon for cross-border indebtedness exceeding CAD 100,000 owed by non-residents, and CRA expects export proceeds to be received within a commercially reasonable period, generally not exceeding 180 days, to sustain the evidentiary credibility of the export claim.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar operates under a free-floating exchange-rate regime with full current-account and capital-account convertibility. No exchange-control legislation or FX licensing requirements exist under the Currency Act; capital contributions, trade settlements, and profit remittances are legally unrestricted. Limitations arise only from general law: (i) Part XIII withholding tax under the Income Tax Act applies to certain non-resident payments (dividends, interest, royalties, management fees), (ii) the Investment Canada Act imposes net-benefit review for large foreign acquisitions in Canada, and (iii) transactions equal to or exceeding CAD 10,000 trigger FINTRAC and CBSA reporting. Repatriation of LP distributions to non-resident limited partners is lawful without FX approval but must transit a regulated Canadian financial institution to preserve treaty benefits and the audit trail.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no mandatory surrender, repatriation, or conversion ratio on export receipts. A copper trading LP may retain USD proceeds in foreign-currency accounts at Schedule I banks indefinitely. The sole mandatory reporting obligations are: (a) CBSA declaration of any physical cross-border movement of currency or bearer negotiable instruments equal to or exceeding CAD 10,000, and (b) FINTRAC electronic funds transfer (EFT) reports by financial institutions for any SWIFT wire of CAD 10,000 or more, regardless of currency. No central-bank approval is required for holding or deploying export proceeds offshore.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), in coordination with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for prudential banking supervision, the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for AML/CFT surveillance, and the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) for cross-border currency declaration enforcement.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Moderate-to-low. Under section 545 of the Bank Act, deposit-taking in Canada is restricted to Schedule I, II, or III chartered banks and federally regulated trust companies; a pure offshore digital EMI or e-money issuer cannot lawfully hold deposits or op-erate a Canadian treasury account directly. Offshore EMIs (e.g., Wise, Payoneer) are acceptable only as ancillary payment gateways for minor disbursements, not for capital escrow, LME settlement, or Documentary LC rails. Any fintech solution must be layered over a domestic chartered bank via an Authorized Foreign Bank (AFB) framework or a partnership with a provincial trust company, and must satisfy FINTRAC registration obligations if it acts as a money services business (MSB). Given the Medium_Risk_Enhanced AML tier and the commodities trading books, exclusive reliance on EMIs is inadvisable.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1 million for goods transactions; CAD 500,000 for services; CAD 250,000 for intangibles (per CRA TP documentation requirements).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have specific Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) but applies general anti-avoidance rules (GAAR) and requires substance for treaty benefits. The partnership must demonstrate real business activity, including physical trading operations, to avoid treaty shopping challenges.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under Income Tax Act s.239; fines up to 200% of tax evaded, imprisonment up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unpaid corporate taxes under s.227.1; civil restitution, travel bans, and imprisonment for fraud.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Adjustment of taxable income, penalties up to 10% of net adjustment (if gross negligence), plus interest.

Director personal liability: Directors can be held liable for penalties if they authorized or acquiesced in the non-compliance.
Anti-Money Laundering (AML)

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 2 million for corporations; criminal charges for money laundering under Criminal Code.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal fines and imprisonment (up to 10 years) for knowingly facilitating money laundering.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty benefits, reassessment of income, and penalties under GAAR (s.245) up to 50% of tax benefit.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for GAAR penalties if they participated in the arrangement.
