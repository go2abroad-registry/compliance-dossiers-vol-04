Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — administered under the Minister of National Revenue; primary e-service portals: My Business Account, Business Registration Online (BRO), GST/HST NETFILE, and the CBSA Assessment and Revenue Management (CARM) platform for customs export reporting.

E-government filing portal

A foreign-owned Canadian Business Corporation must obtain a single 9-digit Business Number (BN) from the CRA, which functions as the universal TIN for federal and Ontario corporate income tax, GST/HST, payroll, and customs accounts. Step-by-step: (1) incorporate under the Canada Business Corporations Act or Ontario Business Corporations Act and obtain a certificate of incorporation; (2) file Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and certain program accounts) through BRO or by mail to the appropriate Tax Centre; (3) indicate the GST/HST program account (RT) indicator only if the SPV makes or intends to make taxable supplies (a pure passive holding vehicle generally does not need one); (4) attach the certificate of incorporation, notice of directors and registered office, and, where the SPV is controlled by non-residents, provide the required Canadian resident director or registered agent details under the CBCA/OBCA; (5) if the SPV carries on business through a permanent establishment in Ontario, identify the Ontario PE address in the RC1 and, where requested, file the CRA's corporate account information for the Ontario Corporations Tax Act — Ontario corporate tax is administered by the CRA under a federal-provincial collection agreement, and no separate Ontario TIN is issued; (6) CRA issues the BN and GST/HST account within approximately 10-15 business days; BN validity can be verified via the CRA BN/Program Account Confirmation service or the GST/HST Account Confirmation API.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — a Canadian Business Corporation is a separate legal taxpayer subject to Part I of the Income Tax Act at the federal level and Ontario corporate income tax at the provincial level; the SPV is taxed on worldwide income with foreign tax credits and cannot elect pass-through treatment; non-resident shareholders do not alter the entity-level liability; the Canadian classification is per se corporate and takes precedence over any US check-the-box or other foreign characterization for Canadian tax purposes.

Available election forms

Canada has no Section 7701 check-the-box regime. Available statutory elections for a PE buyout SPV include: (i) Income Tax Act s. 85(1) rollover (Form T2057) for tax-deferred transfer of target shares or property into the SPV; (ii) s. 85.1 share-for-share exchange election for eligible reorganization transactions; (iii) Part IV RDTOH dividend refund elections via Ontario-consistent dividend refund mechanisms; (iv) s. 212(17) election for treaty-exempt interest reporting on upstream shareholder debt; (v) s. 216(4)/215(6) elections for non-resident service providers; (vi) GST/HST elections under s. 156 (annual filing), s. 167 (sale of business as a going concern), s. 211 (related-party election for financial supplies), and s. 273 (election for supplies to prescribed non-residents to permit input tax credit recovery); (vii) hybrid mismatch limitation rules under ITA ss. 18.2, 18.21, and 94.1 disallow structured avoidance through US LLC or partnership interposition — an upstream check-the-box election does not create a Canadian deduction.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — the small supplier threshold is worldwide gross revenue from taxable supplies (not exempt supplies) over any consecutive four calendar quarters; once exceeded, HST registration is mandatory effective from the first day of the month following the quarter; a PE buyout SPV typically supplies only exempt financial services (s. 123(1) ETA), which do not count toward the threshold and do not trigger registration unless the SPV separately charges management or advisory fees, makes taxable supplies of goods, or voluntarily registers to claim input tax credits (noting that ITCs are generally unavailable on exempt financial services).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods: zero-rated under Part V, Schedule VI of the Excise Tax Act if exported by the registrant as soon after delivery as is reasonable and documentary proof of export is retained; 0% HST applies and full input tax credits are recoverable on related acquisitions. Services: zero-rated under s. 9, Part V, Sch. VI when supplied to a non-resident who is outside Canada at the time the supply is made and the service is not consumed in Canada. Critical PE-SPV caveat: interest on acquisition debt, dividends, loan origination, securities custody, and syndicated lending are financial services under s. 123(1) ETA and are EXEMPT (not zero-rated) both domestically and on export; an exempt supply confers no input tax credit entitlement, with narrow relief under the s. 186 election or the selected financial services institution (SFSI) ITC recapture rules under s. 225.2 for groups with consolidated assets above CAD 1 billion — an SPV below this threshold cannot recover HST embedded in deal costs; separately contracted management, consulting, and administration fees that are not ancillary to a financial service may qualify as zero-rated export services if the non-resident recipient receives no benefit in Canada.

  • Form B13A / CBSA Export Declaration filed electronically via CARM or ACROSS for goods valued above CAD 2,000
  • Commercial invoice with vendor and buyer legal identifiers, full description, HS tariff classification, and payment terms
  • Bill of lading (ocean or air) or Cargo Control Document / CUSIT report for highway and rail exports to the United States
  • Proof of export identification such as container number, seal number, carrier warehouse receipt, and highway carrier trip documents
  • Foreign exchange or inward remittance certificate, SWIFT MT103 confirmation, or bank ledger evidencing receipt of proceeds
  • Executed s. 273 ETA election form where supplies are made to a related qualifying non-resident to permit input tax credit recovery

Canada does NOT require real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or continuous transaction control e-invoicing comparable to Mexico's CFDI or India's e-invoicing regime; CRA accepts paper, PDF, EDI, and e-invoice output provided the invoice is a durable, legible record retained for six years; all corporate GST/HST registrants must file electronically via GST/HST NETFILE; large financial institutions may use XML bulk upload via OFTP (Online Federation of Tax Programs); there is no government-issued clearance code on invoices, but source documents supporting zero-rated exports and input tax credit claims must be made readily available to a CRA auditor under s. 286 ETA.

Canada does not impose a black-letter 180-day export-proceeds repatriation statute; the 180-day benchmark in this dossier is a treaty and transfer-pricing best practice — if the SPV leaves offshore intercompany receivables outstanding, the CRA may impute notional interest under s. 17(1) ITA or a deemed shareholder benefit under s. 15(2) ITA on upstream receivables; audit penalties for erroneous zero-rating claims include: (i) a six-year default GST/HST assessment window, extended to eight years for misrepresentation under ss. 298(1)(c)/(d) ETA; (ii) a 50% gross-negligence penalty under s. 285 ETA plus prescribed-rate interest on unremitted amounts; (iii) criminal liability under s. 327 ETA for knowingly making false statements, with fines up to CAD 25,000 and up to two years' imprisonment; (iv) CBSA penalties under the Customs Act including seizure of goods or a CAD 1,000-per-declaration penalty for false export information; (v) CRA refund integrity controls requiring executive-level adjudication of large GST/HST refunds before payout; (vi) for PE structures, s. 245 GAAR may claw back the benefit of HST-avoidance planning and s. 247 transfer-pricing penalties equal to 10% of the net adjustment apply to non-arm's-length offshore management fees.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada has no general capital controls, exchange controls, mandatory exchange-control registration, or central-bank approval requirement for ordinary inbound or outbound corporate payments. The Canadian dollar is freely convertible through authorized financial institutions. Current-account payments, including trade settlement, interest, fees, dividends, and other ordinary commercial payments, are generally unrestricted. Capital-account transactions, including equity subscriptions, shareholder loans, acquisitions, and intercompany funding, are also generally unrestricted, subject to corporate-law authority, sanctions, anti-money-laundering requirements, beneficial-ownership verification, tax compliance, lender covenants, withholding-tax rules, thin-capitalization rules, transfer-pricing rules, and any applicable foreign-investment, securities, competition, or national-security review. Canadian resident corporations may remit dividends and interest to non-residents without exchange-control approval, but applicable Canadian withholding tax, treaty relief documentation, Part XIII tax procedures, and corporate solvency requirements must be addressed. A private-equity buyout SPV should maintain board approvals, executed subscription or loan documents, source-of-funds evidence, ownership-chain records, and tax characterization support before moving capital across borders.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement, mandatory conversion ratio, or prescribed deadline for converting foreign-currency export receipts into Canadian dollars. Export proceeds may generally be retained in foreign-currency accounts or converted at the holder's discretion. Banks may impose contractual reporting, documentation, settlement, sanctions-screening, or transaction-monitoring requirements. Exporters must comply separately with customs, export-control, sanctions, tax, accounting, and sector-specific reporting obligations. Large international electronic funds transfers may trigger reporting by the financial institution to FINTRAC, including electronic-funds-transfer reporting for transfers of CAD 10,000 or more under applicable anti-money-laundering rules, subject to statutory aggregation and exemption rules.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada. Foreign-exchange transactions are also subject to federal laws administered by the Department of Finance Canada, the Canada Border Services Agency, the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada, the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions, and Global Affairs Canada, depending on the transaction.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for payment initiation, foreign-exchange execution, and collection support; low as a stand-alone substitute for a Canadian deposit-taking bank. A foreign electronic-money institution generally cannot accept Canadian deposits or provide unrestricted Canadian banking services merely because it is licensed offshore. Access normally requires a regulated Canadian bank account, correspondent arrangement, licensed or registered Canadian payment-service-provider structure, or another legally valid model. The Retail Payment Activities Act regime administered by the Bank of Canada may apply to a payment service provider performing in-scope payment functions for end users in Canada, including certain foreign providers. The provider must also address FINTRAC registration where it conducts in-scope money-services-business activities, Canadian sanctions, anti-money-laundering controls, safeguarding, operational-risk, data, and consumer requirements. An EMI should not be used for subscription capital, acquisition escrow, investor distributions, or high-value syndicated-loan flows until its Canadian perimeter, safeguarding model, settlement bank, beneficial-owner controls, and contractual recourse have been confirmed.

Root cause: High-value wires involving jurisdictions subject to enhanced sanctions, corruption, proliferation-financing, or money-laundering risk.

Defense evidence: Sanctions-screening results for all parties, banks, vessels, assets, and beneficial owners; jurisdictional risk memorandum; enhanced due-diligence questionnaire; independent legal review; commercial invoices or closing statement; export-control analysis where applicable; written confirmation that no sanctioned person or restricted sector is involved; documented escalation and approval by the bank's compliance function.
Private-equity buyout activity that resembles securities dealing, investment-fund management, lending, money services, or payment intermediation.

Root cause: Private-equity buyout activity that resembles securities dealing, investment-fund management, lending, money services, or payment intermediation.

Defense evidence: Legal-perimeter memorandum; description of the SPV's own-account investment activity; confirmation that it does not solicit public investors, operate a money-services business, provide payment services, or lend to the public; securities-law exemptions and offering documents where relevant; regulatory registrations or exemption analysis; organizational chart separating the SPV from sponsor, manager, lender, and portfolio-company functions.
Use of virtual office, mail-forwarding address, third-party director, shared service provider, or non-resident authorized signatory.

Root cause: Use of virtual office, mail-forwarding address, third-party director, shared service provider, or non-resident authorized signatory.

Defense evidence: Lease or service agreement; proof of Canadian business nexus; directors' residential-address documents; management-services agreement; power of attorney; identity and role evidence for each signatory; explanation of where treasury decisions are made; local counsel or corporate-service-provider confirmation; documented segregation of duties.
Foreign-exchange activity without clear economic purpose, frequent round-dollar transfers, rapid pass-through activity, or unexplained conversion gains.

Root cause: Foreign-exchange activity without clear economic purpose, frequent round-dollar transfers, rapid pass-through activity, or unexplained conversion gains.

Defense evidence: Treasury policy; approved hedge and dealing limits; counterparty list; trade confirmations; ISDA or other derivatives agreements; cash-flow forecasts; invoices, loan schedules, and closing statements; reconciliation reports; evidence that transactions hedge identifiable exposures rather than facilitate third-party payments.
Request for an escrow or trust-style account without a regulated escrow agent, clear release conditions, or documented beneficial ownership of funds.

Root cause: Request for an escrow or trust-style account without a regulated escrow agent, clear release conditions, or documented beneficial ownership of funds.

Defense evidence: Escrow agreement; release-condition matrix; identification of depositor, beneficiary, and escrow agent; source-of-funds evidence; lawyer's trust-account or regulated trust-account confirmation where applicable; account-control protocol; dual-approval mandate; closing checklist; written bank approval of the account purpose.
Mismatch among corporate name, tax identifiers, invoice details, wire beneficiary, and shareholder or lender documentation.

Root cause: Mismatch among corporate name, tax identifiers, invoice details, wire beneficiary, and shareholder or lender documentation.

Defense evidence: Name-change certificates; corporate profile report; CRA business-number confirmation; beneficial-owner certification; corrected invoice or funding instruction; written explanation of trade names, affiliates, escrow agents, and intermediary banks; independent confirmation from the counterparty through a verified channel.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Route equity subscriptions, shareholder loans, and acquisition funding through a Canadian bank account held in the exact legal name of the SPV. Use a bank-to-bank wire through SWIFT or an equivalent Canadian correspondent route, with the originating account held by the documented investor or lender whenever practicable. The payment narrative should identify the legal basis, such as equity subscription, shareholder loan, capital contribution, acquisition escrow funding, or syndicated-loan drawdown; Canada does not impose a universal statutory FX purpose-code system, so the bank's internal purpose classification and documentary requirements control. Before receipt, provide the bank with the executed funding agreement, board approval, cap table, ownership chain, source-of-funds evidence, expected amount and currency, remitter details, and closing timetable. Reconcile the received amount to the subscription or loan ledger, retain SWIFT confirmations and bank statements, and record any foreign-exchange gain or loss under the applicable accounting policy. Use only verified payment instructions and dual approval; independently confirm any amended account details. The bank may file required FINTRAC reports, including applicable large electronic-funds-transfer reports, and may delay or reject funds pending sanctions and AML review. Obtain tax advice for paid-up capital, shareholder-loan characterization, interest deductibility, withholding tax, thin capitalization, transfer pricing, and treaty entitlement.Use the Canadian operating account or an approved transaction account for portfolio-company acquisition costs, lender payments, professional fees, supplier invoices, and other permitted disbursements. For ordinary wire settlement, provide beneficiary legal name, address, account or IBAN where applicable, SWIFT/BIC, intermediary-bank data, invoice or closing-statement reference, currency, amount, and payment purpose. SWIFT MT103 remains a recognized single-customer credit-transfer format, although banks are migrating to ISO 20022 payment messages; use the format supported by the sending and receiving banks and retain the payment confirmation and UETR. For import or other documentary trade exposure, a commercial documentary letter of credit may be issued or advised through a bank, with clear Incoterms, document requirements, expiry, presentation period, sanctions wording, insurance, inspection, and discrepancy procedures. A standby letter of credit or demand guarantee should be used only where commercially required and after legal review of issuer obligations, collateral, governing law, and counter-guarantees. For acquisition closings, use controlled escrow or lawyer's trust arrangements only where the account holder and release mechanics are legally valid and accepted by the bank. Apply dual authorization, maker-checker controls, beneficiary verification, payment limits, sanctions screening, invoice or closing-document matching, and daily reconciliation. Canada does not generally require prior FX approval for outbound commercial payments, but withholding tax, non-resident reporting, transfer pricing, sanctions, export controls, anti-bribery rules, lender covenants, and solvency requirements remain applicable.Deliverable foreign-exchange forwards with an authorized Canadian bank or appropriately regulated dealer, documented under a master agreement or bank terms, and linked to forecast or committed currency exposures.Non-deliverable forwards where commercially available and legally suitable, generally cash-settled in an agreed reference currency; document the reference rate, fixing date, settlement date, close-out mechanics, collateral, termination rights, and counterparty-credit terms.Currency swaps, including cross-currency swaps, for managing multi-period currency and interest-rate exposure; document principal exchanges, reset dates, benchmark rates, collateral, netting, withholding tax, and early-termination provisions.Foreign-exchange options, collars, and option combinations where approved by the board and treasury policy; assess premium, leverage, break costs, suitability, accounting, and derivatives-disclosure requirements before execution.Hedging must be conducted with an eligible regulated counterparty, supported by documented authority, exposure identification, confirmation, valuation, collateral and margin controls, counterparty limits, and independent reconciliation. Ontario securities and derivatives requirements, Canadian trade-reporting rules, dealer registration or exemption analysis, clearing obligations, and derivatives end-user exemptions may apply depending on the product, counterparty, notional amount, and trading status.Do not use leveraged derivatives, speculative positions, or third-party settlement instructions without specific board approval, documented risk limits, and legal and tax review. A private-equity SPV should generally limit hedging to acquisition consideration, portfolio-company funding, debt service, committed capital calls, and other identifiable exposures.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium for payment initiation, foreign-exchange execution, and collection support; low as a stand-alone substitute for a Canadian deposit-taking bank. A foreign electronic-money institution generally cannot accept Canadian deposits or provide unrestricted Canadian banking services merely because it is licensed offshore. Access normally requires a regulated Canadian bank account, correspondent arrangement, licensed or registered Canadian payment-service-provider structure, or another legally valid model. The Retail Payment Activities Act regime administered by the Bank of Canada may apply to a payment service provider performing in-scope payment functions for end users in Canada, including certain foreign providers. The provider must also address FINTRAC registration where it conducts in-scope money-services-business activities, Canadian sanctions, anti-money-laundering controls, safeguarding, operational-risk, data, and consumer requirements. An EMI should not be used for subscription capital, acquisition escrow, investor distributions, or high-value syndicated-loan flows until its Canadian perimeter, safeguarding model, settlement bank, beneficial-owner controls, and contractual recourse have been confirmed.
