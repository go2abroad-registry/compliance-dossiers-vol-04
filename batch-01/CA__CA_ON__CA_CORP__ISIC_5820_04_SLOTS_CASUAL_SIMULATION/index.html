Tax ID Application Infrastructure

      

        
Competent tax authority
Canada Revenue Agency / Agence du revenu du Canada (CRA) — federal corporate income tax, Canada-wide GST/HST, and Ontario corporate income tax (administered by CRA on behalf of Ontario under a tax administration agreement). Portal: CRA 'My Business Account' at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html.

        
E-government filing portal
1) Incorporate under the Canada Business Corporations Act or the Ontario Business Corporations Act with Ontario jurisdiction; 2) obtain a CRA Business Number (the statutory TIN) by submitting Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) or online through CRA My Business Account; 3) open the required program accounts — RC0001 for corporate income tax, RT0001 for GST/HST, and RP0001 for payroll (if applicable); 4) for Ontario corporate income tax, no separate Ontario tax number is required because CRA administers the Ontario corporate income tax through the T2 return; 5) maintain the BN and CRA account records and file annual T2 corporate returns within six months of the fiscal year-end.

The corporation computes income independently, pays entity-level tax, and distributes after-tax dividends to shareholders subject to the Canadian dividend gross-up and integration rules. — 1) Incorporate under the Canada Business Corporations Act or the Ontario Business Corporations Act with Ontario jurisdiction; 2) obtain a CRA Business Number (the statutory TIN) by submitting Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) or online through CRA My Business Account; 3) open the required program accounts — RC0001 for corporate income tax, RT0001 for GST/HST, and RP0001 for payroll (if applicable); 4) for Ontario corporate income tax, no separate Ontario tax number is required because CRA administers the Ontario corporate income tax through the T2 return; 5) maintain the BN and CRA account records and file annual T2 corporate returns within six months of the fiscal year-end.

    

    

      
Default Tax Classification & Elections

      

        
Default tax classification
Entity_Level_Taxation — a Canadian business corporation is a separate taxpayer under the Income Tax Act; there is no pass-through treatment at the federal or Ontario level for ordinary corporations. The corporation computes income independently, pays entity-level tax, and distributes after-tax dividends to shareholders subject to the Canadian dividend gross-up and integration rules.

        
Available election forms
Canada does not provide a U.S.-style check-the-box election. Relevant elections for a Canadian corporation include: Income Tax Act s. 85(1) rollover on transfer of property to a corporation; s. 45(2) election to defer capital gains on a change of use; s. 44 small-business/residence deferral; s. 256(2) associated-corporation election; alternative fiscal period election under s. 150(2); GST/HST s. 156 election for closely related corporations to treat intercorporate supplies as excluded; GST/HST s. 167 joint election; and anti-hybrid mapping under ITA s. 18.2/18.3 for hybrid mismatch amounts.

      

    

    

      
VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

      

        
Standard VAT / GST rate
13%

        
Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)
CAD 30,000 — the 'small supplier' threshold. A corporation must register for GST/HST when its worldwide taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters. Non-resident suppliers of digital or intangible services to Canadian consumers are also required to register at the same threshold using the simplified non-resident GST/HST registration; the target entity is a Canadian corporation and is expected to register for RT0001 once it earns any taxable supplies in the Ontario/Canadian market.

      

      
      

        Export 0% zero-rating conditions
        
Supplies of social-casino app content, virtual currency, and digital slot-simulation services are zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act when made to a non-resident person, provided the supply is not performed in Canada, is not in respect of real or tangible property situated in Canada, and the customer's ordinary place of residence or permanent establishment is outside Canada. The app-store distribution chain (Apple IAP / Google Play Billing) does not automatically make the supply taxable; the Canadian developer must establish the destination of the consumer through billing address, storefront country, IP/geolocation, device location, and platform settlement reports. For Canadian consumers in Ontario, the 13% HST applies. For non-resident consumers, the zero-rating claim must be evidenced contemporaneously; Canada has no export-licence or bank-administered FX-repatriation requirement for digital services.

No real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or API-based e-invoice clearance exists in Canada at the federal or Ontario level. CRA accepts both paper and electronic invoices; e-invoicing is encouraged and CRA has adopted a voluntary Pan-Canadian Peppol-based standard. Mandatory electronic filing applies to GST/HST registrants with annual revenue above CAD 1.5 million, and to corporate income tax returns (T2) for corporations with gross revenue above CAD 1 million. For GST/HST, returns must be filed through TEL or GST NETFILE; no electronic invoicing certification is required.

      
CRA treats export zero-rating claims as high-risk in digital and app-store businesses. The burden of proof is on the supplier. If zero-rating is denied, CRA reassesses the full amount at 13% HST, reverses input tax credits that relate to the denied export, and charges prescribed arrears interest retroactively to the original reporting period. The normal reassessment limit is four years, but the time limit is unlimited where a claim involves misrepresentation, wilful default, or fraud. Penalties include, for knowingly false or grossly negligent statements, a minimum CAD 100 penalty and an additional penalty of up to 50% of the tax sought to be evaded, plus potential criminal prosecution and director liability. Although Canada imposes no statutory 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation rule for digital services, the 180-day field is treated here as a conservative internal documentation benchmark for tying inward remittances to zero-rated export sales within CRA audit cycles.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

      
Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

    

    

      
Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

      

        
Current-account convertibility
The Canadian dollar is fully convertible on both current and capital accounts. Canada maintains no general foreign exchange control legislation; cross-border capital movements are unrestricted. Profit remittance by a Canadian subsidiary is effected through dividends, which are subject to Part XIII non-resident withholding tax at 25% unless reduced by treaty (e.g., Canada–US Protocol generally 5% for significant shareholding); such remittances are not blocked by FX controls. Foreign direct investment into Canada is subject to notification/review under the Investment Canada Act, and all transactions of CDN $10,000 or more must be reported to FINTRAC for AML/ATF purposes. Banking documentation must evidence source of funds and corporate authority for every high-value remittance.

        
Export proceeds surrender rules
Canada imposes no export proceeds surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no repatriation timeline for export receipts. Exporters may retain proceeds in foreign currency; however, resident financial institutions must report international transactions and balance-of-payments data under the Statistics Act, and monetary amounts exceeding CDN $10,000 or equivalent must be reported to FINTRAC as a large cash or virtual currency transaction. For treasury efficiency, export proceeds may be held in multi-currency accounts and converted at commercial discretion.

      

      
Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as monetary authority; Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) as prudential bank regulator; Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) as AML/ATF regulator

    

    

      
Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

      

        
        

          

            Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails
            Fit: High
          

          
Feasibility rating: Medium (with legal caveats). Offshore digital Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) and payment service providers may be used as supplementary settlement channels but cannot replace a regulated Canadian bank account for core capital escrow or operating liquidity. Any EMI conducting money services business activities in Canada—including foreign exchange dealing, remittance, or virtual currency dealing—must itself be registered as a Money Services Business (MSB) with FINTRAC and maintain a Canadian compliance program. Unregistered offshore EMIs may remain legal for invoicing or disbursement execution provided they do not hold Canadian client funds, but Canadian regulatory and banking partners will generally require 100% of treasury flows to pass through a prudentially supervised Canadian bank account for visibility and audit integrity.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

      

        For a compliant inbound capital injection from a non-resident shareholder: (i) remit via SWIFT MT103 from the investor's bank to the Canadian bank account nominated as capital bank; (ii) the wire must identify remitter as the investor, beneficiary as the Canadian company number, and clearly reference the share-subscription agreement number in field 70 (Remittance Information); (iii) a Canadian bank will apply BOP code under Statistics of Canada's International Transactions Reporting System (e.g., BOP code 1004 for direct investment equity below 10% or code 1001 for controlling direct investment); (iv) before crediting, the bank will request the source-of-funds dossier, the executed subscription agreement, and a signed officers' declaration confirming no third-party financing; (v) for amounts above CDN $10,000, the bank records the transaction as a FINTRAC international electronic funds transfer report (IEFTR); (vi) Canada does not impose conversion on capital injection, but the CAD/USD conversion may be executed internally at the bank's assigned daily rate or through a spot forward; (vii) funds must remain in the designated capital escrow account until corporate minutes confirm share allotment and the Canadian register of shareholders is updated in the Ontario Business Information System (ONBIS) where applicable.Treasury clearing rails for trade settlements are: (a) SWIFT MT103/MT202 for supplier payments and intercompany flows, using CLS/CDN settlement infrastructure with, for CAD, the Lynx high-value payment system owned by Payments Canada; (b) Commercial Letters of Credit (LC) issued under UCP600 for large software licensing and infrastructure procurement from overseas suppliers, especially where the supplier requires security for transaction value above CDN 250,000; (c) for local operations inside Canada, CAD-denominated EFT via Automated Funds Transfer (AFT) and Real-Time Rail (RTR) for lower-value disbursements; (d) for multi-currency vendor settlements, a bank-originated spot FX conversion with segregated USD/CAD treasury account and transaction-level reconciliation; (e) all outbound wires must carry a clear purpose, reference a supporting invoice and purchase order, and be subject to daily transaction limits, dual-approval, and automated AML screening. The entity must never use personal accounts, shared PayPal, cryptocurrency remittance, or third-party cash couriers for trade disbursements.
      

      
E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium (with legal caveats). Offshore digital Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) and payment service providers may be used as supplementary settlement channels but cannot replace a regulated Canadian bank account for core capital escrow or operating liquidity. Any EMI conducting money services business activities in Canada—including foreign exchange dealing, remittance, or virtual currency dealing—must itself be registered as a Money Services Business (MSB) with FINTRAC and maintain a Canadian compliance program. Unregistered offshore EMIs may remain legal for invoicing or disbursement execution provided they do not hold Canadian client funds, but Canadian regulatory and banking partners will generally require 100% of treasury flows to pass through a prudentially supervised Canadian bank account for visibility and audit integrity.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

      
Master / Local File threshold: No de minimis threshold applies for cross-border transactions with non-arm's-length parties: contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation (local file) must be prepared for every non-arm's-length cross-border transaction. A s.247(3) transfer-pricing penalty is imposed only when the net upward adjustment exceeds the lesser of C$5,000,000 or 10% of the taxpayer's gross revenue for the year; a Country-by-Country Report and Master File become mandatory when the multinational group's consolidated revenue is at least C$750,000,000.

      
Economic substance scope: Canada applies the economic-substance doctrine primarily through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA s.245), anti-hybrid rules, and the beneficial-ownership requirement in treaty relief. The CRA and federal courts will disregard a social-casino structure that licenses game IP from an offshore entity with no employees, no premises, and no active decision-making; in such cases the profits are re-attributed to the Ontario operating company under s.247, and the offshore entity is assessed as a sham intermediary. Ontario itself imposes no separate Economic Substance Regulations, but the 'management and control' residency test requires that substantive board meetings, strategic risk control, and operational autonomy occur in Ontario.

      

    

    

      
Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

      
      

        Tax evasion and fraudulent suppression of revenue
        
Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction under ITA s.239(1): fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for a term not exceeding 2 years on indictment; civil gross-negligence penalty under s.163(2) equal to 50% of the understated tax amount, plus compound daily interest.

        
Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorize the false returns can be prosecuted as parties to the offence and face the same imprisonment terms; the CRA can issue director-liability assessments under s.227.1 for unpaid remittances and use jeopardy assessments and s.160 asset-transfer rules to pierce the corporate veil, freeze bank accounts, and impose departure-control orders and travel bans through Federal Court enforcement.

      

      
      

        Part XIII withholding failures on profit repatriation
        
Statutory sanction: The payer is liable under ITA s.227(8) for the entire amount that should have been withheld plus interest and a penalty of up to 10% of the unremitted amount; the CRA may assess outside the normal four-year statute bar where the failure is due to neglect or fraud; a T106 information return filed more than 24 months late disallows the deduction of the underlying payment.

        
Director personal liability: Every director who failed to cause the corporation to withhold is jointly and severally liable for the unremitted Part XIII amounts without any crown-civil-penalty forgiveness; CRA may garnish shareholder distributions, register a certificate against real property, and seek a Federal Court warrant prohibiting travel while the liability remains unsatisfied.

      

      
      

        Transfer pricing mispricing and missing contemporaneous documentation
        
Statutory sanction: CRA reassessment of taxable income under s.247(2) plus a 10% penalty on the net upward adjustment that exceeds the lesser of C$5,000,000 or 10% of gross revenue; the adjustment may be combined with the 50% gross-negligence penalty and daily compound interest; in egregious cases, a misreporting penalty of C$2,000 per document (minimum) and criminal exposure under s.239 for false statements.

        
Director personal liability: Directors may be personally assessed for the corporation's unremitted source deductions and Part XIII amounts embedded in the mispriced payments; the corporate veil can be pierced where the transfer-pricing structure is shown to be a sham and the director signed a false T2 or T106, giving the CRA recourse to the director's personal assets and judicial travel restrictions.

      

      
      

        Shell-company structure and anti-money-laundering non-compliance
        
Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, administrative monetary penalties up to C$500,000 per violation for a body corporate and C$100,000 for an individual, plus orders requiring a compliance audit and potential court penalties without statutory maximum; on conviction, directors face fines and imprisonment for up to 5 years.

        
Director personal liability: Officers, directors, and employees can be prosecuted personally for knowingly instructing or acquiescing in AML non-compliance; the court may impose restitution orders, banking prohibitions, and travel bans as bail conditions, and extradition may be sought for cross-border payment concealment, effectively negating the limited-liability shield of the Ontario corporation.
