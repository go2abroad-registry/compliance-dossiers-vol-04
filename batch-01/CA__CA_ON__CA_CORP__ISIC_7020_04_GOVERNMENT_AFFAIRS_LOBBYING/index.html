Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada — Business Registration online portal: https://www.canada.ca/en/services/taxes/business-taxes/register-business.html; Ontario provincial tax administration is conducted through the Ontario Ministry of Finance (OMF) portal: https://www.ontario.ca/page/ontario-business-tax.

E-government filing portal

A foreign investing entity must apply for a CRA Business Number (BN) using Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts) via CRA Business Registration Online (BRO), by email, fax, or mail. If the corporation expects to make taxable supplies of more than CAD 30,000 in Canada, it must open a GST/HST program account under the same BN. Ontario corporate income tax is harmonized with the federal T2 income tax return, so no separate Ontario tax identification number is required for corporate income tax; however, if the corporation has employees in Ontario, it must also register for the Ontario Employer Health Tax through the OMF portal and for a CRA payroll deduction account. A non-resident corporation carrying on business through a permanent establishment in Ontario must file an Ontario corporate tax return with the CRA as part of the T2 filing.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no check-the-box entity classification. A Business Corporation (federal or provincial) is treated per se as a 'taxable Canadian corporation' under subsection 89(1) of the Income Tax Act and is a separate taxpayer subject to Part I corporate income tax. Hybrid and branch mismatch rules in sections 18.4 and 18.5 of the Income Tax Act may recharacterize payments to or from disregarded branches or partnerships. Available domestic elections include: Section 85 rollover election for tax-deferred transfers of eligible property to a taxable Canadian corporation; the election under subsection 256(2) for associated-corporation status; and a GST/HST combined registration election for closely related corporations. A foreign entity may file Form 8832 with the U.S. IRS for U.S. tax classification, but that election has no direct Canadian domestic tax effect.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies made in Canada (excluding supplies that are zero-rated) over the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters; a non-resident supplier making supplies in Canada above this threshold, or who has a Canadian permanent establishment or carries on a Canadian business, is required to register for GST/HST. Registration is also mandatory for non-residents that solicit orders in Canada via internet unless the small-supplier threshold is met. Voluntary registration is available for persons below the threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

For goods, a zero-rated supply applies under Schedule VI, Part I when tangible personal property is exported by the supplier or delivered to a carrier for export. For services, a supply to a non-resident is zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V, section 23 where the non-resident is not registered for GST/HST, provided the service is not an excluded advisory, consulting, or professional service. Government-affairs, trade-policy, and lobbying advisory services supplied to a non-resident generally fall within the 'advisory/consulting' exclusion and therefore are subject to 13% HST even when delivered to a non-resident, unless a CRA ruling confirms that the service is a supply of neutral information that is not directed at Canadian policy or government-stakeholder influence. Zero-rating may also be available where the advisory service is wholly performed outside Canada and no benefit is enjoyed in Canada; practitioners should obtain a written per-transaction CRA technical interpretation before relying on zero-rating.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada Border Services Agency / Canadian Export Reporting System)
  • Bill of Lading or air waybill evidencing physical export of goods
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / bank transfer confirmation (SWIFT MT103) evidencing foreign-currency payment
  • Signed service agreement or contract with the non-resident recipient
  • Memo of performance describing the place and substance of the services supplied

Canada does not currently mandate real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or government-API e-invoicing. A GST/HST registrant must issue a valid tax invoice containing prescribed information (e.g., supplier's Business Number, date, description of supply, HST rate and amount payable). Electronic invoices are acceptable, and CRA provides online registration, filing, and Business Number verification services. Ontario requires electronic filing for certain provincial tax remittances and employer health tax returns, but these are not transaction-level clearance mandates. Large businesses generally must file GST/HST returns electronically.

Where an exporter or service supplier claims zero-rating without adequate documentary evidence, CRA may deny the zero-rating, reassess the supply at 13% HST, and assess arrears interest and penalties. A false or intentionally misleading statement may trigger penalties under subsection 285(1) of the Excise Tax Act of up to 25% of the tax sought to be evaded, in addition to administrative monetary penalties and interest. Gross negligence or wilful evasion may result in criminal prosecution, fines, imprisonment, and director liability assessments. CRA may also reassess beyond the normal four-year limitation period where a claim is misrepresented as a result of neglect, carelessness, or wilful default.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a free-floating currency with full and unrestricted convertibility on both the current account and the capital account and fully meets IMF Article VIII obligations. There are no exchange-control approvals, no central-bank licensing requirements for inward or outward remittances, and no limits on the timing, currency, or purpose of dividend distributions, interest payments, intercompany settlements, or repatriation of capital. Profit remittance is constrained only by tax law: non-resident withholding tax under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (statutory rate 25% on dividends and similar amounts, treaty-reduced to between 5% and 15% depending on the applicable bilateral convention and shareholding structure, for example a 5% direct-dividend rate under the Canada-United States Convention), a Part XIV branch profits tax for non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada (statutory 25%, treaty-reduced typically to 15% or 5% depending on the convention), and transfer-pricing rules under section 247 requiring arm's-length intercompany compensation. Canadian entities must report foreign-source holdings using prescribed Canadian-dollar equivalents at the Bank of Canada daily noon exchange rate and must file Form T1135 where specified foreign property has a cost amount exceeding CAD 100,000 and Form T1134 in respect of foreign affiliates; these are reporting obligations, not currency-control restrictions. There is no requirement to hold a minimum local-currency balance and no restriction on holding foreign currency in domestic or offshore accounts.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory surrender period, no mandatory conversion ratio, no retention quota, and no obligation to repatriate export proceeds, including proceeds from the export of cross-border trade-policy analysis, government-relations advisory, and foreign-stakeholder lobbying services. Exporters may lawfully retain receipts in any foreign currency within a Canadian foreign-currency account or an offshore account, and may convert such receipts freely in the spot, forward, or options markets. The only compliance obligations are tax and reporting related: foreign-exchange gains and losses must be recognized under the income-tax rules (Income Tax Act section 39(1) for income and section 39(2) for the special foreign-currency adjustment), the Canadian-dollar equivalent must be computed using an acceptable published exchange rate (the Bank of Canada daily noon rate being the accepted standard), and the exporting entity's bank must, under the PCMLTFA, report to FINTRAC any electronic funds transfer of CAD 10,000 or more that it executes or receives within a 24-hour period. The Canada Border Services Agency has no exchange-control or export-proceeds remittance remit for services, although currency movements of CAD 10,000 or more across the border must be declared. Exporters transacting with United States-based stakeholders may receive and hold USD without restriction, subject only to sanctions screening against Canadian, OFAC, EU, UK, and UN consolidated lists.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), constituted under the Bank of Canada Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. B-2), is the central bank responsible for Canadian dollar issuance, monetary policy, and the oversight of clearing and settlement systems under the Canadian Payments Act. Canada does not operate a dedicated foreign-exchange control agency; foreign-exchange administration is distributed across the Bank of Canada (which intervenes only in exceptional circumstances), the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) (which prudentially supervises federally regulated deposit-taking institutions and authorizes foreign bank branches), the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) (which enforces anti-money-laundering and anti-terrorist-financing obligations under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, S.C. 2000, c. 17), and the Department of Finance Canada together with Global Affairs Canada (which administer economic sanctions under the Special Economic Measures Act, the United Nations Act, the Justice for Victims of Corrupt Foreign Officials Act, and the Freezing Assets of Corrupt Foreign Officials Act).

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

25.0% combined federal (15%) and Ontario provincial (10%) general corporate rate for business corporations with a permanent establishment in Ontario; the federal general rate is 15% and Ontario general rate is 10%.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident shareholder (s.212(2) ITA), applied to the gross amount of the dividend before Canadian federal and provincial surtaxes; for portfolio dividends to treaty residents the rate is contractually reduced.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 must be filed no later than 6 months after the corporation's taxation year-end; the balance of tax (if any) is due on the same date, subject to quarterly instalments; the Ontario filing deadline is identical because of the collection agreement with the Province of Ontario.

Box-by-Box Return Breakdown (non-resident focus)

BoxField Non-resident guidanceTax-evasion pitfall
Box 010 – Identification block (Business Number, legal name, registered office, fiscal year-end) Captures the entity's CRA Business Number (BN), legal name, provincial/traditional corporate registration number, tax year-end date, and contact information; the BN will be in the 9-digit format followed by the 'RC' account code. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada imposes contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation (per s.247(4) ITA) when, within a fiscal year, the aggregate value of reportable cross-border transactions with a non-arm's-length non-resident exceeds CAD 1,000,000, or the aggregate principal amount of reportable cross-border loans with such a party exceeds CAD 5,000,000; the CRA also requires a Country-by-Country Report (CBCR Form RC8202) and Master File (Form RC8205) for MNE groups with consolidated revenue ≥ CAD 750,000,000 (GloBE/Pillar Two consolidated threshold) — a foreign-owner US or UK group with a Canadian lobbying subsidiary above this threshold must file CBCR through its Canadian constituent entity.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no UAE-style Economic Substance Regulation; economic substance is enforced through the statutory General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s.245 ITA), the common law sham/substance-over-form doctrine, and the corporate-residence test of central management and control. For a government-affairs advisory entity, deductible expenditures (retainer fees, political outreach, office overhead) require genuine commercial purpose and adequate personnel/office presence; the CRA will recharacterize a shell invoicing structure as a sham if it has no business rationale; large corporations with total assets over CAD 1 billion or revenue over CAD 100 million are also subject to Ontario Corporate Minimum Tax, which disallows preferential deductions; the OECD GloBE/Pillar II large-groups rules apply from fiscal years commencing on or after 31 December 2023.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Criminal tax evasion (willful evasion of federal or Ontario income tax)

Statutory sanction: Prosecution under s.239(1) ITA: fine of 50%–200% of the tax sought to be evaded plus imprisonment up to 2 years on summary conviction; on indictment, fine of 100%–300% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years. Additionally, the CRA assesses the evasion penalty under s.162/Schedule 1 and uses the Voluntary Disclosures Program for early rectification; Ontario may levy parallel penalties under the Taxation Act, 2007 for Ontario income tax evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors who direct, counsel or participate in the evasion are jointly and severally liable for the penalty and taxes; the corporate veil is pierced on fraud grounds; CRA may add the directors to the corruption registry, and the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) can impose immigration travel bans for conduct constituting organized fraud; conviction also bars directors from holding office in regulated corporations.
Gross negligence and false statements on T2 returns

Statutory sanction: Civil penalty under s.163(2) ITA equal to 50% of the understated tax for each false statement/omission; for repeated or systematic conduct, third-party penalties under s.163.2 can reach CAD 100,000 plus restitution; CRA interest at the prescribed daily compound rate accrues on the reassessment from the original filing date.

Director personal liability: The CRA can apply the 'culpable conduct' standard to hold a CFO or director personally liable where he or she signed the return with knowledge of the false statement; civil fraud liability allows creditors, shareholders and the Minister to pierce the veil; directors face monetary restitution and potential personal bankruptcy proceedings.
Transfer-pricing misstatements and inadequate contemporaneous documentation

Statutory sanction: Under s.247(10) ITA, a transfer-pricing penalty of 10% of the net adjustment applies when the adjustment exceeds CAD 5,000,000 or 10% of gross revenue (whichever is lesser) and the taxpayer has failed to make reasonable efforts to determine the arm's-length amount; failure to file information returns attracts a CAD 2,000-per-month penalty plus interest; the CRA also imposes foreign-reporting penalties for unreported foreign-source income of Canadian-controlled firms.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign intercompany agreements that commit the corporation to a non-arm's-length scheme without economic substance can be assessed as principals under s.331 ITA for the corporation's unpaid TP penalties; if the TP adjustment stems from conscious fraud, veil-piercing and criminal exposure under s.239 may follow.
Unregistered lobbying activity (Lobbying Act / Ontario Lobbyists Registration Act)

Statutory sanction: Under s.14 of the federal Lobbying Act: summary conviction – fine up to CAD 50,000 and/or imprisonment up to 6 months; indictment – fine up to CAD 200,000 and/or imprisonment up to 5 years; under Ontario's Lobbyists Registration Act, 1998, provincial offences may add further fines up to CAD 25,000; the Commissioner of Lobbying also imposes administrative civil penalties of CAD 500 to CAD 50,000 and reports violations publicly.

Director personal liability: Every director, officer, agent or partner who authorizes or acquiesces in the unregistered lobbying is guilty of the offence personally; there is no statutory indemnification, and the corporate veil does not shield principals; the Ontario Registry of Lobbyists publishes names, suspension of the right to lobby and debarment from public contracts.
AML/CFT non-compliance and money-laundering predicate offence

Statutory sanction: Penalties under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA): administrative monetary penalties (AMPs) of up to CAD 500,000 per violation for non-MSB reporting entities; conviction for predicate money laundering under the Criminal Code (s.462.31) carries imprisonment up to 10 years and fines at the discretion of the court; FINTRAC names non-compliant entities in public enforcement notices and can suspend/revoke any registration required to provide payment services.

Director personal liability: Senior officers and directors who consciously fail to establish a compliance program or knowingly process the proceeds of foreign corruption are chargeable as parties to the predicate offence; the R v Jorgensen/Canadian jurisprudence confirms director liability for wilful blindness; civil asset-freezing orders, travel bans under the Criminal Code and the Proceeds of Crime Act (restitution orders) apply to listed directors.
