Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital or escrow account is required for a Québec limited partnership; each partner must make the contribution stipulated in the partnership agreement, which may be cash, property, or services for general partners, but only cash or property for limited partners (CCQ arts. 2236–2243).

Funding & unlock path: Fire safety certification and premises inspection by the local fire prevention service in accordance with the Quebec Building Code (Chapter I, Building Code); structural load review for any storage mezzanines; CNESST (Commission des normes, de l'équité, de la santé et de la sécurité du travail) workplace health and safety registration with a compliance declaration concerning manual handling and 3D printer operation/testing areas; electrical inspection certificate for resin-curing and printer test stations.

Paid-in capital rule: No statutory minimum paid-up capital or escrow account is required for a Québec limited partnership; each partner must make the contribution stipulated in the partnership agreement, which may be cash, property, or services for general partners, but only cash or property for limited partners (CCQ arts. 2236–2243).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Canada (including via online marketplace to foreign delivery address) qualify for 0% GST/HST and 0% QST if supplier retains documentary evidence of physical export in a timely manner (typically within 60 days). Goods delivered in Canada to non-residents are generally taxable unless actually exported before 60 days.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

CAD is freely floating and fully convertible under an IMF Article VIII regime with no exchange controls on current account or capital account transactions. Profit remittance is unrestricted; Canadian-source LP income distributed to non-resident partners triggers Part XIII withholding tax (25% statutory; 15% reduced under CUSMA; 5% for qualifying U.S. corporate partners if treaty conditions are met). No prior Bank of Canada authorization or FX license is required for cross-border distributions, foreign-currency borrowing, or trade settlement. The Quebec LP faces no provincial currency restrictions, though Revenu Quebec mirrors federal withholding and reporting for non-resident partners.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada does not impose statutory surrender, mandatory conversion, or repatriation timelines on export proceeds; USD receipts (including Stripe and Shopify Payments settlements) may be retained indefinitely in a USD corporate account. The sole quantitative constraint is amended Income Tax Act s. 17.1: a non-arm's-length receivable from a non-resident must be settled within 36 months (or 120 months if a written extension agreement exists), otherwise imputed interest at the prescribed rate applies. No FINTRAC clearance is required for FX conversion, but every international EFT equal to or greater than C$10,000 is automatically reportable by the bank to FINTRAC on a cross-border EFT report.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) - prudential supervision delegated to the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); AML/CTF enforcement by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). Quebec-domiciled Money Service Businesses must additionally register with the Autorite des marches financiers (AMF) under the Loi sur les activites de services monetaires (2021).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Moderate-High as a supplementary payment rail; Low as the primary treasury warehouse. Offshore digital EMIs (Wise Business, Airwallex, Payoneer, Currenxie) may operate in Canada only as FINTRAC-registered Money Service Businesses, and if active in Quebec, under AMF monetary-services licensing. Legal constraint: the Bank Act (ss. 545-560) prohibits unlicensed deposit-taking, so EMIs cannot legally hold the LP's partner capital as bank deposits; they may only route safeguarded client funds through a licensed Schedule I chartered bank. Recommended structure: use an EMI for retail merchant payouts, low-value supplier disbursements, and small FX conversions; maintain the tier-1 bank account as the sole cash-concentration and escrow venue for partner capital and inventory financing. Stripe and Shopify Payments act as payment facilities (not EMIs) settling in CAD/USD via ACH or SWIFT to the nominated corporate account.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% (Federal 15% + Quebec 11.5% combined general corporate rate; reduced rates apply to Canadian-controlled private corporations)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

A Quebec limited partnership is a flow-through entity; distributions to partners are not classed as dividends and no Part XIII dividend withholding applies. For a hypothetical taxable Canadian corporation, Canada's statutory dividend withholding rate is 25% under Part XIII, reduced by applicable tax treaties. Non-resident corporate partners of an LP are instead subject to Part IV? (branch tax) via section 219 of the Income Tax Act; a 25% statutory branch tax (treaty-reduced) applies to the after-tax Canadian branch profit.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 per annum. CRA expects contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation for controlled transactions with non-residents when the aggregate annual value exceeds C$1,000,000, per CRA's Transfer Pricing Memorandum TPM-05. For smaller transactions, basic records must still prove arm's-length dealing.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no separate Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime comparable to UAE/BVI. Instead, the Income Tax Act's General Anti-Avoidance Rule (section 245) and Quebec's GAAR (section 1074.24 of the Taxation Act) ensure that transactions and structures lacking genuine business purpose or economic substance are re-characterized. The Canada Revenue Agency and Revenu Quebec may apply the business-purpose test, sham doctrine, and 'substance over form' principles. In particular, a limited partnership interposed solely to access treaty benefits or reduce Quebec taxes without real operations, employees, or decision-making in Quebec will attract GAAR reassessments, penalty assessments, and potential director/general partner liability.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Willful Avoidance

Statutory sanction: Under Income Tax Act subsection 163(2), penalty of 200% of the tax sought to be evaded; under section 238, fines up to $50,000 and imprisonment up to 2 years; Quebec Taxation Act section 1029 imposes fines and imprisonment up to 18 months; Criminal Code fraud or false statements can lead to additional imprisonment up to 10 years.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners are jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions and withheld GST/QST (Income Tax Act section 227.1; Excise Tax Act section 323; Quebec Tax Administration Act section 13.1); individuals who direct or knowingly participate in evasion face personal civil restitution, court-ordered travel bans, asset freezes, and imprisonment.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement and Sham Documentation

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act subsection 247(3) imposes a transfer pricing penalty of 10% of the net adjustment, with a minimum penalty of C$5,000 unless reduced by due diligence; CRA may reassess beyond the normal three-year limitation period in cases of misrepresentation; Quebec Transfer Pricing penalty under the Taxation Act can mirror federal sanctions and add provincial fines.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly or negligently approve or prepare false intercompany documents may be personally liable for negligence penalties and restitution; under Canadian corporate veil-piercing jurisprudence, a nominal partner corporation's separate status may be disregarded, imposing civil liability on its shareholders and directors; criminal enforcement for false statements may result in travel bans.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance (GAAR)

Statutory sanction: GAAR under Income Tax Act section 245 denies the tax benefit and may recharacterize the transactions; Quebec GAAR (Taxation Act section 1074.24) imposes a provincial penalty equal to 10% of the tax benefit in abusive avoidance transactions; CRA may also apply subsection 163(2) penalties for false statements; Revenu Quebec can issue a formal assessment for the additional tax, interest, and penalties.

Director personal liability: The general partner of an LP is personally liable for all partnership obligations except where the partnership is commercially legitimate; if the LP is found to be a 'quasi-corporation' or pure conduit, creditors and tax authorities can pierce the veil to reach partners; directors of a corporate partner may be jointly liable for unpaid corporate taxes and employee source deductions; travel bans and imprisonment may follow if the structure is part of a tax fraud scheme.
Anti-Money Laundering and Payment-Rail Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: PCMLTFA administrative monetary penalties for entities can reach C$1,000,000 per violation (C$500,000 for individuals); Criminal Code section 462.31 makes money laundering an indictable offence punishable by up to 10 years' imprisonment and a potentially unlimited fine; FINTRAC may issue compliance orders to suspend/revoke or impose terms; Quebec's Anti-Corruption Act may impose additional civil penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who authorized or participated in the money-laundering offence can be personally prosecuted and imprisoned up to 10 years; corporate directors are jointly and severally liable for fines imposed on the entity unless they exercised due diligence; travel bans, asset freezes, and court-ordered recognizance conditions are common for money-laundering defendants; civil restitution to victims of fraud also applies.
