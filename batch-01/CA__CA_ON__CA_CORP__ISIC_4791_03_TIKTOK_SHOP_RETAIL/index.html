Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA / Agence du revenu du Canada) under the Excise Tax Act (ETA) and Income Tax Act (ITA); registration portals include Business Registration Online (BRO), My Business Account (MyBA), and GST/HST NETFILE. Ontario corporate income tax is collected by CRA on behalf of the Ontario Ministry of Finance under the Ontario Corporations Tax Act; the integrated HST in Ontario requires no separate Ontario revenue registration.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Incorporate under the Canada Business Corporations Act via Corporations Canada or under the Ontario Business Corporations Act via the Ontario Ministry of Government and Consumer Services; obtain the federal/Ontario incorporation number. Step 2: Obtain the 9-digit Business Number (BN) through CRA BRO or Form RC1; allocate the program accounts RP (Part I corporate tax), RC0001 (GST/HST), and RT0001 (payroll) as required. Step 3: For a foreign-owned Ontario corporation, submit BN registration with articles of incorporation, registered office address in Ontario, director particulars, and, where applicable, a non-resident agent for service (Form RC4512C); CRA will tag the account for non-resident ownership. Step 4: Register the GST/HST account; registration is retroactive to the earlier of the date of incorporation or the date commercial activity commenced in Canada. Step 5: No separate Ontario HST account is required because the provincial component is harmonized and administered federally; Ontario corporate tax filings are made via CRA using the same BN plus Ontario tax code.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation – a Canadian federal/provincial Business Corporation is a resident corporation under ITA ss. 2(1) and 250(1) and is taxed on worldwide income; no pass-through or S-corporation analog exists. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation – a Canadian federal/provincial Business Corporation is a resident corporation under ITA ss. 2(1) and 250(1) and is taxed on worldwide income; no pass-through or S-corporation analog exists. Federal Part I rates: 15% general (after 10% abatement and 28% general reduction) and 9% small business rate on the first C$500,000 of active business income if the corporation qualifies as a Canadian-Controlled Private Corporation (CCPC); Ontario rates: 11.5% general and 3.2% small business rate. Because CA_CORP is controlled by non-residents, it is not a CCPC and is excluded from the small business deduction; it remains taxable as a resident corporation and may be subject to Part XIV branch tax or Part XIII withholding on outbound dividends.

Available election forms

Canada has no check-the-box regime; classification is automatic by statute. Available corporate elections: (i) ITA s. 85(1) rollover of property into the corporation on a tax-deferred basis; (ii) ITA s. 85(2)/(3) rollover of accounts receivable and debts; (iii) ITA s. 256(9) associated-corporation designation; (iv) ITA s. 216 election for non-residents' rental/timber income; (v) Part XIV branch tax elections for non-resident corporations; (vi) GST/HST elections: s. 227 Quick Method (relevant for a TikTok Shop retailer; remittance rate 8.8% for retail goods and 13% for most services in Ontario, in lieu of input tax credits), s. 211 closely-related corporation election, s. 156 deemed-supply election for closely related registrants, and s. 157 cash-basis election. An election under ETA s. 211.1 may also apply where digital platform rules deem the platform to be the supplier; the retailer should confirm whether it or the TikTok platform collects HST on consumer sales in Canada.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

C$30,000 – a 'small supplier' must register for GST/HST once it exceeds C$30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies in any single calendar quarter or in the aggregate over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters; non-resident persons are subject to the same threshold unless they make supplies through a permanent establishment in Canada, in which case registration is mandatory. The threshold applies to the CA_CORP entity, not to the TikTok platform.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods: zero-rated under Part V, Schedule VI, ETA (item 1) where the supplier exports tangible personal property by a common carrier or delivers it to a non-resident consignee for immediate export, and proof of exportation is obtained. Service supplies to non-residents are zero-rated under item 7, Part V, Schedule VI where the non-resident is not registered for GST/HST and is not present in Canada at the time the service is supplied, except services rendered in respect of real property situated in Canada or old goods in Canada. For a TikTok Shop retailer, supply chain finance, marketing, and social-commerce support services provided to offshore sellers qualify as zero-rated if the recipient is a non-resident and the services are not connected to Canadian real property or a Canadian establishment. The zero-rating claim requires documentary proof that the goods left Canada within 60 days for commercial shipments and that the purchaser is a non-resident for services; for dropship consignment arrangements, the retailer must retain the contract identifying the offshore principal and the final foreign destination.

  • Form B13A Canada Customs Export Declaration filed through the CBSA CCS/CADEX system for goods exceeding C$2,000 or for controlled/prohibited goods
  • Bill of Lading or House Air Waybill evidencing physical export from Canada to the foreign destination
  • Commercial invoice satisfying ETA s. 3 and CUSMA/Customs Tariff format requirements, showing exporter, consignee, INCOTERMS, and description of goods
  • TikTok Shop payout settlement report and/or Wise B2B SWIFT MT103 confirming receipt of foreign-currency proceeds
  • Proof of non-resident status of the buyer for zero-rated services (e.g., certificate of residence, non-resident registration exemption evidence)
  • Carrier tracking manifest with customs release stamp or border clearance record
  • Dropship contract and platform order log evidencing export direction and the absence of Canadian fulfillment for the specific order

Canada does not currently impose a real-time fiscal clearance, API stamping, or digital validation mandate for B2B or B2C invoices. CRA has announced a voluntary phased adoption of the PEPPOL e-invoicing framework beginning in 2026, initially for government procurement; there is no statutory obligation to transmit invoice data to CRA before or at the time of supply. Registrants must issue tax invoices that meet ETA s. 3 requirements (seller identity, BN, date, description, tax status, consideration, and HST amount or zero-rated indicator) and retain electronic records, including TikTok Shop payout statements, platform transaction reports, e-receipts, and Wise B2B settlement records, for at least six years. Failure to maintain the invoice and export evidence will invalidate ITCs and zero-rating claims on audit.

Canada imposes no statutory 180-day FX repatriation deadline for export proceeds; the 180-day value is retained as the schema default and corresponds to CRA administrative review windows, not a legal repatriation requirement. CRA may reassess GST/HST within 4 years generally, 7 years where misrepresentation is attributable to neglect, carelessness, or wilful default, and without limitation in cases of fraud (ETA s. 298, ITA s. 152(4)). Where export documentation is deficient, zero-rating is reversed and the seller must self-assess HST on the supply with compound daily interest under ETA s. 280 from the original reporting date. Specific penalties include: ETA s. 273 (25% of the evaded amount for false statements or omissions), ETA s. 280.1 (greater of C$1,000 and 50% of the false refund/rebate claim), and ITA s. 163(2) (50% of understated tax for gross negligence). Criminal exposure arises under ITA s. 239 and Criminal Code s. 380 for tax evasion, with fines up to 200% of the amount evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years. Additional CRA scrutiny applies to related-party export sales: transactions with a foreign affiliate or offshore brand controller are subject to transfer-pricing review under ITA s. 247, and the CRA cross-border e-commerce audit program will cross-match TikTok Shop settlement data, customs declarations, and bank inflows; retrospective clawback may include the denial of input tax credits, reassessment of the exporter's net tax, and referral to the Public Prosecution Service of Canada for wilful evasion.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada has accepted Article VIII obligations of the IMF Articles of Agreement and maintains a fully liberalized current and capital account. The Canadian dollar (CAD) is freely convertible at market rates; there is no exchange control legislation, no transaction licensing, and no prior approval requirement for cross-border flows. Current account payments, including dividends, interest, royalties, management fees, and e-commerce profit remittances from TikTok Shop retail operations, are unrestricted. Capital account items, including foreign direct investment, portfolio investment, intercompany loans, and shareholder advances, are likewise unrestricted at the exchange-control level, subject only to: (i) the review thresholds of the Investment Canada Act for non-Canadian acquisitions of control of a Canadian business (net benefit review above the prescribed enterprise value threshold, approximately CAD 1.27 billion for WTO investors in 2024, and CAD 5 million for non-WTO investors in reviewable sectors); and (ii) Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. 1 (5th Supp.)), imposing 25% withholding tax on dividend and similar remittances to non-residents, reducible under applicable tax treaties (5%-15% under the Canada-US Convention; 10%-15% under the Canada-China Agreement). Profit remittance hurdles are therefore tax and documentary in nature, not currency controls: the Board must evidence a valid dividend resolution, the payee must furnish treaty-residence certification, and the payer must file Form NR4 and remit withheld tax to the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA). No governmental pre-clearance is required for inbound or outbound treasury flows.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada abolished statutory exchange controls in 1951 and imposes no surrender, conversion, or repatriation obligation on export proceeds, and no mandatory conversion ratio exists. Under the Currency Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. C-52) and the Bank of Canada Act, a Canadian resident may freely hold foreign currency (USD, CNY, HKD) in permitted foreign currency accounts at any Schedule I chartered bank or authorized foreign bank. TikTok Shop settlement proceeds received from TikTok Pte. Ltd. (Singapore) in USD may therefore be retained indefinitely in USD-denominated operating accounts, converted to CAD at the holder's discretion, or swept into multicurrency treasury structures without any mandated conversion. The only statutory obligations are passive reporting thresholds under the PCMLTFA and FINTRAC regulations: large cash transaction reports at CAD 10,000, electronic funds transfer (EFT) reports for transfers of CAD 10,000 or more, cross-border currency and monetary instrument reports to CBSA at CAD 10,000, and the client risk assessment, record-keeping, and registration duties imposed on banks and money services businesses. No export-receipts surveillance regime comparable to active FX-control jurisdictions applies.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) under the Bank of Canada Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. B-2), acting in coordination with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for prudential bank supervision, Payments Canada for clearing and settlement infrastructure, and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for AML/CFT supervision under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (S.C. 2000, c. 17) (PCMLTFA). The Minister of Finance administers the Exchange Fund Account; no separate foreign exchange control agency exists.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium for offshore digital EMI deployment; High for payment-facilitation sub-rails when paired with a Schedule I bank. Wise (Wise Canada Inc.), Payoneer, Airwallex, Stripe, PayPal Business, and dLocal are not Canadian chartered banks and are legally prohibited from accepting deposits under the Bank Act (S.C. 1991, c. 46). They operate in Canada as Money Services Businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC under the PCMLTFA, or as foreign payment service providers accessing Canada through banking partners. Key legal constraints: (1) an MSB may not hold end-user funds indefinitely; the Retail Payment Activities Act (S.C. 2021, c. 23), administered by the Bank of Canada, now requires registration and safeguarding of funds held beyond the settlement window (typically trust or escrow at a Canadian financial institution); (2) an EU/UK e-money (EMI) licence held by a parent entity does not extend to Canada and does not authorize deposit-taking onshore; (3) Airwallex maintains a Canadian MSB registration and offers multi-currency wallets without CDIC deposit insurance; (4) Payoneer settles through U.S. partner banks and requires a Canadian operational account for CAD conversion. Conclusion: Wise B2B and Airwallex are legally viable for TikTok Shop payout collection, FX conversion, and micro-supplier disbursements, but they cannot replace a Schedule I bank operating account for statutory escrow, CDIC coverage, trade finance, and OSFI-grade governance. The recommended topology is a dual-rail structure: a Schedule I bank as the primary designated clearing account, with a Wise B2B or Airwallex sub-rail capped at CAD 10,000 per transaction and swept daily to the primary account to minimize MSB safeguard exposure and FINTRAC EFT aggregation risk.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

25% combined general rate (federal Part I 15% + Ontario 10% general corporate tax rate); applies to active business income of a non-CCPC or to income above the small-business threshold.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax (s. 212(2) ITA) on dividends paid by the Canadian corporation to non-resident shareholders, subject to treaty reduction: Canada–US DTA Article X – 5% on gross dividends paid to a US corporation owning at least 10% of the Canadian payer's voting shares (and meeting Limitation on Benefits), otherwise 15% for portfolio/individual holdings.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: No statutory CAD threshold exists for preparing transfer-pricing documentation; contemporaneous documentation must be prepared by the T2 filing-due date for every reportable s. 247 transaction. However, Form T106 (with Schedule 10) becomes mandatory where the aggregate fair market value of non-arm's length cross-border transactions with a single non-resident exceeds C$1,000,000 in the taxation year. Canada has not enacted the OECD Master/Local File thresholds as law; CRA applies OECD principles and expects both the Master File and Local File 'equivalent' data for a TikTok Shop business exceeding the T106 threshold. Intercompany benchmarks must cover platform commissions, seller payouts, influencer fees, logistics, and IP royalties.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not impose a GCC-style Economic Substance Regulation. Substance risk is instead enforced by: (1) the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s. 245 ITA), which invalidates avoidance transactions lacking a bona fide commercial purpose where the tax benefit amounts to misuse/abuse; (2) the Canada–US treaty Limitation on Benefits (Article XXIX-A) and the OECDA MLI-consistent Principal Purpose Test trend; and (3) CRA transfer-pricing 'most reliable method' reviews, which demand real Ontario employees, inventory risk, contract conclusion, and cash-management functions to support profit attribution. A functionless Ontario pass-through exposed to the TikTok Shop profit stream will be denied treaty benefits and recharacterized.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Gross Negligence

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction under s. 239 ITA: fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded plus imprisonment up to 5 years on indictment (2 years on summary conviction). Civil gross-negligence penalty under s. 163(2): 50% of the amount of tax understated. CRA may also enforce full interest accruals and a formal 'federal assessment' before the tax court.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorize or knowingly participate in the false filing are personally prosecutable as parties to the offence; the corporate veil is pierced for fraud; courts may order restitution, forfeiture of proceeds, and travel bans as part of a criminal disposition; no insurance indemnity covers criminal fines.
Failure to File and Remit

Statutory sanction: s. 162(1): penalty of 5% on the unpaid amount plus 1% per complete month of default (max 12 months); repeat filings 10% + 2% per month (max 20 months); prescribed interest on arrears (daily compounding, approx. 8-9% p.a.); unremitted Part XIII exposes the payer to the full 25% liability plus s. 227(8) 10% penalty and interest.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable under s. 227.1 ITA for the corporation's unremitted payroll and Part XIII withholdings and GST/HST where they knew or should have known, unless the due-diligence defence applies; CRA registers director liability certificates in Federal Court and may pursue personal bankruptcy, garnishment, and denial of travel (via criminal fraud charges) if the non-remittance is deliberate.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: s. 247(3) penalty: 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment where the adjustment exceeds the lesser of C$5,000,000 or 10% of gross revenue; the CRA may recharacterize the transactions and disallow the deduction entirely; late/absent T106 incurs C$2,500/counterparty penalties; additional net income is subject to ordinary tax plus interest.

Director personal liability: Directors are not statutorily liable for ordinary Part I tax or TP penalties, but a director who signs the mispriced T2 knowing it to be false faces s. 239 criminal exposure and gross-negligence attribution; directors who direct artificial intercompany pricing may be joined in a s. 227.1 claim only for related withholdings, not for TP itself.
Shell Company / Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: GAAR under s. 245 ITA: the avoidance benefit is denied, the royalty/dividend payments may be recharacterized, treaty-based WHT concessions are reversed to the 25% statutory rate, interest and gross-negligence penalties apply on misstatement; CRA may also apply the Canada–US LOB to deny the 5%/10%/0% treaty positions retroactively.

Director personal liability: Outside criminal fraud, the corporate veil protects directors from GAAR itself; however, directors who approve the sham structure may be liable to the CRA for grossnegligence penalties assessed on the company? no - more accurate: they risk personal criminal conspiracy charges when the sham is used to evade tax, and civil liability to other shareholders for breach of fiduciary duty.
Part XIII Withholding and Non-Resident Distribution Failures

Statutory sanction: The Canadian payer is liable for the entire 25% statutory amount that should have been withheld, plus s. 227(8) 10% penalty on the unreported Part XIII amount, plus prescribed interest; NR4 late-filing penalties apply under s. 162(6)-(7); on audit, the CRA assesses the payer, not the recipient, for the deficiency.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable under s. 227.1 for all Part XIII amounts the corporation failed to withhold and remit, regardless of whether the tax was collected from the beneficiary, unless the due-diligence defence is proven; the CRA commonly pursues director bank accounts and cross-border travel restraint is possible in aggravated fraud proceedings.
