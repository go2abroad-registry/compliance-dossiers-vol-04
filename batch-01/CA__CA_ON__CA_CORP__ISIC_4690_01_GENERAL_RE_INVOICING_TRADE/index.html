Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); business registration portal: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services.html); provincial corporate registry: Ontario Ministry of Government and Consumer Services (Ontario Business Registry, https://www.ontario.ca/page/ontario-business-registry).

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity deriving Canadian-source business income or making taxable supplies in Canada must obtain a 9-digit Business Number (BN) from the CRA. For a non-resident corporation, file Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) or the GST/HST Non-Resident Registration package; CRA assigns a BN with program accounts: corporate income tax (RC), GST/HST (RT), payroll (RP), and import/export (RM/RD). Registration may be completed online via CRA My Business Account or by mailing the completed RC1/RC1A forms to the CRA Sudbury Tax Centre. A non-resident corporation must also appoint a representative (fiscal agent) if it has not established Canadian residency, and such representative must be a Canadian resident or a Canadian corporation authorized to act. The GST/HST account is effective as of the date the entity ceases to be a small supplier or earlier if voluntarily elected. For Ontario HST purposes, the federal BN (with RT program account) is the sole tax identifier; no separate provincial VAT ID exists.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no IRS-style 'check-the-box' classification regime; corporations are ipso facto entity-level taxpayers under the Income Tax Act (Canada), Part I. Available elections relevant to cross-border re-invoicing: (1) GST/HST joint election under subsection 167(2) of the Excise Tax Act for supplies between closely related corporations (where the recipient is not a financial institution and the supply is not a prescribed supply); (2) Election under section 156 of the Excise Tax Act to not use the Quick Method; (3) Annual GST/HST filing election for non-resident registrants under section 239 of the Excise Tax Act; (4) Income Tax Act section 216 election for a non-resident to file a separate return for Canadian rental income and property income; (5) Transfer-pricing contemporaneous documentation election (no formal election but a documentation obligation may be satisfied by electing to use the CRA Form T106 or RC4600 filing method); (6) Hybrid-mismatch rules under sections 18.2 and 18.21 of the Income Tax Act (no elective carve-out is available; the rules apply mandatorily to payments structured to exploit hybrid mismatches).

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000: an entity is a 'small supplier' and exempt from mandatory GST/HST registration unless its worldwide taxable supplies (including associated persons' supplies, excluding capital gains and zero-rated exports) exceed CAD 30,000 in any single calendar quarter or in the aggregate over the last four consecutive calendar quarters; once the threshold is exceeded, registration is mandatory and becomes effective as of the beginning of the quarter in which the threshold was crossed (Excise Tax Act, subsection 240(1)); exporters and non-residents may voluntarily register below the threshold to claim input tax credits and zero-rate supplies.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Part VI of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act zero-rates: (a) supplies of tangible personal property exported by the supplier or by a non-resident recipient in accordance with section 1 and 2 of Part V (property delivered to a carrier for export, or export documentation obtained); (b) supplies of services to non-residents under section 7 of Part V where the service is in respect of tangible personal property situated outside Canada, or the service is performed wholly outside Canada, or the service is supplied in respect of real property outside Canada, or (for advisory/similar services) the recipient is a non-resident and the services are not for consumption in Canada; (c) arranging for or assisting in the export of goods by a registrant (section 8 of Part V), which covers a re-invoicing intermediary or trade agent whose supply consists of arranging the export of Canadian goods; (d) commercial re-invoicing hubs may claim zero-rating where title to the goods passes outside Canada (delivery terms DAP/FOB foreign port) and the supplier maintains contemporaneous records proving disposal outside Canada. For supplies of goods that are not shipped from Canada (pure offshore re-invoicing, STO/string sales), the place-of-supply rules in rules 1-3 apply: if the goods are not delivered in Canada and are not exported from Canada, the supply is generally outside the scope of GST/HST if the supplier is a non-resident, but for a Canadian supplier it may be deemed to be made in Canada or outside Canada depending on whether the goods are situated in Canada at the time of supply; such offshore intercompany transfers should be documented as offshore services or agency disbursements rather than Canadian taxable supplies to eliminate unintended domestic VAT.

Canada does not currently mandate real-time fiscal clearance (no continuous transaction control, CTC, API stamping, or digital signature regime analogous to e-invoicing mandates in Italy or France). However: (1) all monthly GST/HST registrants with a filing threshold of CAD 5 million in taxable supplies must file and remit electronically through CRA My Business Account or via EDI/XML protocols (mandatory electronic filing since 2023); (2) the Government of Canada has adopted the Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Standard (PEPPOL BIS Billing 3.0, based on the Pan-European Public Procurement On-Line (PEPPOL) network) and, effective 2023, suppliers to federal departments must be able to receive and process e-invoices if the department requests them; (3) taxpayers must retain valid tax invoices (supporting GST/HST registration numbers of supplier and recipient, the amount of tax charged, and the recipient's name) in a retrievable electronic or paper format for six years, but no XML schema validation or government fiscal stamp is imposed; (4) Revenu Québec operates a separate e-invoicing and request-to-pay system under the Pan-Canadian standard for provincial purposes, but this does not apply to Ontario registrants; (5) CRA follows the OECD TIC 3.0 interoperability guidelines for taxpayer data exchange, and GST/HST return information is transmitted using the EDI (Electronic Data Interchange) protocol.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a freely floating exchange rate regime with no exchange controls and full convertibility of the Canadian dollar on both current and capital account items. Dividends, interest, management fees, trade settlements, and profit remittances may be transferred cross-border without foreign-exchange regulatory approval. The only material hurdles are tax compliance (e.g., Part XIII withholding tax, transfer-pricing documentation), PCMLTFA reporting thresholds, and bank-level client due diligence. No Canadian central bank authorization is required for currency conversion or remittance.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no export-proceeds surrender rules and no mandatory conversion ratio. A Canadian re-invoicing exporter is not legally required to repatriate or convert foreign-currency export proceeds. However, all revenues and foreign-currency transactions must be reported in Canadian dollars for Income Tax Act purposes using the relevant Bank of Canada or book exchange rate, and cash or negotiable instrument transactions of CAD 10,000 or more trigger FINTRAC reporting obligations.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); foreign exchange policy and regulatory oversight are administered by the Department of Finance Canada, with AML/trade-screening supervision by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Low as a primary treasury or escrow provider. Offshore digital EMIs and non-bank fintech institutions are not chartered banks under Canada's Bank Act and cannot lawfully accept deposits or provide full clearing/escrow services from within Canada. They may be used as ancillary payment-service providers for FX execution or remittance support, but the core capital escrow, commercial LC, and SWIFT settlement infrastructure must sit with a Schedule I bank or an authorized foreign bank branch duly registered in Canada.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound shareholder capital is routed as a SWIFT MT103 business credit transfer to the Canadian bank through a correspondent clearing relationship. Canada does not mandate a central-bank authorization or purpose-code registry; however, for FINTRAC client identification and the bank's internal Accountable-Transaction monitoring, the remittance information line must clearly state 'capital subscription' or 'paid-in capital / foreign direct investment.' The first injection should be accompanied by a certified board resolution, share subscription ledger, source-of-funds statements, and a bank-signed escrow direction where the account is a dedicated capital escrow account. Deposits denominated in foreign currency will be converted at the bank's posted arm's-length rate, and the resulting Canadian-dollar balance will be recorded for Income Tax Act reporting.Trade settlements to third-party suppliers are cleared through the following compliant rails: SWIFT MT103 (customer credit transfer) for direct wire payments to overseas beneficiary accounts; SWIFT MT700 (documentary letter of credit) for import/export purchase transactions requiring payment against presentation of shipping documents; SWIFT MT760 (standby letter of credit) as a performance or payment guarantee; and bank-to-bank correspondent transfers through Fedwire or CHIPS for USD clearances involving U.S. counterparties. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined statutory headline rate: 15% federal general corporate income tax + 11.5% Ontario general corporate income tax. Non-resident-owned or captive re-invoicing entities do not qualify for CCPC preferential rates.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% statutory Part XIII withholding tax under ITA s. 212(2) on dividends paid by a Canadian-resident corporation to non-residents; a Canadian re-invoicing hub is the withholding agent and must remit on gross dividends.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: No statutory revenue threshold requires a Local File or Master File in Canada; however, contemporaneous TP documentation is required as a condition of the 'reasonable efforts' defense. Mandatory filing thresholds: (i) T106 Information Return if non-arm's-length transactions with any non-resident exceed CAD 1,000,000; (ii) Country-by-Country Report (CBCR) where the multinational group consolidates revenue of at least CAD 750,000,000; (iii) TP penalty applies when the gross adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 and 10% of gross revenue.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone Economic Substance Regulations like offshore financial centres; nevertheless, an economic substance doctrine is enforced through: (i) General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR, ITA s. 245) denying tax benefits from transactions lacking genuine business purpose; (ii) Transfer pricing recharacterization under ITA s. 247; (iii) treaty shopping defences under DTA LOB/PPT articles; (iv) the principle of corporate residence requiring central management and control in Canada with real employees, premises, contracts and risk management; and (v) CRS/FATCA reporting exposing low-substance shell arrangements. An Ontario re-invoicing hub must demonstrate real decision-making, risk-bearing, and intercompany contracts to avoid CRA reattribution of profit to a foreign principal.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Unremitted Part XIII Withholding Tax (Profit Repatriation Failure)

Statutory sanction: Reassessment for the full gross amount of unwithheld tax plus CRA prescribed interest; gross negligence penalty of 50% of the unremitted amount (ITA s. 163(2)); failure to deduct/withhold under ITA s. 227(8) creates a separate liability equal to the amount not withheld.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for the corporation's unremitted Part XIII tax under ITA s. 227.1, including interest and penalties; CRA can collect from personal assets, register liens, and pursue civil recovery; egregious failure may lead to an injunction prohibiting the director from leaving Canada and surrender of passports.
Transfer Pricing Misdeclaration and T106 Non-Filing

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing recharacterization adjusting arm's-length price; a penalty equal to 10% of the gross transfer pricing adjustment when the adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 and 10% of gross revenue, unless the taxpayer demonstrates 'reasonable efforts'; separate T106 penalties up to CAD 25,000.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly approve false TP documentation or participate in a scheme to shift profits to a tax haven may be assessed as promoters or counsel under ITA s. 239 (counselling tax evasion), exposing them to fines of 50%-200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment up to 5 years; civil liability can arise for breach of fiduciary duty to creditors.
GAAR - Artificial Shell / Low Substance Structure

Statutory sanction: The tax benefit is annulled and the transaction recharacterized; GAAR penalty of 25% of the benefit under ITA s. 245(8); redetermination of foreign tax credits and potential reallocation of income to the true economic owner.

Director personal liability: Directors of a shell re-invoicing corporation can be personally assessed for the recovered tax if the corporation is insolvent or when a 'stop order' under the Income Tax Act is used to collect unpaid corporate tax from directors; criminal liability arises where misleading statements or books were created to conceal the arrangement.
Criminal Tax Evasion and Money Laundering

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction under ITA s. 239: fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded plus imprisonment of up to 5 years; under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) penalties for unregistered MSB activity and missing FATCA/FINTRAC reporting reach CAD 500,000 on summary conviction and CAD 1,000,000 on indictment; customs and trade misinvoicing penalties apply under the Customs Act.

Director personal liability: The 'piercing the corporate veil' doctrine applies in cases of fraud and sham transactions; director personal liability covers restitution, legal costs, fines, and civil damages; courts may issue travel bans, surrender of passports, freeze personal bank accounts, and impose imprisonment; directorial resignation does not extinguish liability if the director was complicit in the evasion.
