Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA / Agence du revenu du Canada); Business Registration Online portal and My Business Account; provincial counterpart for Ontario: Ministry of Finance (Ontario) / Ontario Business Registry.

E-government filing portal

A non-resident limited partnership conducting commercial activity in Canada must obtain a federal Business Number (BN) from the CRA before registering for GST/HST. In Ontario, the partnership must also register through the Ontario Business Registry for the provincial tax accounts, including the Employer Health Tax (EHT) account if applicable. A non-resident general partner or agent must file Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Accounts) with the CRA, provide the partnership agreement and declaration of non-resident status, and designate a Canadian representative for service of process. The LP itself is the registrant for GST/HST and other federal accounts using its BN; a separate Ontario tax registration identifier (if any) is consolidated under the same BN where applicable. Required supporting documents include: certified copy of the limited partnership certificate or declaration from the jurisdiction of formation, proof of address of principal place of business in Ontario, identification of all general partners, and, if the partnership is a specified member carrying on taxable supply activity in Canada, the T2/T5013 information return filing obligations must be accepted and the CRA’s non-resident account application completed. Registration is generally required before the first taxable supply is made in Canada or before importing goods into Canada if GST/HST applies.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

A Canadian limited partnership formed in Ontario is a flow-through entity for income tax purposes and is not itself a taxable entity, but its members are allocated income/loss and are liable for tax. For GST/HST purposes, the partnership is a separate registrant supplied by its members. Ontario permits a limited partnership to elect to be treated as a partnership for Ontario income tax (default) without an elective mechanism. A non-resident limited partnership may be subject to the 'Canadian limited partnership' anti-deferral rules under the Income Tax Act section 94.1/94.2 where it holds foreign property and has Canadian-resident members, unless an election to avoid the application is available. The GST/HST regime does not generally permit a 'check-the-box' election equivalent to U.S. entity classification; however, the CRA allows joint election under section 273 of the Excise Tax Act for closely related corporations, and for partnerships, an election may be made to treat the partnership as a registrant where the partnership itself carries on the commercial activity. For hybrid mismatch purposes, Canada has adopted BEPS Action 2 rules under section 18.4 and 18.6 of the Income Tax Act, which may deny a deduction for hybrid mismatch amounts if a Canadian partner is taxed on a different basis. If the LP is treated as a partnership in Canada but as an opaque entity in a foreign jurisdiction, specific elections to treat certain payments as dividends or interest may be prescribed by treaty and domestic rules.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 worldwide taxable supplies for non-resident entities (unless making taxable supplies in Canada; non-residents must register if they make taxable supplies in Canada unless they qualify for the exception for a 'non-resident that does not carry on business in Canada' under the Excise Tax Act section 240(2.1)). For resident Canadian businesses and non-residents carrying on business in Canada, the threshold is CAD 30,000 of total taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies) in a calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating under GST/HST is available for exported goods and certain services under Schedule VI of the Excise Tax Act, provided the supplier satisfies the 'export conditions' in section 23 (goods) or the 'outside Canada' and 'consumed wholly outside Canada' criteria for services (section 25). For a surgical suture needle assembly business, zero-rating applies to the sale of goods supplied to a non-resident and shipped directly to a destination outside Canada, supplied under a contract for sale to a non-resident, and exported as part of the supply. If the goods are consigned to a Canadian exporter in the United States or elsewhere, the supply may be zero-rated under section 23 or 25 as applicable. Manufacturing and processing operations in Ontario do not themselves qualify for export zero-rating unless the goods are physically exported. For services such as contract manufacturing or assembly services performed in Canada on tangible personal property for a non-resident, zero-rating may be available if the goods are subsequently exported and the services are supplied to a non-resident who is not a consumer. The seller must obtain and keep evidence of export, including transport documents, customs declarations, and any other proof acceptable to the CRA. Sale of surgical sutures to Canadian hospitals is not zero-rated and is subject to HST at 13%, unless the purchaser provides a resale certificate or an exemption certificate under the Excise Tax Act. Supplies of medical devices and human tissue products may be exempt if they fall under the definition of 'medical or assistive devices' in Schedule VI, but surgical suture needles and absorbable sutures generally do not qualify as exempt medical devices unless specifically listed; classification must be reviewed carefully. The exporter must charge and collect GST/HST on supplies made to a non-resident that are not zero-rated if the supply is not an exported supply.

  • Customs Export Declaration: Form B13A (Export Declaration) filed with the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CES) or via the Canadian Single Window Integrated Import Declaration, for exports with value exceeding CAD 2,000 or controlled/prohibited goods; for surgical suture needles, an export declaration may not be mandatory if below threshold, but must be retained as proof.
  • Commercial Invoice: Invoice describing goods, quantities, value, parties, terms of sale (e.g., FOB, CIF), and statement that goods are exported
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill: Through bill of lading or ocean/air carrier document showing consignment to a destination outside Canada and the foreign consignee
  • Packing List: Detailed packing list matching the commercial invoice and shipping marks
  • Proof of Export: Cargo control documents (e.g., ACI eManifest cargo report, domestic bill of lading, forwarder’s cargo receipt) evidencing actual departure of goods from Canada
  • Inward Remittance Certificate: Evidence of foreign exchange repatriation from the foreign buyer, such as a bank SWIFT MT103 transfer receipt naming the Canadian seller as beneficiary, or a payment instruction from the foreign buyer's bank showing the foreign currency was received in Canada; the CRA does not statutorily mandate a specific 'inward remittance certificate' form, but documentary evidence of payment from a non-resident is expected in audit to support that the supply was made to a non-resident

Canada has not implemented a mandatory real-time e-invoicing or fiscal clearance mandate such as Peppol BIS or SAT-style digital stamping. The CRA requires GST/HST registrants to issue and retain valid tax invoices in a retrievable format, including the BN and total tax charged. Electronic invoicing is permissible if the electronic record is accurate, accessible, and readable. The CRA may require that invoices be produced in French or English. For imports, the CBSA requires electronic customs declarations via the Canadian Single Window / eManifest portal, and for zero-rated exports of goods, the exporter must keep the goods' export documentation and prove that the goods were exported. Real-time electronic reporting of GST/HST is not mandated; GST/HST returns are filed electronically via the CRA’s GST/HST NETFILE portal on a periodic basis. No digital fiscal stamp is required. Ontario does not have a separate real-time clearance system for HST; the HST is administered by the CRA as part of the Harmonized Sales Tax under a single federal administration.

The CRA may reassess GST/HST zero-rated export transactions within the normal reassessment period of four years from the filing date if it determines that the supplier failed to obtain sufficient evidence of export or that the supply was not properly zero-rated. If the period is extended or in cases of misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, or wilful default, reassessment may be made without a time limit under subsection 298(1) of the Excise Tax Act. The penalty for knowingly making a false statement or omission in a return, or participating in a false GST/HST refund claim, is the greater of CAD 250 and 40% of the amount by which the tax that would have been payable is reduced, under section 285.1 of the Excise Tax Act. A claim to zero-rating that is unsupported or fraudulent may result in: (1) denial of refund and immediate assessment of unpaid HST plus interest; (2) a penalty of up to 40% of the net tax adjustment if knowingly false; (3) criminal prosecution for fraud under the Criminal Code or Excise Tax Act sections 327, 327.1, 328, and 329, with fines up to CAD 100,000 and imprisonment up to five years; (4) revocation of GST/HST registration and suspension of Business Number; and (5) for negligent filing, a 'gross negligence penalty' of up to 50% of the tax understatement (for income tax purposes, similarly under the Income Tax Act section 163.2 for false statements or omissions, up to CAD 24,000 for the penalty for making false statements) and, in cases of tax evasion, additional civil penalties of 50% of the federal and provincial tax evaded. Retrospective clawbacks may include waiver of the four-year reassessment limit for wilful misrepresentation, recovery of interest, and referral for tax evasion prosecution. Export substantiation records must be maintained for six years from the end of the last tax year to which they relate, per section 286 of the Excise Tax Act.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

CAD is fully convertible on both current and capital accounts under a free-floating exchange rate regime. There are no exchange control statutes requiring approval for dividend remittances, management fees, rate royalties, loan repayments, or partnership distributions. For an Ontario Limited Partnership (CA_LP) engaged in surgical suture manufacturing, profit remittance to non-resident partners is unrestricted, subject only to Part XIII withholding tax and annual partnership information returns (T5013). Capital repatriation on wind-up is likewise unrestricted. A potential regulatory checkpoint is the Investment Canada Act review for acquisitions of control of a Canadian business by non-Canadians, but organic start-up investment and day-to-day commercial capital flows below applicable thresholds do not require review, and no convertibility barrier exists for trade or treasury transactions.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender obligation exists in Canada. There is no mandatory timeline for repatriation of export proceeds and no minimum conversion ratio for export receipts to CAD. Exporters may maintain proceeds in original foreign currency, hold them in foreign-currency bank accounts at Canadian chartered banks (via correspondent accounts) or authorized foreign banks, and convert to CAD at their discretion for working capital or settlement. Consistent with the OECD Codes of Liberalisation, Canada applies full current-account freedom; export invoice proceeds from sales of absorbable polyglycolic acid braided sutures may be cleared directly into the exporting entity’s operating account.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (central bank) is the monetary authority for the Canadian dollar (CAD). Foreign exchange administration is exercised through the Minister of Finance under the Currency Act, with prudential oversight of chartered banks by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI). No separate foreign exchange control agency or exchange allocation council exists.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Medium – High as technological clearing layer, but Low/Prohibited as primary deposit-holding or lending institution. In Ontario, non-bank fintech entities cannot take deposits under the Bank Act; they may operate as money services businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC for foreign exchange or remittance. Offshore digital EMIs (e.g., EU/UK e-money institutions) are not licensed to hold Canadian deposit accounts or issue Canadian payment cards, and they are not Schedule-I/Schedule-II banks. Their use is permissible only as technical payment facilitators via approved Canadian MSB partners or by utilising the correspondent banking services of a domestic chartered bank for payout and receivable settlement. For an LP producing surgical consumables, a fully licensed Canadian bank account is mandatory as the settlement anchor; offshore EMIs should be limited to non-critical payment access points for international suppliers or distributors. Legal constraints include Federal Financial Consumer Agency prudential supervision, registration thresholds, anti-money-laundering obligations under PCMLTFA, and restrictions on cross-border inbound investment into Canadian fintech payment service providers.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital injections from non-resident partners or parents must clear via SWIFT MT103 using plain CP (Customer Transfer) or as a corporate funding transfer, indicating payment purpose 'Capital Contribution' or 'FDI'. For CAD settlement, if the remitting bank is not a Canadian member, the correspondent bank settles through the Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) using ISO 20022 pacs.008.000e.03 instructions. The recipient must attach a Credit Advice with purpose code 'C001' (Capital Contribution) or 'COVE' (Cost of Capital) as relevant. The operating bank will verify the CIP/CDD source-of-funds declaration and may freeze a portion of the escrow account until the Ontario LP registration and legal statement are delivered. Compliance requirement: execute a foreign exchange declaration (Form 149?) not required for ordinary capital contributions; a Circular 230 notification is not needed. Keep all underlying transaction correspondence, board resolutions, and trust/partner structure nexus in the bank’s customer file.Permitted outbound clearing rails for purchase of raw polymers, stainless steel needles, packaging, and precision tooling include: (i) Classic SWIFT MT103 for TT wire settlements – ideal for suppliers with account at correspondent banks, immediate finality upon beneficiary credit in CAD or foreign currency; (ii) Commercial Letters of Credit (documentary L/C) under UCP 600, typically irrevocable and confirmed at the supplier bank, enabling quality assurance while a shipment is pre-shipment inspected; (iii) SWIFT MT700/MT760 for standby credits and guarantees for security to vendor group; (iv) Direct settlement through a Canadian bank's foreign currency account if the LP maintains a USD settlement pool (USD Operational Account) to avoid FX on each invoice. For domestic Ontario suppliers, LVTS same-day CAD settlement is preferred. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility Rating: Medium – High as technological clearing layer, but Low/Prohibited as primary deposit-holding or lending institution. In Ontario, non-bank fintech entities cannot take deposits under the Bank Act; they may operate as money services businesses (MSBs) registered with FINTRAC for foreign exchange or remittance. Offshore digital EMIs (e.g., EU/UK e-money institutions) are not licensed to hold Canadian deposit accounts or issue Canadian payment cards, and they are not Schedule-I/Schedule-II banks. Their use is permissible only as technical payment facilitators via approved Canadian MSB partners or by utilising the correspondent banking services of a domestic chartered bank for payout and receivable settlement. For an LP producing surgical consumables, a fully licensed Canadian bank account is mandatory as the settlement anchor; offshore EMIs should be limited to non-critical payment access points for international suppliers or distributors. Legal constraints include Federal Financial Consumer Agency prudential supervision, registration thresholds, anti-money-laundering obligations under PCMLTFA, and restrictions on cross-border inbound investment into Canadian fintech payment service providers.
