Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a fully free-floating, fully convertible currency with no exchange controls on current or capital account transactions. CAD conversions for trade, investment, dividends, interest, repatriation of capital, or intercompany financing are unrestricted and require no central bank approval. Profit remittance from a Quebec corporation to foreign shareholders is freely permitted, subject only to normal Income Tax Act Part XIII non-resident withholding tax (typically 25%, frequently reduced under treaty) and to sanctions/anti-money-laundering screening. There is no profit remittance licensing board, no surrender requirement, and no parallel-rate mechanism.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canadian law imposes no statutory timeline and no mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts. An exporter may hold export proceeds in a foreign currency account in Canada or abroad, convert CAD at flexible market rates, or use the same proceeds directly to settle offshore supplier invoices. Record-keeping obligations remain under the Income Tax Act and Excise Tax Act, and cross-border wire transfers are subject to standard FINTRAC reporting thresholds and beneficial-owner records, but there is no foreign-currency surrender obligation.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as monetary and currency authority; Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) as prudential bank regulator; Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) as AML/ATF and KYC compliance supervisor. Canada has no dedicated foreign exchange control administration.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Moderate. Cross-border digital EMI platforms (e.g., Wise, Payoneer, Airwallex) are lawful in Canada as unregulated-or-lightly-regulated money services businesses only if they are registered with FINTRAC and comply with Canadian AML/KYC rules. They are feasible for low-value operational settlements, international supplier payments, and convenient FX execution, but they are not a substitute for a Schedule I Canadian bank for capital escrow facilities, documentary letters of credit, sanctioned-currency clearing, or large treasury limits. Their use for shareholder capital injections is generally disfavored by Canadian counsel because of enhanced source-of-funds opacity and because bank treasuries will require the ultimate proceeds to pass through a regulated Canadian bank if the funds later support an LC or credit line.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity injections should be routed via SWIFT MT103 or MT202 from the shareholder's regulated bank to the corporate operating/Capital Escrow Account at a Tier 1 Canadian bank. Beneficiary account must carry the legal corporate name and Quebec REQ number. Payment narrative should clearly state 'Shareholder equity contribution' or 'Capital injection for electric cargo bicycle manufacturing operations'. Canada requires no official foreign-exchange purpose code or central bank approval; however, the Canadian receiving bank may request the underlying board resolution, subscription agreement, and source-of-funds records for FINTRAC risk assessment. CAD-denominated wires clear in real time on Payments Canada LVTS; USD wires clear through correspondent bank systems (Fedwire/CHIPS) and post in one to two business days. Cash deposits above CAD 10,000 require large-cash-transaction reporting to FINTRAC. SDN and sanctions screening must confirm no blocked person or high-risk jurisdiction appears in the payment chain.The main outbound clearing rails are SWIFT MT103 direct telegraphic transfers to supplier accounts, and documentary letters of credit for larger OEM component shipments. For direct TT, provide SWIFT BIC, IBAN or correspondent routing, invoice number, and cargo description such as 'electric cargo bike battery packs', 'hub motors', or 'frame components'. For LC-backed settlements, a Canadian commercial bank will issue an irrevocable, at-sight or usance documentary credit (SWIFT MT700) payable in CAD, USD, or EUR, with amendment via MT707, documents forwarded under MT400/MT750 series, and settlement advice via MT910. CAD commercial payments above the LVTS threshold settle intraday through Payments Canada; USD payments clear through U.S. correspondent banks. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Combined federal and Quebec headline rate of 26.5% (federal general rate 15.0% + Quebec general rate 11.5%); for a Canadian-controlled private corporation the combined small-business rate is 12.2% on the first CAD 500,000 of eligible active business income (federal 9.0% + Quebec 3.2%).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act on dividends paid or credited by a Canadian-resident corporation to non-resident shareholders; rate is reduced to 15% for most treaty jurisdictions and to 5% under the Canada-US Treaty where the US corporate shareholder holds at least 10% of voting shares.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

Six months after the fiscal year-end; e.g., a December 31 fiscal year-end must be filed by June 30. Instalments are due quarterly on the 15th day of the following month.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Six months after the fiscal year-end; a December 31 year-end is due June 30 both federally and provincially.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CRA Information Circular IC 87-2R expects contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation for related-party cross-border transactions exceeding: (i) CAD 1,000,000 for purchases/sales of goods; (ii) CAD 250,000 for services or intangibles; (iii) CAD 400,000 for interest-bearing debt. Documentation must be available in writing within three months of a CRA request; Canada has no statutory Master File/Local File deadline, but Country-by-Country Reporting applies to Canadian ultimate parents of MNE groups with consolidated revenue over CAD 1,000,000,000.

Economic substance scope: Canada imposes no discrete Economic Substance Regulation (ESR) akin to UAE/Caribbean regimes, but the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (section 245 of the Income Tax Act) denies tax benefits arising from 'avoidance transactions' lacking genuine business purpose, economic substance, or commercial reality; treaty-shopping claims are rejected where the claimant lacks real nexus, functions, assets, and risk-bearing capacity in the residence state; section 94 and foreign-accrual-property-income rules apply to offshore shell intermediation.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Under section 239 of the Income Tax Act, summary conviction attracts a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and/or imprisonment up to 2 years; on indictment, imprisonment up to 5 years and the same 50%-200% fine scale; gross negligence penalty under subsection 163(2) is 50% of the understated tax, plus interest.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false returns or direct the concealment of income may be prosecuted as parties; CRA may assert director liability for unremitted source deductions and Part XIII amounts under section 227.1, and fraudulent conveyances through shell entities expose directors to civil restitution and equitable-fraud tracing orders.
Late Filing / Non-Filing of T2 and CO-17

Statutory sanction: Federal late-filing penalty of 5% of the unpaid balance plus 1% per month for up to 12 months; Quebec penalty of 5% plus 1% per month on the provincial balance; an additional CAD 25 per day Quebec penalty applies after 2 years of non-filing; gross negligence penalty of 50% may also apply where the failure conceals assessable income.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally assessed for unremitted federal source deductions and Part XIII non-resident tax, and for unremitted Quebec QST/GST under the applicable director-liability provisions; knowingly failing to file while the corporation retains profits in a shell may support piercing the corporate veil.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement and Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: Subsection 247(3) imposes a penalty of 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment when the taxpayer fails to make reasonable efforts; loss of the 'reasonable efforts' protection automatically arises where documentation is not produced within 90 days; reassessment interest compounds on the adjusted amount; CRA may apply gross negligence penalties and, in egregious cases, criminal charges under section 239.

Director personal liability: Directors who direct the preparation of false transfer-pricing studies or approve targeted imbalances exposing the Canadian entity to artificial losses may face director liability for Part XIII and payroll amounts; personal fines up to CAD 25,000 apply for knowingly furnishing false TP information under subsection 163.2(4).
Treaty Shopping / Shell Company without Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: GAAR under subsection 245(2) denies the tax benefit and re-characterizes the transaction at full 25% Part XIII withholding; treaty-benefit denial under the LOB (limitation on benefits) provisions; administrative penalties for misrepresentation of the NR302/TRC and interest on late remittances.

Director personal liability: Directors who certify false treaty declarations may be personally fined and held jointly and severally for the Part XIII withholding remitted under section 215, with the CRA also pursuing unwinding of preferential transfers made to shield assets.
Failure to Remit Payroll and QST/GST Source Deductions

Statutory sanction: CRA penalty of 20% of the unremitted amount applies (severe misconduct rates of 20% to 40% for repeat failures), plus daily interest; Revenu Quebec applies equivalent QST/GST penalties; criminal prosecution for fraudulent withholding is possible.

Director personal liability: Director liability under subsection 227.1 of the Income Tax Act and equivalent Quebec provisions is absolute and does not require fraud: directors become personally liable for the full unremitted source deductions up to the amount of the corporation's shortfall, regardless of corporate form.
Money Laundering / FINTRAC Compliance

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1,000,000 per violation for corporations; Criminal Code section 462.31 money-laundering offense carries imprisonment of up to 10 years; terrorist-financing offenses under section 83.03 carry up to 10 years.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who knowingly facilitate laundering can be convicted as principal parties or as accomplices; travel bans and entry refusals may be imposed by foreign jurisdictions; Canadian mutual-fund-type registration and export controls add personal liability for sanctions violations involving battery components.
