Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); GST/HST is administered federally by CRA under Part IX of the Excise Tax Act, while the Ontario 8% provincial component of the Harmonized Sales Tax (HST) is collected by CRA on Ontario's behalf under a harmonization agreement. Primary portals: CRA 'My Business Account' (canada.ca/my-business-account) and Business Registration Online (BRO) at canada.ca/business-registration; Ontario Ministry of Finance (ontario.ca/page/ministry-finance) and the Ontario Business Registry (ontario.ca/page/business-registry-ontario).

E-government filing portal

A foreign limited partnership registers for a Canadian Business Number (BN) with program-account suffixes RT (GST/HST) and RD (CBSA importer/exporter) as follows: (1) verify the LP is carrying on a commercial activity in Canada (manufacturing plant, warehouse, employee or agent, or sales through Canadian premises in Ontario); (2) apply online via BRO or by submitting Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number', disclosing the full legal partnership name, principal business address, all general and limited partners, each partner's tax identifier, and the first fiscal year-end; (3) obtain the 9-digit BN and RT account; the LP is a GST/HST registrant 'person' for Part IX of the Excise Tax Act, although it files T5013 partnership information (not corporate tax) for income tax; (4) file the Ontario Limited Partnership Declaration in the Ontario Business Registry under the Limited Partnerships Act (Ontario), including the Ontario registered office and agent for service, a condition precedent to the LP's legal operation in Ontario; (5) register for payroll (RP) and import/export (RD) accounts if applicable; (6) each non-resident partner may be subject to Canadian income tax on allocated Canadian-source income, with security arrangements and withholding obligations arising under ITA ss. 220(1.1) and 194.

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible. The Ontario LP is a hybrid person for tax law: it is fiscally transparent for income tax (not a taxpayer under the Income Tax Act; income is allocated to partners and reported via T5013), but it is itself a 'person' required to collect and remit HST and claim input tax credits under Part IX of the Excise Tax Act. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible. The Ontario LP is a hybrid person for tax law: it is fiscally transparent for income tax (not a taxpayer under the Income Tax Act; income is allocated to partners and reported via T5013), but it is itself a 'person' required to collect and remit HST and claim input tax credits under Part IX of the Excise Tax Act. Ontario does not impose partnership-level income tax; Ontario corporate income tax applies only to corporate partners through their share of allocated income.

Available election forms

Canada has no 'check-the-box' regime; elections available include: (1) Income Tax Act s. 97(2) rollover election for transfer of property to the LP at an agreed amount without immediate gain recognition; (2) Excise Tax Act s. 273(1) election for a registrant partnership and its registrant member(s) to treat transfers of property as non-supplies at cost where control and relevance conditions are met; (3) Income Tax Act s. 102(1) joint election (with Minister consent) to compute Canadian-source income of a partnership having non-resident partners as if the partnership were a separate taxpayer; (4) GST/HST fiscal-year and instalment elections under ETA ss. 237 and 243; (5) OECD BEPS hybrid reporting: where a partner is a US LLC, CRA's administrative position may apply the LLC's US federal classification, requiring the LP to test for hybrid mismatch outcomes under ITA ss. 94 and 95.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of taxable supplies (excluding zero-rated exports) made in the course of a commercial activity, measured in a single calendar quarter or across four consecutive calendar quarters (ETA s. 148(1) small-supplier threshold). A non-resident LP carrying on business in Canada must register for GST/HST once the threshold is exceeded; non-residents soliciting orders in Canada or with Canadian reciprocal-supply structures may be required to register despite small-supplier status.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A registrant LP zero-rates exported goods when it makes a supply by way of sale in Canada, ships the goods directly to a destination outside Canada, and the goods are exported as soon as reasonably possible after the supply (ETA Schedule VI, Part V, s. 1); CRA's administrative position treats 60 days as the standard benchmark for physical export. Ancillary services performed in Canada that form part of the exported goods supply (freight, handling, labeling) qualify for zero-rating under Part V ss. 5-7 where the services are rendered to a non-resident or relate to goods destined outside Canada. Because the output is zero-rated at 0%, the LP claims input tax credits on Canadian manufacturing inputs, packaging, machinery and logistics, generating a net HST refund through its regular GST/HST NETFILE return. The exothermic heating element should be classified as dangerous goods under the Transportation of Dangerous Goods Act and CBSA export-reporting rules (B13A/G7) when shipped; this regulatory requirement does not affect zero-rating.

  • CBSA B13A Export Declaration or G7 electronic export report (mandatory for goods with a value over CAD 2,000 and for controlled or dangerous exothermic goods)
  • Bill of Lading / Waybill / Cargo Manifest evidencing outbound movement to a foreign destination
  • Commercial Invoice and Packing List with Incoterms, purchase order reference, and HS classification for self-heating food preparation kits
  • SWIFT MT103 / TT payment credit advice, letter of credit, or bank settlement record evidencing foreign-currency inward remittance from the buyer
  • Carrier certificate, terminal exit gate receipt, customs broker export clearance notice, and warehouse movement records proving uninterrupted export

Canada has no real-time fiscal clearance, no digital stamping, and no mandatory e-invoicing regime. Taxable suppliers must issue invoices satisfying ETA ss. 224 and 225 content requirements (supplier identity and BN, recipient identity, date, total consideration, HST rate and amount); CRA does not pre-approve or clear invoices. GST/HST returns are filed electronically (GST/HST NETFILE/TELEFILE), and CBSA export declarations are submitted electronically via G7 EDI/API for the logistics chain. CRA has adopted a pilot PEPPOL infrastructure for interoperable e-invoicing, but it remains optional for business-to-business transactions.

On CRA audit, the exporter must show physical export within a reasonable period (normally 60 days); goods warehoused in Canada or exported only after domestic sale are taxable at 13% HST. Where zero-rating is denied, CRA may: reassess the supply at 13% HST and restrict offsetting ITCs; impose the ETA s. 285 false-statement or evasion penalty (greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the tax sought to be evaded); apply arrears interest compounded daily under ETA s. 280; and assess failure-to-file penalties for repeated delinquency. Criminal liability under ETA s. 327 (evasion or deliberate false statement) carries fines up to CAD 200,000, imprisonment up to 5 years, and a court-imposed fine of up to double the tax evaded. The normal four-year reassessment period is extended indefinitely for misrepresentation attributable to negligence or fraud. Canada does not statutorily fix an FX repatriation deadline; the 180-day figure in this dossier is retained as the pipeline's prudent documentary and liquidity benchmark rather than a Canadian statutory limit.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current-account and capital-account convertibility for the Canadian dollar. There is no general exchange-control law that restricts capital inflows, capital outflows, dividend distributions, or profit repatriation, and no central-bank authorization is required for cross-border transfers or for the purchase and sale of foreign currency. Profit and capital remittances from an Ontario Limited Partnership to non-resident partners are nevertheless subject to three practical hurdles: (i) the statutory solvency test for distributions under the Limited Partnerships Act (Ontario), under which the partnership must be able to pay its debts as they become due; (ii) Canadian withholding tax under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act (generally 15% for distributions to non-resident partners, reduced by an applicable income tax treaty); and (iii) FINTRAC large funds transfer reporting obligations for transfers of C$10,000 or more. No prior exchange-control filing or monetary approval is required for profit remittance.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no surrender, repatriation, or mandatory conversion requirement on export proceeds. Exporters, manufacturers, and limited partnerships may retain export receipts indefinitely in any currency, may hold funds in deposits within or outside Canada, and may convert receipts at negotiated commercial rates without central-bank permission. There is therefore no statutory timeline and no mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts. The only applicable compliance obligations are: (i) FINTRAC record-keeping and large funds transfer reporting for transfers of C$10,000 or more; (ii) documentary evidence supporting the underlying export transaction, including invoices, bills of lading, and customs declarations; and (iii) normal accrual and accounting treatment of foreign-exchange gains and losses for income tax purposes under the Income Tax Act and applicable Canadian generally accepted accounting principles.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) is the central monetary and financial-oversight authority; foreign-exchange administration and exchange-control policy are jointly carried out by the Bank of Canada, the Department of Finance Canada, and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) under the Currency Act, the Currency and Exchange Act, and the Financial Administration Act. Canada does not maintain a dedicated foreign-exchange administration comparable to SAFE; day-to-day compliance and enforcement functions are distributed among OSFI, FINTRAC, and the Payments, Clearing and Settlement Supervision function of the Bank of Canada under the Canadian Payments Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: MEDIUM, subject to substantial legal constraints. Offshore fintech or digital EMI providers cannot lawfully hold partner deposits or provide deposit-taking services in Canada unless they obtain an authorization as a Schedule I or Schedule II bank under the Bank Act or operate through a Canadian branch. Unlicensed EMIs may act only as a payment facilitation layer, must be registered as a Money Service Business (MSB) with FINTRAC, and must route all funds through a Canadian chartered bank clearing account. For an Ontario LP with cross-border trade needs, a foreign EMI is viable only as a secondary collection rail for small- to mid-ticket transactions; primary treasury clearing should be conducted through a Schedule I Canadian bank to preserve legal certainty, SWIFT routing, and correspondent access.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital contributions should be routed by SWIFT MT103 (or MT202 for interbank funding) from the contributing partner's bank directly to the Ontario LP's account at a Canadian Schedule I bank, denominated in CAD or USD. The beneficiary account must be identified by the Canadian institution number, branch-transit number, and account number (Canada follows a 6-digit routing scheme rather than IBAN). The payment reference must state, in the narrative field, 'capital contribution to Ontario Limited Partnership CA_LP; underlying reason: partnership capital injection for food-manufacturing operations under ISIC 1079'. The sending bank must verify that the contribution matches the partnership agreement and that no artificial splitting of transfers is made to evade the C$10,000 FINTRAC large-funds-transfer reporting threshold. The Canadian receiving bank will require a source-of-funds dossier identical to the KYC defense pack and will place the funds into the capital escrow account until the onboarding questionnaire is completed; after validation, the funds are released to the operational account for trade purposes.Outbound trade settlements should be executed through SWIFT MT103 international wire transfers via the Canadian bank's correspondent network, cleared for CAD using Lynx (Canada's real-time gross settlement high-value system) or for USD using a New York correspondent through CHIPS or Fedwire. For documentary risk mitigation, commercial letters of credit (documentary credits) should be issued by the Ontario LP's issuing Canadian bank, with a foreign advising bank nominated at the buyer's location, particularly for export sales to buyer markets in Asia and North America. Domestic CAD supplier payments may be settled via Lynx for time-critical disbursements or via the Automated Payment Exchange / Real-Time Rail for lower-value electronic payments. All outbound transactions must be matched to export invoices, maintain complete supporting transaction records, and comply with FINTRAC reporting rules when the transfer is C$10,000 or more. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined general corporate rate for an Ontario-resident corporation (15% federal Part I + 11.5% Ontario general corporate provincial rate). The Limited Partnership itself is fiscally transparent; tax is borne by each partner on allocated income.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Income Tax Act s.212(2), applicable when a Canadian resident corporation pays a dividend to a non-resident shareholder. A limited partnership is not a corporation, so ordinary profit distributions to non-resident limited partners are not treated as dividends; instead, the non-resident partner's allocated share of Canadian active business income is subject to Part I tax and, if paid out as an actual remittance, may attract domestic withholding or treaty-affected liability depending on the facts.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 aggregate value of reportable transactions with each non-resident related party in a taxation year triggers the requirement to file Form T106 and to prepare and retain contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation. For country-by-country reporting, the threshold is group consolidated annual revenue of at least C$1.0 billion (approximately €750 million). Canadian law does not use the formal 'Local File/Master File' labels, but the CRA expects substantially equivalent supporting records.

Economic substance scope: Canada has not enacted Caribbean-style Economic Substance Regulations. However, the Income Tax Act contains a broad General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) that may deny tax benefits where ordinary business transactions are arranged lacking economic substance and misuse or abuse the tax provisions. Treaty benefits also require the recipient to be the beneficial owner and, under the Principal Purpose Test in Canadian treaty practice, the LP must demonstrate a genuine Ontario business presence, tangible assets, actual employees and a real decision-making function. A purely passive Ontario limited partnership used as a conduit for treaty-shopping will be challenged by CRA.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraudulent Concealment

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s.239(1): upon summary conviction, fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded plus imprisonment up to 2 years; upon indictment, imprisonment up to 5 years. CRA may also impose a 50% gross-negligence penalty under s.163(2) plus interest. Criminal Code s.380 may apply for fraud over C$5,000 with imprisonment up to 14 years.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, and controlling limited partners who authorized or participated in false filings are jointly and severally liable under ITA s.227.1 for withheld source deductions and GST/HST amounts. Criminal Code liability attaches personally; CRA may obtain civil judgments, freeze bank accounts, impose travel bans, and pursue extradition through mutual legal assistance treaties.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: GAAR under ITA s.245(2) may recharacterize the entire structure and deny treaty and domestic tax benefits; under s.245(4) a penalty of 50% of the tax benefit may be added, with interest. The CRA may also deny treaty relief under the beneficial ownership doctrine and the OECD Principal Purpose Test. Where a sham is proven, the CRA may reassess the corporate general partner directly.

Director personal liability: The individuals acting as general partner or directing the operation may be personally assessed for tax benefits denied to the partnership if funds were distributed without satisfying partnership obligations. Where the arrangement is found to be a fraudulent scheme, criminal liability may extend to controlling partners, and courts may lift the Ontario LP veil to render them personally liable for the partnership's tax debts.
Failure to File / Withholding Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Late-filing penalties as described above; T106 failure attracts C$100 per day per form up to C$500 for each late or failed form, plus additional penalties for gross negligence. Withholding failures make the payer liable for the full unremitted amount plus daily compound arrears interest; s.215(1) and s.227(8.6) may impose additional penalties of up to 10% of the unpaid amounts.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate general partner are jointly and severally liable for unremitted Canadian withholding tax and interest if the corporation fails to collect or remit, and if the director failed to exercise reasonable care. Partnership losses or insolvency does not shield directors, and CRA may pursue directors without first realizing on partnership assets.
AML / Money Laundering

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and Criminal Code s.462.31, laundering proceeds of crime carries imprisonment up to 10 years; FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties for unregistered MSB activity can reach C$100,000 for an individual and C$500,000 for a corporation/business entity. Failure to report suspicious transactions to FINTRAC may also lead to prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who knowingly direct funds through the partnership without AML controls can be charged as parties to the laundering offence. They are also exposed to FINTRAC penalties, asset seizure, travel and banking restrictions, and in serious cases convictions leading to imprisonment and exclusion from serving as directors in Canada.
