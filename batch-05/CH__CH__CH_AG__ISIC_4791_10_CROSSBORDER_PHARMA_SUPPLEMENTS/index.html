Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Swiss Federal Tax Administration (Eidgenössische Steuerverwaltung ESTV / Administration fédérale des contributions AFC) and its SuisTax portal; official e-services at https://www.estv.admin.ch.

E-government filing portal

A non-resident enterprise must register for Swiss VAT under Art. 10 para. 2 lit. b VAT Act if its Swiss-sourced turnover exceeds CHF 100,000 per year. Registration is made through the SuisTax online portal (or Form 101) with the FTA, providing evidence of the applicant's foreign corporate status, the nature of Swiss supplies, turnover computations, and, if the applicant has no permanent establishment in Switzerland, the appointment of a resident fiscal representative. The issued Swiss VAT number is the UID (Unternehmens-Identifikationsnummer) in the format CHE-xxx.xxx.xxx MWST.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

None materially comparable to US check-the-box. Swiss corporate law imposes an entity-level tax on an Aktiengesellschaft; no pass-through election is available for an AG. Hybrid mismatch restrictions under Swiss tax law (2019 OECD BEPS-aligned amendments) operate as disallowance rules, not elections.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

8.1%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

Resident businesses: CHF 250,000 annual worldwide turnover (excluding exempt real-estate supplies). Non-resident businesses with no residence or place of business in Switzerland: CHF 100,000 annual taxable turnover derived from Swiss supplies (Art. 10 VAT Act).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of goods qualify for 0% VAT when removed from Switzerland to a foreign destination (export) or transferred as deemed export; services qualify under Art. 23 VAT Act if the beneficiary is resident abroad, provided the place of supply is in Switzerland. The supplier must hold reliable documentary evidence that export has occurred (e.g., customs export record).

  • Customs Export Declaration/Ausfuhrzollanmeldung (via Swiss Customs e-dec system)
  • Transport documents evidencing international carriage (Bill of Lading, Air Waybill, CMR)
  • Commercial invoice and, where applicable, packing list
  • Proof of export date and destination; for services, foreign beneficiary and economic activity evidence
  • Inward remittance certificate or bank transfer evidence (where used for commercial proof; not a statutory VAT requirement)

No real-time fiscal clearance or mandatory continuous controls (CTC). E-invoicing is voluntary; Swiss QR-bill and e-bill standards are accepted. The law requires invoices to state the supplier's UID MWST number and all Swiss VAT elements; no fiscal API, no digital stamping, and no private-sector e-invoicing mandate exists. Export invoices must be supported by customs export declarations.

Swiss VAT export zero-rating is reviewed ex post by the FTA through documentary and customs audits. If export status is not substantiated, the VAT becomes payable at 8.1% together with delinquency interest; a wrongful claim may be treated as tax evasion (Art. 56 VAT Act) and carry fines up to three times the tax evaded and potential criminal sanctions. Reassessments for VAT are generally not time-barred where the taxpayer acted with intent.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Switzerland operates a fully liberalised and convertible currency regime for both the current and capital accounts. The Swiss franc (CHF) is not subject to exchange controls, minimum holding periods, quantitative restrictions, or central bank licensing for cross-border fund movement. Profit repatriation, dividend distributions, shareholder loan servicing, and intra-group treasury flows are legally unrestricted; no SNB approval or declaration of intent is required for cross-border payments. The only frictions are (i) the Swiss 35% withholding tax (Verrechnungssteuer) on dividend distributions, which may be reduced or eliminated under Switzerland's network of double tax treaties or refunded to qualifying Swiss-resident corporate shareholders; and (ii) Switzerland's implementation of international sanctions, which requires that all cross-border payments and counterparties be screened against SECO (State Secretariat for Economic Affairs), FINMA, OFAC, EU, and UN sanction lists. For e-commerce supply chains sourcing dietary supplements from Asia, the practical constraint is not convertibility but compliance with Swiss food-labelling law, the Therapeutic Products Act, and the counterparty/KYC standards imposed by Swiss banks on high-risk merchandise segments.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There are no statutory export-proceeds surrender, earmarking, or mandatory conversion obligations in Switzerland. Swiss exporters and cross-border e-commerce operators are free to retain 100% of their export and sales receipts in any currency (USD, EUR, CNY, etc.) in Swiss or foreign bank accounts, and may convert those proceeds on the market at any time without SNB permission. The only compulsory reporting is statistical: under the SNB Ordinance on the gathering of statistical data, direct exporters and treasury entities must report aggregate balance-of-payments statistics (customs data for goods, and financial flows over CHF 10 million) at fixed deadlines. There is no minimum conversion ratio, no forced sale into CHF, and no requirement to use a designated state bank.

Central bank supervision: Swiss National Bank (SNB; German: Schweizerische Nationalbank; English: Swiss National Bank, headquartered in Bern and Zurich)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Viability: Low-to-Medium as principal banking rail, but Medium as a secondary receivables-acquirer under FINMA supervision. Full EU/EEA Payment Institution (PSD2) and E-Money Institution passports do NOT automatically extend to Switzerland, which is not an EEA member. For an EU/UK-based EMI to offer professional services to the Swiss AG, it must either rely on a Swiss-regulated discovery-passport arrangement (limited for payment services) or obtain a FinTech Licence under Art. 1b of the Swiss Banking Act as a FINMA-supervised operator of a deposits-holding payment system. The Swiss FinTech licence regime permits interest-free deposits up to CHF 100 million but does not itself authorise lending; additionally, the company is subject to the Swiss Anti-Money Laundering Act (GwG/AMLA) and must be affiliated with a recognised AML self-regulatory body (e.g., VQF or AMLS). For the specific nutraceuticals e-commerce profile, most Swiss Tier-1 banks refuse acquiring for dietary-supplement merchants classified as high risk; therefore the practical blueprint is to onboard a regulated Tier-1 bank for treasury, and to pair it with either (i) a specialised FINMA-licensed FinTech/PSP (operating under an Art. 1b license or a full banking license) for card acquiring, or (ii) a PCI-DSS Level-1 payment orchestration platform with licensed EU acquiring backbone operating per Article 3(b) of the Swiss Payment Services Act (PSA) through an EU agent arrangement. The strictly offshore EMI (e.g., Malta, Lithuania, UK, or US) is legally viable only as a technical gateway if it holds a valid FINMA passport or the Swiss entity completes a fiduciary agency agreement with a Swiss licensed bank; otherwise using an offshore EMI for core CHF liquidity or settlement of trade payables should be treated as a critical regulatory blocker

The strictly offshore EMI (e.g., Malta, Lithuania, UK, or US) is legally viable only as a technical gateway if it holds a valid FINMA passport or the Swiss entity completes a fiduciary agency agreement with a Swiss licensed bank; otherwise using an offshore EMI for core CHF liquidity or settlement of trade payables should be treated as a critical regulatory blocker

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

8.5% federal direct tax on net profit after tax (equivalent to 7.83% on pre-tax profit); combined federal plus cantonal plus communal effective rates range from 11.9% (Zug) to 21.6% (Geneva) depending on the canton of domicile; the composite jurisdiction applies a unitary national system with mandatory federal tax uniformity.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

35% flat Swiss federal withholding tax (Verrechnungssteuer) on gross dividends paid by the Swiss AG, levied under Articles 1 and 13 of the Federal Withholding Tax Act (VStG), irrespective of the shareholder's residence or nationality.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Swiss law prescribes no fixed statutory monetary threshold for the Local File or Master File; the Federal Tax Administration and cantonal tax authorities expect full transfer pricing documentation where intragroup transactions with related parties exceed CHF 5 million per transaction category in a given year or where annual turnover exceeds CHF 50 million; Country-by-Country (CbC) reporting is mandatory for multinational groups with consolidated revenue of at least CHF 900 million under Article 129b DBG; the tax authority retains discretion to issue a documentation order in any audit irrespective of entity size.

Economic substance scope: Switzerland has no Economic Substance Regulations statute of the UAE/Gulf type; however, economic substance is enforced through the place-of-effective-management doctrine (Article 50 DBG), the general anti-abuse rule (Article 21 DBG), the OECD Principal Purpose Test (PPT) implemented via the Multilateral Instrument, and the Federal Withholding Tax Act's anti-avoidance practice for holding companies. To claim treaty benefits, participation relief, or group financing privileges, a Swiss AG must demonstrate real local substance: a physical office, genuine board decision-making in Switzerland documented by minutes, an operational e-commerce platform managed from Swiss premises, adequate payroll and staff, and audited financial statements. A tax-resident shell with solely non-resident beneficial owners, no employees, and no business activity is presumptively abusive and may be disregarded for tax purposes, with profits reattributed to the foreign beneficial owners and treaty access denied.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion under Federal Direct Tax Law (Steuerhinterziehung)

Statutory sanction: Article 175 DBG: penalty of 1x to 3x the evaded tax plus default interest at 4.5% p.a.; qualified tax fraud under Article 186 DBG (use of forged or falsified documents) carries imprisonment up to 3 years or a monetary penalty, with an extended 15-year statute of limitation; cantonal law mirrors federal penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors who prepare, sign, or cause false declarations are joint and severally liable for the evaded tax amounts, penalties, and interest; criminal conviction triggers personal fines, economic disqualification, travel bans, and extradition risk; if combined with money laundering, Swiss Criminal Code Articles 305bis and 305ter impose imprisonment up to 5 years and asset freezing.
Withholding Tax (Verrechnungssteuer) Default

Statutory sanction: Article 16 VStG: the AG's board is personally liable for the amount of tax not withheld; Article 65 VStG: fine up to CHF 30,000 for procedural violations; default interest at 5% p.a.; the Federal Tax Administration may seize bank accounts and initiate administrative debt collection.

Director personal liability: Directors and the fiscal representative are jointly and severally liable for the unremitted 35% amount as third-party debtors under Article 12 paragraph 5 VStG; wilful non-compliance is a penal offence leading to fines, director disqualification, and potential criminal extradition in cross-border enforcement proceedings.
Shell Company and Abusive Tax Structuring

Statutory sanction: Article 21 DBG (Steuerumgehung) permits the tax authority to disregard the corporate vehicle; profits are reattributed to the foreign beneficial owners; treaty relief is denied under the OECD Principal Purpose Test; back taxes and interest are assessed for the full statutory period (10 years for recklessness, 15 years for fraud); criminal referral for qualified tax evasion is possible.

Director personal liability: Nominee directors who knowingly lend their titles without exercising genuine control are personally liable for restitution of the tax advantages, including the 35% withholding tax; they may be prosecuted for aiding and abetting evasion, subjected to Schengen-wide travel bans, and disqualified from serving as directors in any Swiss company.
Transfer Pricing Adjustment and Constructive Dividend

Statutory sanction: Recharacterisation of the excess payment as a hidden profit distribution (verdeckte Gewinnausschüttung): the adjustment is added to taxable profit, subject to the 35% Swiss withholding tax if the shareholder is non-resident, plus a penalty of 1x to 3x of the underpaid tax under Article 175 DBG; failure to produce documentation triggers a one-third assessment surcharge under Article 130 DBG.

Director personal liability: Directors who signed the non-arm's-length contracts are jointly liable for the assessed withholding tax under Article 16 VStG, interest, and penalties; arrangements that prejudice creditor rights or the tax authority expose directors to criminal liability for fraudulent bankruptcy or asset stripping under Swiss Criminal Code Articles 163 and 164, carrying imprisonment up to 5 years.
AML and High-Risk E-Commerce Violations

Statutory sanction: Violations of the Swiss Anti-Money Laundering Act (GwG) and Swiss Criminal Code Article 305bis (money laundering) are punishable by imprisonment up to 5 years or a fine; participation in a criminal organisation raises the minimum term; the Therapeutic Products Act (HMG Article 86) imposes fines up to CHF 50,000 with product seizure; payment processors may terminate high-risk merchant accounts, and financial intermediaries must file suspicious transaction reports.

Director personal liability: Directors with knowledge or suspicion of laundering are criminally liable in personam and subject to asset confiscation, travel bans, and extradition; the corporate veil is pierced for restitution of illicit proceeds under Swiss Criminal Code Articles 70 to 73; the company's bank accounts are frozen and the directors may be banned from managing any Swiss business for up to 10 years.
