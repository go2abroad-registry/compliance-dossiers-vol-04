Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) - Business Registration Online portal: https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses.html. Ontario Ministry of Finance coordinates the Ontario HST component, which is administered by the CRA.

E-government filing portal

Non-resident groups incorporate a Business Corporation (federal CBCA or Ontario OBCA), then register online via CRA Business Registration Online to obtain the 9-digit Business Number (BN). The GST/HST (RT) account must be opened when annual worldwide taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000, or immediately when the corporation carries on business in Canada and makes taxable supplies; the Import/Export (RM) customs account is separately opened for cross-border apparel trade. CRA issues a BN confirmation notice and requires a Canadian service address or representative for legal service.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not provide US-style check-the-box elections; a Business Corporation is taxed at the entity level under the Income Tax Act and the Excise Tax Act. Available elections relevant to an apparel wholesaler include the GST/HST related-party election under ETA s. 156, the GST/HST Quick Method election for qualifying registrants, the ITA s. 216 election for non-residents reporting Canadian net rental income, and the Canadian hybrid-mismatch provisions neutralizing cross-border deduction/non-inclusion outcomes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 - annual worldwide taxable supplies (excluding financial services) above which GST/HST registration is mandatory for a person carrying on business in Canada; non-resident vendors with export-only zero-rated supplies may register voluntarily to claim input tax credits.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

The supply of finished garments is zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act when made by a GST/HST registrant, the goods are exported from Canada as or as a result of the supply, legal title and risk of loss pass to a non-resident purchaser, and the registrant retains prescribed evidence of export. The goods must leave Canada in substantially the same state within a reasonable time; if the goods remain in Canada or are consumed or used in Canada, the supply is taxable at 13% HST.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA B13A or CAED electronic export declaration)
  • Bill of Lading (ocean) or Airway Bill (air) showing non-resident destination
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / inbound foreign-currency payment and FX conversion records
  • Commercial invoice and packing list identifying the non-resident purchaser
  • Carrier or customs broker outward confirmation (ACI eManifest / cargo-control records)
  • Purchase order or contract and Incoterms evidencing transfer of title and risk offshore

No real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control, or digital stamping regime exists in Canada. Registrants with annual taxable supplies exceeding CAD 1,500,000 must file GST/HST returns and prescribed information electronically via CRA NETFILE/EDI/My Business Account. CRA participates in a voluntary Pan-Canadian Trust Framework for e-invoicing aligned to the Peppol network. Exporters must submit export declarations electronically through the CBSA Canadian Export Reporting System (CAED).

Where CRA determines that goods were not actually exported or that the prescribed export evidence is missing or false, zero-rating is denied, the supply is reassessed as taxable at 13% HST, and the exporter must remit the deficient tax plus interest. False statements attract a penalty of 25% of the net tax under the ETA, with increased fines and imprisonment of up to five years available for wilful evasion. CRA may also suspend registrations, apply gross-negligence penalties, and reassess beyond the normal four-year limitation period where misrepresentation is established. Records supporting the export must be retained for six years.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible under a free-floating exchange-rate regime. Canada maintains no current or capital account exchange controls; capital inflows, outflows, and profit remittances are not subject to exchange-control licensing. Private-sector capital transactions, including repatriation of dividends, branch profits, and loan repayments, may proceed freely, but outbound dividends and certain profit remittances attract Part XIII non-resident withholding tax at the statutory rate of 25%, which may be reduced under an applicable tax treaty (e.g., Canada-U.S. treaty reduces to 5% on portfolio dividends and 15% for treaty-rate cases; U.K., Germany, other treaties vary). For an off-price apparel wholesale business, the only practical convertibility hurdles are (i) documentary KYC and source-of-funds verification mandated by financial institutions under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, and (ii) reporting obligations for cross-border transactions of C$10,000 or more, which do not constitute exchange-control barriers but require robust compliance records.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender or mandatory conversion requirements on export proceeds. Exporters are free to hold, invest, or convert foreign currency receipts. For settlements involving physical cash or monetary instruments crossing the border, or for wire transfers of C$10,000 or more, reporting to FINTRAC is required as a terrorist-financing and money-laundering safeguard. There is no mandatory timeline for domestic repatriation, no minimum conversion ratio into CAD, and no export-licensing prerequisite for finished garments off-price wholesale, other than normal customs documentation and trade-policy compliance (e.g., anti-dumping or countervailing duties on certain textile product classifications, though garments covered under ISIC 4641/NAICS 424320 remain generally exempt from restrictive trade measures). Export receipts may be retained in foreign currency in authorized foreign-currency accounts at Canadian Schedule I or II banks, provided the account holder maintains full AML/KYC records and reports transactions over C$10,000.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) with policy administration for AML and cross-border reporting delegated through the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Medium-Low for primary treasury clearing; acceptable as an auxiliary wire or receivable channel. Legal constraints: Fintech EMI platforms domiciled outside Canada (e.g., UK, EU, Singapore-licensed EMI/payment institutions) are not authorized to hold Canadian bank accounts under the Bank Act and cannot directly access Canadian clearing systems (ACSS, Lynx) without partnering with a Schedule I Canadian bank. They may operate as money-service businesses (MSBs) in Canada only if registered with FINTRAC, which subjects them to the full AML/KYC compliance regime. For an off-price garments wholesale entity, relying solely on an offshore EMI is inadvisable because: (i) client funds may be held outside Canada, creating balance-sheet and operational risk; (ii) Canadian counterparties often reject non-bank settlement references and lack CDI clearing codes; (iii) incoming wire from an EMI can trigger enhanced due diligence or slow-funds holds at domestic receiving banks; and (iv) the entity loses the ability to issue escrow or documentary LC instruments from a prudentially regulated bank. Best practice is to maintain a domestic Schedule I bank account as the primary clearing anchor, using an EMI only for micro-collection from remote ACH markets where the EMI holds a local rails licence.

Core KYC Rejection Triggers & Defense Dossier

Root cause: Beneficial ownership structure involving non-resident shareholders located in a jurisdiction considered high-risk or non-cooperative by FATF

Defense evidence: Prepare and submit a corporate ownership diagram; certified constitutional documents (Articles of Incorporation, By-laws, Register of Directors); a trust deed or share-transfer registry if any nominee holder is involved; personal identification of each beneficial owner (passport or government-issued ID) with a bank officer verifying a notarized copy; proof of source of funds from the ultimate shareholder (bank statements, tax returns, liquidation payoff report showing asset conversion); and a written explanation of the commercial purpose of the Canadian entity with customer risk assessment documentation.
Trade classification as 'used clothing/off-price garments' or 'liquidation apparel' raising counterfeit or OEM-abuse red flags

Root cause: Trade classification as 'used clothing/off-price garments' or 'liquidation apparel' raising counterfeit or OEM-abuse red flags

Defense evidence: Provide trademark licensing letters or brand-authorization certificates from the original apparel brand owners; invoices or distribution agreements proving the goods are genuine overstock liquidation lots; original supplier invoices; bills of lading; import/export customs entries with HS classification; photographs of the merchandise and stockflow documentation; a copy of the entity's anti-counterfeiting policy and product provenance audit trail; and evidence of unrealistic pricing margins that are consistent with liquidation channel deals rather than forged goods.
High volumes of cross-border wire transfers to/from a newly incorporated entity that has no domestic revenue history or physical operating presence in Canada

Root cause: High volumes of cross-border wire transfers to/from a newly incorporated entity that has no domestic revenue history or physical operating presence in Canada

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Mandatory contemporaneous documentation under s. 247(4) and IC89-16R applies when total gross revenue for the year or the immediately preceding year exceeds CAD 30,000,000 and the aggregate fair market value of covered transactions / notional amounts with non-resident related persons exceeds CAD 5,000,000 in the year; documentation must be completed and retained by the statutory filing due date (six months after year-end); where thresholds are not met, ordinary record retention (six years) still applies

Economic substance scope: Canada does not impose a standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime akin to UAE/Oman; however, substance is dispositive for (i) corporate residence (central management and control test), (ii) the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s. 245) applying an avoidance-transaction test, (iii) treaty entitlement via Limitation-on-Benefits/PPT clauses (e.g., Canada-US treaty Art. XXIX A), and (iv) CRA's power to recharacterize transactions; an off-price garment liquidation corporation must therefore demonstrate Ontario board minutes, warehouse and sales operations, employees, decision-making, and bank accounts in Canada, and avoid nominee directorship arrangements

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False or Deceptive Filings

Statutory sanction: Indictable offence under s. 239(1) Income Tax Act: fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax evaded plus imprisonment up to five years; civil gross negligence penalty under s. 163(2) of 50% of the misreported tax; denial of losses plus daily compound interest (approx. 8% annually for 2025)

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and tax preparers who authorize or acquiesce are subject to personal criminal prosecution; upon conviction a foreign national may receive a s. 36(1) IRPA inadmissibility order (travel ban from Canada); CRA may also assess directors jointly and severally under s. 227.1 for unremitted withholdings and under corporate-veil-piercing where the corporation is found to be a mere facade
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust the arm's-length consideration under s. 247(2); a 10% adjustment penalty applies under s. 247(3) where the net adjustment exceeds the greater of the stated statutory minimum; interest runs from the normal balance-due date

Director personal liability: Penalty generally assessed on the corporation only; however, directors who sign false transfer-pricing declarations or knowingly participate in a sham can be prosecuted under s. 239 and s. 246.1 conspiracy charges; travel inadmissibility follows any money-laundering/gross fraud conviction
Part XIII Withholding Failures on Profit Repatriation

Statutory sanction: Unremitted Part XIII tax is immediately collectable with daily compound interest; an additional 10% penalty on unreported amounts under s. 162(7) applies as a cumulative failure penalty; failure to withhold when required also triggers the payer's liability for the full grossed-up amount

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for all unremitted Part XIII withholdings under s. 227.1, regardless of whether the corporation is financially distressed; CRA may immediately issue a Director's Liability assessment without first exhausting corporate recovery; travel bans and personal bankruptcy exposure follow
Shell / Sham / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA and Ontario courts apply the sham doctrine and s. 245 GAAR to re-deduct and reattribute income; all treaty benefits denied under Limitation-on-Benefits where substance fails; fines, tax reassessments, and reversal of losses follow

Director personal liability: Nominee directors who lend their names to a shell can be personally assessed for unremitted taxes of the corporation under s. 227.1 and may face criminal liability for facilitation of tax evasion; bankruptcy and immigration inadmissibility consequences apply to principals
GST/HST (Ontario HST 13%) Evasion and Input Tax Credit Manipulation

Statutory sanction: Under the Excise Tax Act: 5% defendant's penalty plus 0.25% per month interest on unremitted net tax; civil penalties up to 50% for false ITC claims; criminal fines of up to CAD 50,000 and six months imprisonment on summary conviction (s. 328) or 2-5 years on indictment; CRA may deny refunds and impose an audit-wide re-estimation

Director personal liability: Section 323 of the Excise Tax Act imposes strict joint and several liability on directors for all unremitted GST/HST amounts; CRA commonly issues director assessments simultaneously with corporate assessments and will pursue personal residences, bank accounts, and travel bans where cross-border flight risk exists
