Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation (no pass-through eligibility). A Canadian Business Corporation (Inc./Corp.) is a separate taxpayer under Income Tax Act (ITA) s.14; shareholders are not taxed on corporate income until dividend distribution. Territorial scope: Canadian-resident corporations are taxed on worldwide income, mitigated by foreign tax credits (ITA s.126) for the overseas hotpot branches; no territorial exemption regime. Tax-exempt zone: none applicable in Ontario; foreign active business income of a controlled foreign affiliate may be deferred under the FAPI regime. Foreign branch losses and profits are included in Canadian taxable income with foreign tax credit relief.

Available election forms

Canada has no US-style check-the-box classification election. Available statutory elections: (1) ETA s.240 - GST/HST group registration election for closely related Canadian corporations to file as a single registrant; (2) ETA s.156 - election to treat taxable supplies to a closely related corporation as made for nil consideration; (3) ITA s.216 - non-resident landlord election to compute net-basis Canadian tax on rental income; (4) ITA s.217 - non-resident employee election for Canadian-source employment income; (5) ITA s.219 - branch tax election for non-resident insurance/corporations; (6) ITA Regulations s.5907 - elections relevant to hybrid arrangements and foreign affiliate surplus/ FAPI calculations; (7) treaty-based return election (ITA s.115) allowing a non-resident corporation to reduce Part XIII withholding on Canadian-source amounts. An Ontario-resident corporation cannot elect pass-through status; entity-level taxation is mandatory.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies under ETA Schedule VI: (i) exported tangible personal property (TTP) - hotpot condiments/sauces/base products sold by the Canadian corporation to a non-resident customer and shipped or delivered by the supplier or its agent to a destination outside Canada are zero-rated under Schedule VI Part V, s.22, provided the supplier effects the export and retains evidence; (ii) services physically performed outside Canada (overseas direct-operated restaurant dining) fall outside Canadian GST/HST place-of-supply rules (ETA s.142.1) - no Canadian tax is charged on offshore branch sales; (iii) food and beverages for human consumption sold for domestic retail are zero-rated under Schedule VI Part III only when not supplied as a restaurant/meal service; hotpot dining in Canada is taxable at 13% HST; (iv) intangible property (trademark royalties and franchise fees charged to the overseas subsidiary) is zero-rated under Schedule VI Part V, s.10 where supplied to a non-resident not registered for GST/HST and the property is for use exclusively outside Canada. Evidence must conform to CRA GST/HST Memorandum 17-1 (valid commercial proof of export), obtained no later than the return filing date.

  • CBSA Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A / Canadian Export Reporting System (CAED) receipt or e-export record)
  • Commercial invoice reflecting the sale, Incoterms, and zero-rated status
  • Bill of Lading or airway waybill issued by the carrier showing final destination outside Canada
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / bank SWIFT or wire-transfer receipt evidencing consideration received from the non-resident
  • Packing list with weights, quantities, and product descriptions
  • CFIA export permit or food-safety certificate for exported condiment/food products (Canadian Food Inspection Agency)

  • GST/HST registration number of the foreign importer and self-issued zero-rating declaration on the invoice

No real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or API-based tax clearance mandate exists in Canada. Suppliers must issue invoices containing prescribed information (ETA s.223 and Schedule V): supplier name, Business Number, date, description, consideration, and GST/HST amount or rate; invoices may be issued electronically. The Pan-Canadian B2B e-invoicing standard (PEPPOL) is the CRA-recommended interoperability framework and is mandatory for federal government suppliers; Ontario has no standalone e-invoicing clearance. CRA may inspect electronic records during audit; records must be retained for six years (ETA s.286).

CRA may reassess zero-rated export sales at 13% HST where proof of export is absent or insufficient (ETA ss.168 and 169(4)), disallow related input tax credits, and claw back refunds for up to four years (normal reassessment period), extended to eight years where misrepresentation or fraud exists. Penalties include: ETA s.280 (failure to register - greater of CAD 250 and 1% of net tax), ETA s.285 (failure to report - 5% of unreported net tax plus 1% per month up to 12 months), ETA s.285.1 (false statement or omission - CAD 100 minimum plus 5% of the amount, higher for repeat violations), and ETA s.286 (records failure - prosecution with fines up to CAD 25,000). Income Tax Act s.163(2) imposes a 50% gross-negligence penalty on understated tax. Note: Canadian law imposes no statutory export-FX repatriation deadline; the 180-day benchmark is the prudent internal documentary target recommended to substantiate the cross-border nature of export sales and to withstand transfer-pricing and FAPI review. Overdue or unsubstantiated export claims carry daily compound arrears interest and retrospective clawback of the entire 13% HST amount.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada does not maintain exchange controls. The Canadian dollar is fully convertible on both current account and capital account transactions. Profit remittance through dividends, branch profits, interest, royalties, and management fees is unrestricted and is governed by tax withholding rules under the Canadian Income Tax Act (Part XIII non-resident withholding tax, typically 25% subject to treaty relief). Sanctions administered under the Special Economic Measures Act and the United Nations Act may restrict transactions with sanctioned states, entities, or individuals. Compliance with FINTRAC reporting thresholds and bank-level source-of-funds verification remains mandatory.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory surrender requirement, mandatory conversion ratio, or repatriation deadline for export proceeds in Canada. The restaurant's overseas export/condiment sales receipts may be held in any freely convertible currency. Export transactions must be reported to the Canada Border Services Agency under the Customs Act, and proceeds moving through Canadian bank accounts must satisfy FINTRAC large-value transaction reporting (CAD 10,000 or more) and standard anti-money-laundering record-keeping.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); exchange-control administration is delegated in part to the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for AML/ATF reporting and to Payments Canada for clearing and settlement system oversight; the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) supervises federally chartered banks.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Low-Medium. A foreign electronic-money institution (EMI) is not a Canadian chartered bank and cannot take deposits or establish direct clearing membership. An offshore EMI may lawfully support cross-border payment flows only by partnering with a licensed Canadian payments service provider and by complying with the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) registration regime (Bank of Canada oversight) and, if engaged in money services business, FINTRAC MSB registration. For a Canadian-domiciled restaurant, EMIs should be supplementary to commercial banking, not the primary liquidity rail. POS acquirers, Interac debit, and card settlement must route through a licensed Canadian processor.

A foreign electronic-money institution (EMI) is not a Canadian chartered bank and cannot take deposits or establish direct clearing membership. An offshore EMI may lawfully support cross-border payment flows only by partnering with a licensed Canadian payments service provider and by complying with the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) registration regime (Bank of Canada oversight) and, if engaged in money services business, FINTRAC MSB registration. For a Canadian-domiciled restaurant, EMIs should be supplementary to commercial banking, not the primary liquidity rail. POS acquirers, Interac debit, and card settlement must route through a licensed Canadian processor.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: No statutory CAD monetary threshold triggers Local File or Master File preparation; however, contemporaneous documentation must be prepared whenever a transaction with a non-arm's length non-resident occurs, and Form T106 is mandatory for reportable transactions exceeding CAD 50,000 in aggregate with a non-resident in a taxation year; country-by-country reporting applies to Canadian-resident multinational groups with consolidated revenue exceeding CAD 1,000,000,000

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate a separate Economic Substance Regulations regime applicable to a conventional operating restaurant; however, Canada applies the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) and common-law substance-over-form doctrines. A shell or conduit entity whose central management and control is outside Canada may be denied treaty benefits or subpart-F-type attribution; a Canadian-resident hotpot operating company must maintain real staff, premises, operational decision-making, and accounting substance in Ontario

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Reporting

Statutory sanction: Criminal tax evasion under the Canadian Income Tax Act and Excise Tax Act carries a penalty of 50% to 200% of the evaded taxes plus a fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded; imprisonment up to 5 years on indictment and up to 2 years on summary conviction; the CRA may also impose gross-negligence penalties of 50% of the understated tax

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in the falsification can be personally assessed for unpaid corporate tax under the director-liability regime, restrained from leaving Canada through a criminal travel ban, subject to asset seizure orders, and sentenced to imprisonment for tax evasion
Shell Company Abuse and Psychological Presence

Statutory sanction: CRA may apply the statutory residence test and deem the corporation non-resident of Canada, triggering a Canadian departure tax, the denial of Ontario small business deductions, and full exposure to foreign-source income taxation in the offshore jurisdiction plus Part XIII penalties on unrendered distributions; a sham or agency finding invalidates planned intercompany payments

Director personal liability: Directors of an artificial shell can be held jointly and severally liable for the corporation's unremitted withholding and GST/HST; a civil fraud claim supports veil-piercing, causing the director's personal assets to satisfy corporate obligations; criminal charges for culpable neglect are possible
Failure to Implement Transfer-Pricing Documentation and Arm's Length Pricing

Statutory sanction: CRA is empowered to reassess the taxpayer to return the profit to arm's length levels, apply a 10% transfer-pricing penalty on the net adjustment grossly exceeding the arm's length amount, charge interest on deficiencies, and disallow the offsetting deduction for management fees or royalties

Director personal liability: Directors who approved the non-compliant intercompany pricing face personal gross-negligence penalties and civil restitution; the CRA may issue a director liability assessment for the unpaid corporate portion if the failure to remit statutory withholdings was accessory to the scheme
Anti-Money-Laundering (AML) and Structuring of Payments

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, failure to register as a money service business, file suspicious transaction reports, or maintain records carries administrative monetary penalties of up to CAD 100,000 for a corporation and CRA will add unassessed revenue from the attempted concealment plus 50% gross-negligence penalties

Director personal liability: Directors may be prosecuted for laundering proceeds, face imprisonment of up to 10 years, criminal forfeiture of corporate and personal assets, and an order prohibiting the issuance of a business licence for the restaurant
Non-Compliance with Provincial Business Licensing and Food Safety

Statutory sanction: Ontario municipal licensing and food safety legislation authorizes immediate closure of the premises, daily monetary penalties of CAD 500 to CAD 10,000, revocation of the business licence, and the provincial prosecutorial body may seek restitution for all enforcement and clean-up costs

Director personal liability: The Restaurant Permit holder is personally identified as the operator; directors can be barred from holding food-service permits in Ontario for up to 5 years, fined per violation, and held personally liable for unpaid municipal taxes assessed against the premises
