Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada; online portal: My Business Account (cra-arc.gc.ca/my-business-account), GST/HST NETFILE; Ontario Ministry of Finance for provincial tax program registration where applicable.

E-government filing portal

A foreign corporation must request a federal Business Number (BN) and a GST/HST program account from the CRA. Non-resident entities use Form RC1A (Request for a Business Number and Certain Accounts for Non-Residents), or Form RC1 when establishing a Canadian resident presence. The applicant submits corporate constitutional documents, proof of registered agent or Canadian representative, evidence of nexus or business activities, and contact information. The CRA issues a 9-digit BN and a GST/HST registration number formatted as BN/RT0001, which also serves as the VAT identification number for indirect tax purposes.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation — a Business Corporation is a taxable Canadian corporation under the Income Tax Act. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — a Business Corporation is a taxable Canadian corporation under the Income Tax Act. Resident corporations are assessed on worldwide income; non-resident or branch operations are taxed on Canadian-source business income and may be subject to branch tax.

Available election forms

No U.S.-style check-the-box election exists under Canadian federal tax law; incorporation results in entity-level taxation. Statutory elections relevant to foreign-owned Canadian corporations include the subsection 216(1) election for non-resident net rental income, the GST/HST small-supplier voluntary registration election, and certain foreign-affiliate elections. Hybrid mismatch outcomes are governed principally by Canada's mandatory BEPS Action 2 rules and Income Tax Act provisions rather than elective entity-classification mechanisms.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide gross revenue from taxable supplies, including zero-rated supplies, over the immediately preceding four calendar quarters, aggregated with associates; non-resident persons making taxable supplies in Canada must register once the threshold is exceeded, and may voluntarily register to recover input tax credits.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act, a supply of goods is zero-rated for GST/HST purposes when the goods are exported from Canada as part of the supply, or delivered outside Canada by the supplier or at the supplier's direction, and ownership and possession transfer to a non-resident. For Grade A refined copper cathodes, the exporter must evidence physical removal from Canada, identify the non-resident buyer, and retain contemporaneous commercial and transport documentation. Supplies of services directly related to exported goods and rendered to non-residents may also qualify if not tied to Canadian real property. Zero-rated exports permit the exporter to claim input tax credits on related Canadian expenses and obtain a net refund where input tax credits exceed output tax.

  • B13A / Canadian Customs Export Declaration (or authorized electronic equivalent submitted to CBSA)
  • Commercial invoice and packing list
  • Bill of Lading (ocean, air, rail, or truck) evidencing physical removal from Canada
  • Freight forwarder or carrier certification of export
  • Sale and purchase contract with the non-resident counterparty
  • Inward remittance evidence or documentary letter of credit settlement confirmation from a bank
  • LME Deliverable Warrant transfer or warehouse receipt transfer evidence where the copper cathodes are sold under LME warehouse procedures
  • Proof of GST/HST registration number and identification of the zero-rated supply category under Schedule VI Part V

Canada does not operate a real-time fiscal clearance, pre-clearance API stamping, or digital tax-stamp regime. Electronic invoices and records are accepted by the CRA if they are complete, legible, and capable of substantiating the GST/HST treatment of transactions. GST/HST returns are filed electronically through GST/HST NETFILE or CRA-approved web services. For exported goods, CBSA export declarations must be transmitted electronically through the Canadian Export Reporting System or equivalent approved EDI channels where applicable.

The CRA may deny zero-rating and re-assess the supply at the full 13% Ontario HST within the normal four-year reassessment period, or indefinitely where the exporter made a misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, wilful default, or fraud. Civil penalties include the section 280 Excise Tax Act penalty of 25% of the understated net tax for knowing participation in false statements, plus interest and administrative penalties. Criminal prosecution under section 327 of the Excise Tax Act can lead to fines up to CAD 100,000 and imprisonment up to five years on indictment. Additional penalties under the Customs Act for false export declarations and retrospective clawbacks of input tax credits are available to the authorities.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is freely convertible for ordinary commercial and capital transactions. Canada generally permits non-resident ownership, foreign-currency accounts, dividend payments, interest payments, principal repayments, trade settlements, and repatriation of sale proceeds without prior foreign-exchange approval. There is no general capital-control approval for equity funding or shareholder loans. Practical remittance constraints arise from bank sanctions screening, anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing review, beneficial-ownership verification, source-of-funds evidence, tax withholding compliance, transfer-pricing support, corporate authorization, solvency requirements, and documentation of the underlying commercial purpose. Dividends, interest, royalties, and certain other payments to non-residents may be subject to Canadian withholding tax unless reduced or eliminated under an applicable tax treaty. Related-party financing must be reviewed for thin-capitalization, transfer-pricing, interest-limitation, withholding-tax, and debt-versus-equity consequences. Large-value or unusual transactions may be delayed pending enhanced due diligence.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no general statutory deadline requiring a Canadian exporter to convert foreign-currency receipts into Canadian dollars. Export proceeds may generally remain in foreign-currency accounts or be converted at the company’s discretion. The corporation should retain contracts, invoices, bills of lading, customs records, warehouse or warrant documentation, payment confirmations, and reconciliation records. Financial institutions and other reporting entities must comply with applicable FINTRAC reporting obligations, including reporting qualifying international electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more, including aggregated transactions within the prescribed 24-hour period. The corporation must separately assess corporate tax reporting, GST/HST treatment, customs requirements, Statistics Canada reporting, foreign-affiliate and foreign-property reporting, and any applicable sanctions or export-control obligations. Bank-specific foreign-exchange documentation and transaction-purpose information may still be required even though no statutory surrender rule applies.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada. Canada has no general foreign-exchange administration imposing capital-account controls, mandatory currency conversion, or export-proceeds surrender. Prudential supervision of federally regulated deposit-taking institutions is principally conducted by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions, while financial-crime compliance is administered by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for payment administration and foreign-exchange execution, but low as a substitute for a full Canadian commercial-bank relationship for a physical copper-trading business. An offshore electronic-money institution or payment institution cannot automatically provide Canadian deposit-taking, Canadian-dollar account services, documentary letters of credit, borrowing facilities, or safeguarded funds equivalent to a Canadian bank account. Use is subject to the provider’s licensing perimeter, correspondent-bank access, sanctions controls, Canadian recipient-bank acceptance, source-of-funds review, transaction limits, safeguarding arrangements, and continuity risk. A Canadian money-services business may provide certain payment or foreign-exchange services but remains subject to applicable registration and AML obligations and generally is not a deposit-taking institution. Use regulated Canadian banks for core operating funds, trade finance, and settlement of high-value metal transactions; use fintech providers only for approved ancillary payments after legal and counterparty due diligence.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined federal (15%) and Ontario provincial (11.5%) general corporate income tax rate for active business income not eligible for the small business deduction; Ontario provincial rate is 11.5% for general corporations. The combined rate for Canadian-controlled private corporations claiming the Ontario small business deduction on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income is approximately 12.2% federal and provincial combined, subject to Ontario's phase-out and clawback rules.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under the Canadian Income Tax Act Part XIII on dividends paid or credited by a corporation resident in Canada to a non-resident shareholder.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return must be filed within six months after the corporation's fiscal year-end. For a corporation with a December 31 year-end, the filing deadline is June 30 of the following calendar year. Tax payments are due in monthly installments plus a final balance within two months (or three months for Canadian-controlled private corporations, in limited circumstances) after the fiscal year-end. Ontario provincial tax is due under the same schedule. Where the six-month filing deadline falls on a Saturday, Sunday, or CRA-recognized holiday, the filing deadline is extended to the next business day.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Under the CRA's transfer pricing documentation requirements (subsection 247(4)), all Canadian corporations engaged in transactions with non-arm's length non-residents must keep complete and contemporaneous documentation. There is no de minimis threshold for the documentation obligation; however, the CRA's administrative policy imposes a CAD 1 million threshold for loans, CAD 5 million for tangible property transactions, and CAD 1 million for intangible property and services transactions for purposes of applying penalties for failing to provide documentation. The Master File must be prepared by multinational groups with consolidated group revenue of CAD 50 million or more, and Local File documentation is required for Canadian-resident entities with controlled transactions exceeding the specified thresholds. The CRA's standard documentation position also requires an annual TP policy documentation set, including a benchmarking study for intercompany commodity trading, if relevant. Canada's country-by-country (CbC) reporting obligations apply to multinational enterprise groups with consolidated group revenue of at least CAD 750 million in the preceding fiscal year.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a separate statute called the Economic Substance Regulations (ESR); the substance doctrine is applied through multiple anti-avoidance rules: the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) in section 245 of the Income Tax Act, which requires an 'avoidance transaction' and a misuse or abuse of the provisions read as a whole; transfer pricing rules under section 247, which require arm's length terms and adequate documentation; and the 'paid-up capital' and 'capital dividend account' rules, which restrict tax-free distributions from corporations without economic substance. A Canadian corporation must have real commercial presence, actual physical trading and asset ownership, and genuine risk management functions for the copper cathode wholesale business to avoid recharacterization as a conduit or sham. The Federal Court of Appeal in Canada v. Oxford Properties and the GAAR jurisprudence, including the Supreme Court of Canada in Deans Knight, emphasize that a transaction must result in actual economic changes for the taxpayer. Ontario also maintains corporate minimum tax rules (Ontario Corporate Minimum Tax, Part II of the Corporations Tax Act) which can apply where large corporations have substantial accounting profits but low taxable income. The Canada Revenue Agency may disregard a transaction or series of transactions whose primary purpose is tax avoidance under GAAR, impose transfer pricing penalties of 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment (maximum CAD 5 million) and, where documentation is not provided, impose a CAD 1,000 per month penalty for each month that a complete and accurate international financial institution or foreign affiliate information return is missing, up to certain limits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (income tax fraud under the Income Tax Act and Criminal Code of Canada)

Statutory sanction: Under subsection 239(1) of the Income Tax Act: on conviction by indictment, imprisonment for up to five years and a fine ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded; on summary conviction, imprisonment for up to two years less a day and a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded. The Tax Evasion penalty under subsection 163(2) is 50% of the understated tax, or 50% of the false or non-allocated tax credit claimed. CRA may assess gross negligence penalties equal to 50% of the tax understatement. Ontario may similarly levy penalties under the Corporations Tax Act and the Ontario Provincial Offenses Act. The CRA can also immediately freeze bank accounts under the Income Tax Act section 224, issue a requirement to pay to third parties, and register a lien against assets. Restitution orders and forfeiture of proceeds of crime may be imposed under section 462 of the Criminal Code.

Director personal liability: Crown officers and judges may pierce the corporate veil for fraudulent or dishonest conduct. Directors are personally liable for unpaid corporate income tax remittances withheld from employees under subsection 227.1(1) of the Income Tax Act; for GST/HST; and for wages owed to employees under the Ontario Employment Standards Act. If the corporation is found to be a mere agent or sham instrumentality for its shareholder, the court may lift the corporate veil and impose liability for the full tax debt. Directors of the Ontario corporation are also exposed to personal liability for payments made with the intent to defeat creditors under the Ontario Business Corporations Act (OBCA) section 130(1). Criminal tax evasion prosecutions target the directing mind, including the CEO, CFO, and in-house tax director. Convictions can include imprisonment, travel bans through Interpol Red Notices triggered by Crown prosecutions, and seizure of personal assets under proceeds-of-crime provisions.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance (international tax mispricing or failure to maintain contemporaneous documentation)

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing adjustment reassessment at the CRA's discretion. Penalties under subsection 247(3) equal to 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment (maximum CAD 5 million) where the taxpayer fails to make a reasonable attempt to determine an arm's length transfer price and fails to provide, within 90 days of a request, the required documentation. Failure to produce a complete or accurate CbC report under section 237.2 incurs CAD 2,500 per violation, plus CAD 250 per day each day the violation continues. Interest accrues from the date the reassessment is issued. The CRA can also apply GAAR (section 245) to disavow any tax benefit from non-arm's length arrangements lacking economic substance. Documentation-related penalties of CAD 1,000 per month under subsection 239(1) may apply in respect of international information returns like Form T1134.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers are not automatically personally liable for transfer pricing penalties unless they are found to have participated in the making of false statements or omissions, in which case the gross negligence penalties and criminal sanctions under section 239 of the Income Tax Act may be pursued personally. The CRA has the power to assess a director personally for unpaid corporate taxes under section 227.1 and to issue a director's liability assessment for third-party freeze and garnishment. A director who knowingly signs a false T2 return or directs the concealment of transfer pricing documentation can face fines and imprisonment under the Criminal Code. Courts may order restitution for the tax evaded, grant civil recovery against directors for intentional misconduct, and issue travel constraints pending trial.
Thin Capitalization and Excessive Interest Expense (debt loading / capital stripping)

Statutory sanction: Interest deductibility is denied under subsection 18(4) to the extent debt exceeds the 1.5:1 ratio, and the disallowed amount is recharacterized as a dividend for withholding purposes. Under the EIFEL, deduction of net interest and financing expenses is limited to 30% of tax EBITDA, with a de minimis exempt threshold of CAD 1 million of interest subject to transitional complexity. An assessment by CRA can trigger additional tax, interest, and penalties, and the denial of funding costs can push the company into insolvency. CRA may impose a 10% penalty under subsection 247(3) for a pricing adjustment on the excess interest. In egregious cases, GAAR can apply to deny the entire loan structure's tax benefit and subject the disallowed amount to withholding tax under Part XIII.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally exposed for unpaid corporate taxes and withholding taxes to the extent caused by the disallowed interest expense if they authorized the loan arrangement knowing it was designed to strip earnings. A director can be assessed under subsection 227.1. In a bankruptcy scenario, the directors may be liable for corporate obligations found to be fraudulent preferences under the Bankruptcy and Insolvency Act if the loan arrangement was made to defeat creditors.
Profit Repatriation and Withholding Tax Failure (unreported deemed dividends / cross-border distributions)

Statutory sanction: The CRA can assess the Canadian corporation for the full amount of unremitted withholding tax, plus arrears interest compounded daily and penalties. Under subsection 215(1) of the Income Tax Act, a withholding failure carries a penalty of 10% of the withholdable amount plus 5% of the amount where the failure is made knowingly; under subsection 227(8), the amount withheld is a Crown debt. The corporation may also be subject to a fine of up to CAD 5,000 or imprisonment up to two years (summary conviction) under section 239. For a false statement in connection with a deemed dividend, gross negligence penalties apply. Ontario does not assess a distinct provincial dividend withholding tax under the OCT structure; the federal withholding tax is the operative charge.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers are personally liable for withholding tax that the corporation fails to withhold, remit, or pay, under subsection 227.1 of the Income Tax Act. The CRA can issue an assessment against a director personally after the corporation has been assessed and failed to pay. A director cannot avoid liability by resignation if the withholding tax accrued during their term; liability extends to current and former directors and is a joint and several obligation. The penalty exposure is compounded where the distribution is part of a scheme to strip value from the Canadian corporation for the benefit of a non-resident parent.
AML / Anti-Money Laundering and Proceeds of Crime (POCA / FINTRAC / Criminal Code 462)

Statutory sanction: Failure to report a suspicious transaction or a large cash transaction: administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 2 million for a corporate entity, and imprisonment for up to five years for willful non-compliance under the PCMLTFA. Knowingly engaging in money laundering is an offense under section 462.31 of the Criminal Code punishable by imprisonment for up to 10 years, and a first-time money laundering conviction can impose fines of up to CAD 1 million or more, depending on the proceeds traced. The CRA and FINTRAC jointly share intelligence; a conviction under the Criminal Code can also result in forfeiture of property and proceeds, including seized LME warrants and bank balances. Canadian courts can issue restraint and freezing orders over assets. If the copper trading business is classified as a Money Service Business (MSB) under FINTRAC's regulations, the entity must register as an MSB; failure to register is an offense with fines up to CAD 500,000 and imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Corporate officers and directors are exposed to personal criminal liability where they knowingly participate in or direct money laundering infrastructure (section 21 Criminal Code as parties to offense). Under the PCMLTFA, due diligence and record-keeping require a compliance officer; personal liability arises for breach of the reporting duties and for directing transactions designed to escape FINTRAC reporting thresholds. The CRA and FINTRAC publish names of convicted directors. Senior personnel may face travel bans pending prosecution and asset seizures. In civil proceedings, the corporate veil is pierced to recover proceeds hidden in personal accounts; a director who personally guarantees maritime or LC obligations may also be directly liable.
False Statements and Fraudulent Conveyance (contract fraud / excise / customs misdeclaration)

Statutory sanction: Under the Customs Act, false statements on imported goods trigger a penalty of 50% of the value of the goods (or the duties evaded) plus the loss of goods; knowingly making false statements for export/import is an offense with a fine up to CAD 500,000 and imprisonment up to five years. Under the Criminal Code making a false document, forgery, or uttering a forged document is an indictable offense with imprisonment up to 10 years. Breach of the Canada Business Corporations Act (or OBCA) annual return requirements, such as failure to file corporate returns, can lead to dissolution of the corporation under the OBCA, resulting in the forfeiture of corporate property to the Crown and restoration compliance costs. In Ontario, unlicensed commodity trading not involving a regulated dealer generally does not attract an Ontario Securities Commission penalty unless the activity falls within commodity futures contracts under Ontario securities law; but fraud-based trading does attract OSC enforcement and fines up to CAD 1 million or three times the profit made, plus imprisonment up to five years under the Ontario Securities Act.

Director personal liability: Under section 239 of the Income Tax Act, a director who has agreed to or assented to the filing of a false T2 return may be personally charged with tax evasion. Under the OBCA, directors are liable for prohibited acts including issuing false financial statements, and courts may order them to compensate the corporation for losses caused by their breach of fiduciary duty. Misrepresentations to lenders in LC and trade finance contracts expose directors personally where they have provided personal guarantees; the lender may seek civil restitution and may seize personal assets. A conviction for forgery carries personal imprisonment and is an absolute bar to continuing as a director of a Canadian corporation.
