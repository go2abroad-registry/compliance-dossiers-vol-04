Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital exists under the QBCA; a corporation may be incorporated with zero issued shares. E-government filing portal

A foreign-incorporated payment-service provider carrying on business in Canada obtains a CRA Business Number (BN) via Business Registration Online or Form RC1, quoting its foreign certificate of incorporation and appointing a Canadian attorney for service; program accounts RC (Part I/Part XIV income tax), RT (GST/HST), RP (payroll) and RM (import-export) are activated under the same BN. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: a Business Corporation is a separate taxpayer resident in Canada taxed under ITA Part I on worldwide income; for non-resident corporations the scope is source-based taxation plus Part XIV 25% branch tax. No check-the-box or pass-through eligibility applies; no tax-exempt-zone status applies to a commercial PSP in Quebec. Federal general corporate rate is 15% (28% base less 13% general reduction), with the CCPC small-business rate at 9% on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income; Quebec adds provincial rates of 11.5% general and 5% for qualified small-business income.

Available election forms

No US check-the-box election exists; entity-level taxation is automatic. Relevant statutory elections include: ETA s.156 joint election of closely related registrants to treat intercompany supplies as taxable; ETA s.150(1) GST25 election by a financial institution to treat otherwise-exempt financial services as taxable so the PSP can claim input tax credits on acquiring, gateway and interchange infrastructure; ITA s.85 rollover election to transfer merchant contracts and software IP into the Canadian corporation on a tax-deferred basis; ITA s.93(2) and s.113 elections for foreign-affiliate surplus repatriation; CCPC classification and associated-corporation elections under ITA ss.125 and 256; and the 2024 interest-limitation (EIFEL) election, waiver and filing mechanics set by ITA ss.18.2 to 18.21, with transfer-pricing documentation under ITA s.247(4).

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000: GST/HST small-supplier threshold (ETA s.148) and the identical QST registration threshold; taxable supplies of goods and services made worldwide determine the threshold, while zero-rated export supplies and settlement flows are excluded; voluntary registration is available below CAD 30,000 to recover input tax credits.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Canada are zero-rated under ETA Schedule VI, Part V ss.16-17 with proof of export (supplier export or delivery to a carrier). Services, including acquiring and clearing services, are zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V s.7 when supplied to a non-resident who is not registered and does not use the service in a Canadian business or in relation to Canadian real or capital property; financial services supplied to non-resident non-registrants are zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part II s.20. For the cross-border payment gateway, fees invoiced to foreign merchants on Visa/Mastercard/Fedwire rails qualify only where KYC files evidence foreign residence, settlement is made in foreign currency to a foreign account, and the service is not connected to a Canadian location or Canadian customer base.

  • Canadian Automated Export Declaration (CAED) or Canada Customs Export Declaration line items
  • Commercial invoice matched to acquirer settlement and interchange fee reports
  • Bill of Lading, air waybill or electronic transport document for physical export shipments
  • Inward Remittance Certificate, SWIFT MT103/202, or foreign exchange conversion proof evidencing settlement within 180 days
  • Merchant KYC and non-resident status file: tax residency certificate, foreign VAT/GST registration, registered business address and board or regulatory attestations

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control or digital stamping exists at any Canadian level. Canada operates the voluntary Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Service (PCEI) on Peppol BIS 3.0 for B2B and B2G; CRA and Revenu Québec require electronic filing of GST/HST/QST returns by registrants with annual taxable supplies above CAD 1,500,000 and demand that all e-books and records be kept in original machine-readable form for six years. For a PSP, daily interchange clearing records, merchant settlement balance files and Fedwire/Visa/Mastercard network trace data constitute the primary fiscal evidence trail.

CRA may reassess zero-rated export treatment within four years under ETA s.298 and without time limit where misrepresentation or fraud exists; any clawback carries statutory daily compounded interest, plus the ETA s.285 gross-negligence penalty of 5% of underreported net tax and a minimum monetary penalty per false statement. Non-repatriation beyond the 180-day benchmark exposes the receivable to transfer-pricing recharacterization under ITA s.247 and to deemed-interest or dividend treatment, while Revenu Québec mirrors the exposure under the QST Act and the Tax Administration Act s.59 (50% penalty for deliberate or grossly negligent false declarations). Directors and officers face joint and several liability under ETA s.323 for unremitted GST/HST/QST and criminal prosecution under ETA s.327 for knowing evasion, attracting fines up to the amount evaded and imprisonment up to five years on indictment.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible for residents and non-residents. Canada imposes no exchange control restrictions on current-account transfers, capital-account transactions, dividends, interest, royalties, management fees, or loan repayments. A Quebec corporation may maintain foreign-currency bank accounts with Canadian Schedule I banks and may freely pay imports, merchant payouts, and third-party suppliers abroad. Profit remittance is constrained only by tax law: non-resident shareholders are subject to Part XIII withholding tax under the Income Tax Act (Canada) at a 25% statutory rate, generally reduced to 15% (portfolio) or 5% (10% ownership) under an applicable tax treaty; a Canadian branch of a non-resident corporation is subject to Part I tax plus 25% branch tax, subject to treaty reduction. Quebec's Taxation Act contains equivalent rules for Quebec-source distributions. The Investment Canada Act also requires a non-Canadian establishing a new Canadian business or acquiring control of a Canadian business to file an initial notification or, above prescribed review thresholds, a net-benefit application. The Gateway must additionally register under the Retail Payment Activities Act with the Bank of Canada and, if operating in Quebec, hold an AMF money-services business licence.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Not applicable. Canada has no statutory timeline for repatriating export proceeds, no mandatory conversion of foreign currency into Canadian dollars, and no surrender requirement for payment-gateway settlement balances. Exporters and payment service providers may retain 100% of foreign-currency receipts in Canadian or offshore foreign-currency accounts. The only relevant duties are FINTRAC reporting obligations: cash or monetary instruments of C$10,000 or more crossing the border must be reported to the Canada Border Services Agency (Form E677); large cash transactions at or above C$10,000 within a 24-hour period require a Large Cash Transaction Report; and FINTRAC Travel Rule data, including originator and beneficiary information, must accompany international wire transfers.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada, acting as monetary authority and, since the Retail Payment Activities Act (Canada), as the retail payments supervision authority. Canada has no central foreign-exchange administration: the Foreign Exchange Control Act was repealed in 1951, and no exchange control approval is required by the Bank of Canada or the Minister of Finance for any cross-border payment, capital injection, or profit remittance. Prudential regulation of banks rests with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); Payments Canada operates Lynx, the high-value Canadian-dollar real-time gross settlement system, and the ACSS batch clearing system. Anti-money-laundering supervision of money services businesses is conducted by FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA); in Quebec, the Autorité des marchés financiers (AMF) licenses Money-Services Businesses under the Money-Services Businesses Act (Quebec).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium feasibility with substantial legal constraints. Canada has no equivalent of the European 'EMI' or 'payment institution' passport; an offshore digital EMI cannot clear Canadian-dollar payments through Lynx or provide local settlement accounts on its own. The Quebec gateway must itself be the regulated entity: it must be a Canadian resident for FINTRAC MSB registration, hold an AMF money-services business licence for Quebec operations, and maintain a settlement account with a Schedule I bank or with Payments Canada participant bank. An offshore EMI may be used only as a supplementary technology or correspondent partner, provided it is regulated in its home jurisdiction, is not on any sanctions list, and is onboarded by the Canadian bank through a full correspondent-bank due-diligence process. Because OSFI-regulated banks treat cross-border PSPs as high-risk, the gateway should expect enhanced due diligence and a capital-escrow or segregated-client-funds structure. Direct clearing membership in Lynx is not available to the PSP itself; bank sponsorship is required.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium feasibility with substantial legal constraints. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.2% combined Quebec and federal headline rate for a Quebec active business corporation (11.5% federal general rate plus 11.5% Quebec provincial rate, before the federal general rate reduction and provincial abatement mechanics; actual combined effective statutory rate on active business income is approximately 26.2% in Quebec for 2025, assuming no M&P reduction and standard rate settings)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% for dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder under subsection 212(2) of the Income Tax Act; the rate is 25% for Quebec if the corporation is resident in Quebec and pays a dividend to a non-resident under the Taxation Act; the statutory rate is 25% but reduced under applicable tax treaties typically to 5% for a 10% shareholder and 15% for portfolio holdings when a treaty applies.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Mandatory contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation (Local File and Master File in the conventional sense) is not codified under a fixed CAD monetary threshold in Canada, but Canadian TP compliance under section 247 of the Income Tax Act requires documentation only when a penalty is sought; the CRA's administrative policy and evaluation thresholds set out that a penalty for a transfer pricing adjustment of CAD 10 million or more (subsection 247(3)) attracts a penalty equal to 10% of the adjustment if a document has not been prepared and provided within 90 days of a CRA demand; the CRA also requires that documentation be in place before filing the T2 return for transactions exceeding CAD 50,000 in gross revenue for each transaction class or where the value of the transaction is over CAD 1,000,000 in aggregate with a single related group, while the formal TP Master File/Local File documentation is recommended for multinational groups with gross revenue above CAD 50 million, and CRA Information Circular 87-2R clarifies the expectations; Revenu Quebec similar obligations are set out in section 1018.7 of the Taxation Act (the documentation requirements apply to corporations with gross revenue exceeding CAD 1,000,000 for each of the last two fiscal years and transactions with foreign non-arm's length parties exceeding CAD 1,000,000)

Economic substance scope: Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) as defined in the OECD Base Erosion and Profit Shifting Action 5 (BEPS) and implemented in Canada's GAAR (Section 245 of the Income Tax Act) and Quebec's anti-avoidance rule (Section 1099.1 of the Taxation Act) apply whenever an entity claims treaty or tax holiday benefits; a payment service provider operating through a Quebec subsidiary must demonstrate real physical presence, employees who conduct management and operational decisions in Quebec, full corporate records and bank accounts maintained at the Quebec office, and non-routine control of merchant settlement funds; a pure digital PSP that lacks a Quebec office and has only a registered agent will fail the economic substance test, be deemed to have a permanent establishment or sham, and attract recharacterization of revenue; the Quebec Business Corporations Act and Canada's corporate laws allow for a registered agent office, but the Income Tax Act's definition of permanent establishment requires a place of management or fixed base, thus a foreign PSP with a Quebec marketing office could be deemed to have PE; in addition, the Canada Revenue Agency considers the digital platform business to have a PE in Canada if it has a dependent agent who habitually exercises the authority to conclude contracts (section 253.1 and treaty Article 5); the ESR doctrine in Canada does not impose a fee-based regime as in the Cayman Islands, but economic substance is a factual requirement for treaty eligibility and for the payment of the 5% reduced withholding rate.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Transfer pricing non-compliance and base erosion (Section 247 of the Income Tax Act)

Statutory sanction: A transfer pricing adjustment may be assessed against the Quebec PSP as if the transaction was at arm's length, with the additional taxable income subject to a 10% gross-up on the assessment under subsection 247(8); penalties under subsection 247(3) of 10% of the total adjustment apply where documents were not delivered within 90 days of a written demand, and interest accrues on the reassessment; a deduction denial on royalty payments may apply under section 18(1)(e) and 67.5 for unreasonable deductions, and a Part III tax under subsections 212(1) and 212(4) could impose 25% withholding tax on hidden distributions. Quebec may assess a provincial penalty under section 1029.9 of the Taxation Act of 10% of the Quebec adjustment.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers of a PSP who participate in the creation of false transfer pricing records or who direct tax evasion are jointly and severally liable under section 160.1 and 160.2 for kiting and intercorporate transfers, and a court can lift the corporate veil under the Quebec Civil Code Article 317 where the corporation is a mere conduit; CRA can levy against personal assets and use collection procedures; a director may be personally liable for unpaid corporate tax withheld at source (subsection 227.1), and where fraudulent misrepresentation is proven, directors can face imprisonment under section 239(1) of the Income Tax Act (up to 5 years) and under section 992 of the Taxation Act (up to 5 years), plus penalties equal to 150% of the tax evaded.
Tax evasion and fraudulent misrepresentation (Criminal Code Section 380 and Income Tax Act Section 239)

Statutory sanction: Summary conviction: CAD 10,000 fine and/or up to 2 years imprisonment; indictment: CAD 25,000 fine and/or imprisonment up to 5 years, plus court-ordered fine equal to up to double the tax evaded (subsections 239(1) and 239(2) of the Income Tax Act); Quebec Taxation Act imposes a penalty of 50% of the amount assessed where misrepresentation but not wilful default (subsection 1040), a penalty of 150% for wilful evasion and up to CAD 250,000 and/or imprisonment of up to five years for tax evasion (sections 996 and 997); the CRA will demand full payment of all taxes, interest, and penalties before the court hears an appeal, and a conviction triggers publication in the CRA's name-and-shame list (since 2014) of the individual and the corporation.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly assent to or participate in tax evasion face personal criminal liability under subsection 239(1), and under Quebec under section 998 of the Taxation Act, and a director who is not individually named but whose acts lead to a corporate conviction may be charged with conspiracy or accessory liability; civil restitution is enforceable through a CRA Secured Certificate filed in Federal Court under section 223(2), which results in a legal hypothec on all property; directors can be prohibited from leaving Canada through a court order under the Criminal Code or the Income Tax Act, and a travel ban can be imposed upon charge; a director may also be disqualified from serving as a director under the Quebec Business Corporations Act and may be ordered to compensate the corporation or creditors of the sweep of funds.
Money laundering and unlicensed money services business (MSB) activity (Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), Criminal Code section 462.31)

Statutory sanction: Unlicensed MSB activity can result in administrative monetary penalties (AMPs) under PCMLTFA up to CAD 3,000,000 per violation for corporate entities (Section 73.12), prosecution of senior officers under Section 74.1 for up to CAD 500,000 plus imprisonment of up to six months, and a court order compelling registration or shutdown of the business; failure to report suspicious transactions attracts AMPs of up to CAD 1,000,000, and laundering proceeds of crime under section 462.31 of the Criminal Code is indictable and punishable by imprisonment not exceeding 10 years; a designated business must have a compliance program that includes a written AML/ATF policies and procedures, training, compliance officer, and risk assessment.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers of a PSP can be prosecuted under the Criminal Code if they direct, authorize, assist, or participate in money laundering, and the court may impose up to 10 years in prison; under the PCMLTFA, penalties may be imposed on individuals who agreed to the violation, and the director can be named personally; a conviction triggers a proscription of the offender from acting as director of any corporation subject to the Business Corporations Act for a maximum period of 10 years (federal Director's disqualification section), and civil asset forfeiture can reach personal property under provincial Quebec forfeiture rules (Section 7.1 of the Civil Code and Code of Penal Procedure provisions); a travel embargo is possible under the Criminal Code when a person is charged with money laundering involving foreign transportation of funds.
Ontario/Quebec licensing and unregistered payment processor (Quebec's Money Services Businesses Act and AMF/Ontario's Registration Act) – non-compliance with provincial payment processor licensing regulations

Statutory sanction: AMF may issue a cease-and-desist order and impose administrative penalties of up to CAD 500,000 for each contravention; Quebec legislation also imposes a maximum fine of CAD 100,000 for a corporation and imprisonment for affected officers in certain cases; failure to remit QST/TVQ on gateway fees attracts a civil penalty of 10% of the net tax amount and interest, and the Commissioner of Revenue may issue a corrigendum and impose fraud penalties of 50% or 150% if the omission is in bad faith; transactional money laundering under PCMLTFA may also lead to a federal injunction against the PSB, freezing all Quebec bank accounts.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly authorize unlicensed activities are required to compensate any aggrieved merchant under Quebec's consumer protection and financial services legislation, and the Quebec enterprise registrar can cancel all permits if a director's individual licence has been revoked; Quebec courts may prohibit the director from managing any money services business; for a non-resident director, travel bans imposed under the Canada Evidence Act or provincial enforcement orders prevent re-entry into Canada until the statutory penalties are paid; and under the joint civil liability regime of the Quebec Civil Code Article 316, directors can be liable to the PSP's creditors when the business is operated contrary to public order.
Failure to register as a resident corporation or permanent establishment and abuse of the limited liability vehicle (corporate veil piercing, misuse of agent agreements)

Statutory sanction: The CRA can treat the corporation as a sham or sham trust and assess tax on the true foreign parent under section 160, and can impose a penalty for false statements or omissions equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the understated tax (subsection 163(2), 20%; Quebec section 1040.1 at 50% or 150%); if the corporate entity is used to commit tax fraud or evade a liability, Quebec courts will impose civil liability directly on the sole shareholder and director under the doctrine of piercing the corporate veil; the name company's registration may be dissolved for failure to file the Quebec declaration immatriculation and federal annual return (Canada Business Corporations Act); unlawful corporate stripping of a Canadian active business results in the disallowance of the 50% provincial abatement and the application of the 25% withholding on deemed dividends to the foreign parent.

Director personal liability: Under subsection 227.1 of the Income Tax Act, directors are jointly and severally liable for the corporation's unremitted source deductions, and for unremitted QST and GST/HST (GST/HST section 323), and this liability can be collected against personal assets; Section 160 of the Income Tax Act may apply to transfer tax liability to the shareholder; Quebec allows a court order to seize personal property of a director who intentionally failed to observe corporate formalities; travel bans at the federal level are available under the Income Tax Act collection procedure when a director's debts exceed CAD 1,000,000 and there is a risk that the director will flee Canada; criminal liability can extend to a fine of 100% of the evaded tax plus imprisonment of up to five years; and Canadian immigration law (Immigration and Refugee Protection Act) excludes a director of a dissolved tax non-filer from entry where there are reasonable grounds to believe they are involved in organized criminal activity or misrepresentation for financial gain.
