Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

Ontario imposes no statutory minimum paid-up capital and no escrow account requirement for a limited partnership. The limited partners' capital contributions are fixed by the limited partnership agreement and must be stated in the Declaration of Limited Partnership; the contribution must be actual and not merely nominal, and a limited partner may not withdraw or receive a return of contribution if the limited partnership is insolvent or would be rendered insolvent.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — national tax authority for GST/HST, income tax, and payroll administration, accessible through the 'My Business Account' portal and Business Registration Online at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html. For Ontario: the 8% provincial HST component is collected and administered exclusively by the CRA under the Comprehensive Integrated Tax Coordination Agreement (CITCA) with the Ontario Ministry of Finance. Customs and export control functions are administered by the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS).

E-government filing portal

A non-resident limited partnership carrying on business in Ontario must obtain a 9-digit CRA Business Number (BN) with a four-character program account suffix by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts), submitted electronically through Business Registration Online or by mail to the CRA Sudbury Tax Centre. Because the LP is deemed a separate 'person' for GST/HST purposes under subsection 272.1(1) of the Excise Tax Act (ETA), it must apply for its own GST/HST program account (identifier: BN + RT0001) notwithstanding fiscal transparency for income tax. The RC1 filing must attach the Ontario limited partnership certificate, the partnership agreement, and identify the general partner as responsible representative; where the general partner is non-resident, the CRA may require a Canadian resident representative or a security deposit under section 298(1) of the ETA. Registration is voluntary where worldwide annual taxable supplies do not exceed CAD 30,000, but the LP should register proactively to claim input tax credits (ITCs) on Canadian warehousing, logistics, and import costs. For income tax, the partnership is not itself a taxpayer; the LP files an annual T5013 partnership information return under its dedicated partnership program account (typically suffixed PN0001) and allocates income to each partner. Prior to tax registration, the LP must be registered with the Ontario Business Registry under the Limited Partnerships Act (R.S.O. 1990, c. L.16); no separate Ontario sales tax registration is required because HST is federally administered.

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible — dual-regime result. (1) Income tax: an Ontario LP is fiscally transparent under subsection 96(1) of the Income Tax Act (Canada); income and losses flow to partners, including foreign limited partners, and no entity-level federal income tax is imposed on the partnership. (2) Indirect tax: under subsection 272.1(1) of the ETA, the LP is deemed a separate person for GST/HST and must register, charge, collect, and remit HST in its own name on taxable supplies made in Ontario. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — dual-regime result. (1) Income tax: an Ontario LP is fiscally transparent under subsection 96(1) of the Income Tax Act (Canada); income and losses flow to partners, including foreign limited partners, and no entity-level federal income tax is imposed on the partnership. (2) Indirect tax: under subsection 272.1(1) of the ETA, the LP is deemed a separate person for GST/HST and must register, charge, collect, and remit HST in its own name on taxable supplies made in Ontario. (3) Customs: the LP acts as exporter/importer of record under the Customs Act. Territorial scope: Canada taxes worldwide income of resident taxpayers; a Canadian LP is resident in Canada, so export-sale profit is allocated to partners under Canadian income tax rules unless treaty relief applies at partner level, while non-resident partners are taxable only on Canadian-source business income attributable to a Canadian permanent establishment.

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a US-style 'check-the-box' election. Functional equivalents available to this LP: (1) ETA section 240(2.1) — voluntary non-resident registration election permitting the LP to register for GST/HST even without making taxable supplies in Canada, thereby allowing ITC recovery on Canadian inputs; (2) ETA section 273 closely related person election — where a corporation owns at least 90% of the LP's interests, inter-entity supplies may be treated as nil consideration to eliminate HST leakage on internal distribution movements; (3) ITA hybrid mismatch rules (sections 12.7 and 18.4) apply automatically and require no election, but the foreign general partner entity (if a US LLC) should file US Form 8832 to elect its US classification consistently with Canadian transparency expectations; (4) small supplier 'separate person' arrangements under ETA subsection 148(1.2), which aggregate associated entities for the CAD 30,000 threshold; and (5) treaty-based partner-level elections, including waivers of treaty protection for non-resident partners allocating Canadian-source business income. All elections must be filed with the CRA within statutory deadlines and retained in the LP's Canadian tax election binder.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies in the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters (small supplier threshold, subsection 148(1) of the Excise Tax Act); zero-rated export supplies count toward this computation, but the mandatory registration rule in section 240(1) is not triggered solely by zero-rated supplies where the non-resident is not carrying on business in Canada. Once the threshold is exceeded, GST/HST registration is mandatory within 30 days. The federal Budget 2025 (Bill C-69) proposes raising the threshold to CAD 50,000 for domestic suppliers effective 2026; a foreign-controlled LP should continue testing against CAD 30,000 under current enacted law.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of acetate ophthalmic eyewear frames qualify for 0% GST/HST under Schedule VI, Part V (section 1, general export of goods) of the Excise Tax Act when: (i) the LP supplies the goods to a non-resident purchaser; (ii) the goods are 'exported as part of the supply' (ETA subsection 23(1)), meaning the LP or its agent delivers the goods to a carrier for direct shipment outside Canada and title passes outside Canada; (iii) exportation is completed within 60 days of shipment, per CRA GST/HST Memorandum 20.2 evidentiary practice; and (iv) the LP retains contemporaneous evidence of continuous physical export — CERS export declaration, through bill of lading, and proof of foreign customs clearance. Ancillary offshore services supplied to non-residents (e.g., logistics coordination, warranty administration) qualify for zero-rating under Schedule VI, Part III if the recipient is a non-resident and the service does not relate to Canadian real property. Temporary Ontario warehousing before export does not defeat zero-rating provided the goods are not used or consumed in Canada and the export moves through the documented flow. The LP should request a private GST/HST ruling from the CRA Rulings Directorate to fix the classification of HS 9003.11 eyewear frames in the specific distribution structure.

  • Canadian Export Declaration — electronic filing via the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS/CBSA) showing HS 9003.11 classification, commodity value, destination country, exporter identifiers, and the unique export transaction reference (mandatory for goods valued at CAD 2,000 or more; successor to the former Form B13).
  • Commercial Invoice and Packing List — issued by the LP with Incoterms 2020 delivery terms (e.g., FOB Toronto or CIF foreign port), stating the purchaser's full foreign legal name and address, unit price, quantities, currency, and total export value in CAD equivalent.
  • Through Bill of Lading or Air Waybill — issued by the underlying carrier naming a consignee at a foreign destination, showing the export port, onboard endorsement, and final foreign discharge port; a marine through-B/L is primary evidence of overseas delivery.
  • Inward Remittance Confirmation — bank statement or certificate from a Canadian chartered bank (Schedule I) confirming receipt of export proceeds in a freely convertible foreign currency into the LP's account within the contractual settlement window; this functions as the inward remittance certificate for CRA and FINTRAC purposes.
  • CBSA eManifest Export Confirmation — carrier-submitted manifest and CERS transaction receipt evidencing that the goods physically departed Canada under the legislated export reporting regime.
  • Foreign Customs Entry Document — destination-country import declaration, free-zone entry, or transit document proving arrival and clearance at the foreign point, which CRA Memorandum 20.2 treats as the best evidence of actual delivery.
  • Zero-Rating Substantiation Log — per-invoice attestation and shipment register reconciling foreign purchase orders, CERS declarations, and payment receipts, retained for six years to satisfy ETA record-keeping requirements.

Canada has no mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control (CTC), or digital stamping regime. E-invoicing is voluntary under the Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Standard (PEPPOL BIS 3.0), supported through the CRA's e-invoice pilot and certified Canadian access points; there is no government pre-clearance of invoice data. Mandatory electronic filing applies to the GST/HST return itself (GST/HST NETFILE in XML format) for all registrants, with CRA-mandated XML schema versioning. On the customs side, the CBSA mandates: (a) eManifest pre-arrival transmission for commercial imports; (b) electronic export reporting through CERS for all exports valued at CAD 2,000 or more, filed via API/EDI before export; and (c) coordinated border data exchange with the CRA. Statutory invoice content under ETA section 223 must include the supplier's BN, customer business identity, date, description, HST rate and amount (or an explicit zero-rating statement); non-compliant invoices weaken export zero-rating evidence during audit.

CRA export-verification audits focus on documentation continuity. Absence of a through bill of lading or foreign customs entry exposes the LP to reassessment of 13% HST on the full invoice value, plus late-payment interest compounded daily. Specific exposures: (1) gross-negligence penalty of 25% of the tax assessed under ETA subsection 281.1 where zero-rating is claimed without reasonable care; (2) criminal offence under ETA section 280 for false or misleading statements — summary conviction fine up to CAD 25,000 and/or two years' imprisonment, or indictable fine up to CAD 100,000 and/or five years; (3) extended reassessment: the ordinary four-year limitation period under ETA subsection 298(1) does not apply where misrepresentation is attributable to neglect, wilful default, or fraud, allowing the CRA to assess at any time; (4) partner-level liability: the CRA may pursue the general partner and any limited partner who participates in management or accepts priority allocations for the partnership's unpaid GST/HST, with recharacterization of allocated income as Canadian-source business income; (5) cross-border penalties: the CBSA Penalties Program may impose administrative monetary penalties for inaccurate export declarations, while the CRA Voluntary Disclosures Program offers limited relief for wilful non-disclosure; and (6) retroactive clawback: denied zero-rating converts to 13% HST for the preceding years, with ITCs on related import flows unwound and 25% penalties imposed. The LP should maintain pre-export substantiation binders, engage a Canadian GST/HST practitioner for the annual T5013 and GST/HST reconciliation, and obtain a private CRA ruling before implementing the wholesale export flow.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a fully convertible currency regime under the Foreign Exchange and Other Transactions Regulations administered by the Bank of Canada. Both current account transactions and capital account movements, including profit remittances to foreign partners, are permitted without prior approval or quantitative restrictions. Profit repatriation faces no statutory hurdles beyond standard corporate governance requirements, beneficial ownership disclosure, and compliance with the Income Tax Act for tax reporting.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No mandatory surrender of export proceeds applies in Canada. Export receipts may be converted freely into Canadian dollars on the open market at prevailing spot rates. There are no statutory timelines, conversion ratios, or surrender obligations imposed by the Bank of Canada or the Department of Finance for legitimate trade transactions.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.50% combined federal and Ontario general corporate income tax rate, comprising 15.00% federal tax and 11.50% Ontario tax. A Canadian limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and does not itself pay corporate income tax; taxable income is allocated to its partners. An eligible Ontario-controlled private corporation may generally access a combined small-business rate of approximately 12.20% on qualifying active business income within the applicable limit.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25.00% Canadian Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident, subject to reduction under an applicable income tax treaty. An LP distribution is not automatically a dividend; its treatment follows the legal and tax character of the underlying amount and the recipient's status. A Canadian corporate partner may receive partnership income rather than a dividend, with subsequent distributions governed by the partner's own tax profile.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: For non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents, Canadian contemporaneous documentation is generally required under Income Tax Act section 247 unless the aggregate value of the transactions for the year does not exceed CAD 1,000,000 or another statutory exception applies. Canada does not impose a universal local-file/master-file threshold in the same form used by some jurisdictions. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to an ultimate parent or designated surrogate entity of a multinational enterprise group meeting the approximately EUR 750 million consolidated-revenue threshold, with Canadian filing and notification requirements where applicable.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no separate general Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to the UAE or certain other jurisdictions. Substance remains relevant under section 247 transfer-pricing rules, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule, treaty anti-abuse principles, beneficial-ownership analysis, partnership anti-avoidance rules, residency and permanent-establishment analysis, and judicial doctrines concerning sham transactions. The LP should maintain genuine Canadian commercial activity where claimed, including decision-making, personnel or outsourced operational capability, inventory and logistics records, banking control, customer and supplier contracts, risk assumption, capitalization, and evidence that the general partner performs its stated functions. Related-party purchases of frames, financing, licensing, distribution support, and management services require arm's-length pricing and contemporaneous evidence.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Reporting

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act section 239 can result in prosecution, fines generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, and imprisonment of up to two years. False statements and gross-negligence penalties can also apply administratively, commonly at 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit.

Director personal liability: An LP has general partners rather than directors as its primary management and liability actors. General partners, directors of corporate partners, officers, and other responsible persons can face personal exposure for criminal conduct, statutory withholding obligations, GST/HST amounts, civil restitution, and derivative or trust claims. Imprisonment is possible for criminal convictions. No automatic tax-related travel ban applies, but bail conditions, warrants, immigration consequences, or court orders can restrict travel.
Failure to Withhold or Remit Non-Resident Tax

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for the full amount that should have been withheld, together with arrears interest and late-remittance penalties. Repeated or serious failures can attract substantially increased penalties. Directors and other responsible officers may be personally liable for source deductions and certain remittance debts where statutory conditions are met.

Director personal liability: For an LP, the general partner and responsible signing officers are the principal risk persons. Corporate directors may face director-liability assessments for trust-like payroll and withholding amounts where the statutory due-diligence defence is unavailable. Criminal prosecution is possible for wilful non-compliance.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: The CRA may adjust income under Income Tax Act section 247, deny or recharacterize deductions, impose a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory conditions are met, and charge interest. Reassessments may extend the normal limitation period where relevant documentation is not maintained.

Director personal liability: Ordinary transfer-pricing adjustments normally create entity-level tax liability, not automatic director liability. Personal exposure can arise from knowing participation in fraud, false documents, obstruction, breach of fiduciary duty, or withholding and GST/HST failures.
Shell Company, Sham Partnership, or Treaty Conduit

Statutory sanction: The CRA may deny treaty benefits, apply the General Anti-Avoidance Rule, recharacterize transactions, deny deductions, impose withholding tax, assess penalties and interest, and challenge the existence or legal characterization of the partnership. Fraudulent implementation can lead to prosecution under the Income Tax Act or Criminal Code.

Director personal liability: General partners and controlling persons may face civil claims, restitution, trust remedies, oppression or fiduciary claims, and criminal liability where they knowingly implement sham transactions. There is no automatic travel ban, but criminal proceedings may produce arrest warrants, bail conditions, or other court restrictions.
Anti-Money-Laundering and Beneficial-Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: The Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act can impose administrative monetary penalties and criminal penalties, including significant fines and imprisonment, depending on the contravention. Criminal Code proceeds-of-crime, laundering, fraud, and sanctions offences may apply in addition to tax consequences.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, officers, compliance personnel, and beneficial owners can face personal criminal liability for knowing participation, counselling, possession, laundering, or obstruction. Conviction can result in imprisonment, forfeiture, restitution, substantial fines, and court-imposed travel restrictions. Corporate separateness does not protect a person from liability for personal criminal conduct.
Customs, Import Valuation, and GST/HST Fraud

Statutory sanction: The Canada Border Services Agency and CRA may assess duties, GST/HST, interest, civil penalties, seizure, forfeiture, administrative monetary penalties, and prosecution for intentional fraud or false declarations. Repeated non-compliance can jeopardize import privileges and tax registration.

Director personal liability: General partners and responsible officers may be personally exposed for knowingly false customs or tax declarations, obstruction, or statutory remittance debts. Criminal convictions can include fines, imprisonment, forfeiture, restitution, and court-imposed travel conditions.
Failure to Maintain Records or File Information Returns

Statutory sanction: Daily late-filing penalties, minimum and maximum information-return penalties, gross-negligence or false-statement penalties, failure-to-file assessments, audit reassessments, interest, and prosecution for obstruction or destruction of records may apply.

Director personal liability: Responsible general partners, directors, and officers may be assessed where legislation provides personal liability for remittances or where they personally participated in obstruction, false statements, or destruction of records. Corporate liability does not immunize individuals from their own statutory or criminal conduct.
