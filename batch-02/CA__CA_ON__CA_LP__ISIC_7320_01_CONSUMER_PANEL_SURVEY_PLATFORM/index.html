Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada — Business Registration Online at canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html; Ontario Ministry of Finance and Ontario Business Registry (ServiceOntario) for Ontario limited partnership registration and provincial tax account coordination.

E-government filing portal

The Ontario Limited Partnership must first register under the Ontario Limited Partnerships Act through the Ontario Business Registry. It then applies for a nine-digit Business Number (BN) via CRA Business Registration Online. The tax registration application must be made in the partnership's own name; CRA will issue the BN as a flow-through partnership with program accounts, including a GST/HST program account (RT) for HST collection, an Import/Export program account (RM) if goods are shipped outside Canada, and a Payroll program account (RP) if employees are engaged in Canada. No corporate tax account (RC) is required because the LP is tax-transparent for income tax purposes; individual partners report allocated income using the partnership BN. Although the LP is Canadian, non-resident partners must provide foreign entity tax identification numbers and treaty residency certificates to CRA for withholding and reporting purposes.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible; Territorial_Scope — the Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent for Canadian income tax: it is not a taxpayer, allocations flow through to resident and non-resident partners, and only Canadian-source partnership income is subject to Part I tax. For GST/HST purposes the partnership is a separate 'person' and must register as a registrant in its own right if it makes taxable supplies in Canada, even though income tax classification is pass-through.

Available election forms

Canada has no check-the-box election or optional entity classification equivalent to Subchapter K. The tax-transparent status of a limited partnership is mandatory under the Income Tax Act and the Excise Tax Act. No hybrid mismatch election is available to reclassify the partnership as taxable, although the allocation of income and losses among partners may be structured by the partnership agreement subject to commercial substance and Canadian reallocation rules. For GST/HST, the partnership may elect under certain related-party provisions only where all statutory conditions under the Excise Tax Act are met; there is no general election to zero-rate intra-partnership supplies.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in total taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies) made in a single calendar quarter or in the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters, as applicable to a Canadian partnership under subsection 240(1) of the Excise Tax Act. A commercial LP will typically register from inception because its taxable turnover is expected to exceed CAD 30,000. Non-resident digital-service suppliers and online platform operators may be required to register regardless of the CAD 30,000 threshold under Canada's simplified GST/HST rules for the digital economy.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Cross-border consumer polling, market research, and SaaS-based survey platform services supplied by the Ontario LP to non-resident recipients are zero-rated under Part V of Schedule V of the Excise Tax Act. The supply must be a service made to a non-resident person, must not be rendered to an individual physically present in Canada, must not relate to real property situated in Canada, and must not relate to tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time the service is performed. If the non-resident recipient is not registered for GST/HST, the service is generally zero-rated without requiring a separate export declaration. Where the non-resident recipient is a GST/HST registrant, the supply may still be zero-rated if it is not performed in Canada for, or received in Canada by, the non-resident registrant and no Canadian property nexus exists. The LP must obtain contemporaneous documentary evidence of the non-resident status of the client and the offshore nature of the service.

  • Form B13A Export Declaration (only if physical goods, survey kits, or tangible samples are shipped from Canada; not required for pure cross-border services or SaaS market research subscriptions)
  • Signed service agreement or statement of work identifying the non-resident client, services supplied, and absence of Canadian property nexus
  • Official evidence of non-resident status (foreign certificate of incorporation, outside-Canada business address, foreign tax identification number, or notarized non-resident declaration)
  • Commercial invoice clearly stating 'Zero-Rated Export Supply — Schedule V, Part V, Excise Tax Act'
  • Payment proof and confirmation of remittance from the non-resident client (wire transfer confirmation, SWIFT receipt, or inward remittance record)
  • Record of any panel participant compensations and proof that such payments do not constitute taxable supplies to individuals present in Canada

No real-time fiscal clearance, mandatory API reporting, or digital stamping is required for HST in Canada. The CRA has not mandated a national electronic invoicing clearance regime; e-invoices are acceptable if they are complete, legible, and retained for six years. Ontario does not impose a separate provincial invoice clearance mandate. The Pan-Canadian Peppol framework is voluntary for B2B electronic invoicing, and a Canadian registrant may use any reliable electronic invoicing platform. The CRA retains the right to require stamped or paper copies during an audit.

CRA may reassess within the normal four-year period, and is entitled to reassess beyond that period where misrepresentation, neglect, fraud, or concealment is established. If export zero-rating is not substantiated or is later denied, the LP is liable for the entire 13% HST from the date of the original invoice plus statutory interest and penalties. False claims for zero-rated export services attract CRA gross-negligence penalties, monetary fines under the Excise Tax Act for false statements or omissions, and potential criminal liability in cases of deliberate fraud. A retrospective denial also triggers recovery of the Ontario 8% provincial component because Ontario participates in the HST through the CRA-administered federal collection system. The 180-day documentary proof obligation requires all export evidence to be secured contemporaneously; failure to retain such evidence can lead to a full assessment, clawback of notional input tax credits claimed by downstream clients, and penalties for willful participation in a false refund claim.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full capital and current account convertibility for CAD under the International Monetary Fund Articles of Obligation. There are no statutory surrender requirements, no capital controls, and no restrictions on foreign exchange transactions. Profit remittances to non-residents are freely permitted, subject to Part XIII Canadian withholding tax, compliance with section 212 of the Income Tax Act (Canada), and general anti-avoidance considerations for non-arm's length payments. For a Canadian limited partnership with non-resident partners, allocations of income and distributions must be documented and may attract non-resident withholding tax on certain Canadian-source income. Transfer pricing documentation under section 247 of the Income Tax Act is required for cross-border intercompany financial and treasury arrangements.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no mandatory surrender or forced conversion timeline for export proceeds. Exporters may hold foreign currency receipts in foreign currency accounts domestically or offshore. Cross-border movements of funds exceeding CAD 10,000 must be reported to FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), and any suspicious transactions must be filed as Suspicious Transaction Reports (STRs). No exchange rate conversion of export revenues is required, but all books and records must be maintained in Canadian dollars for tax reporting using the Bank of Canada daily exchange rate or an acceptable spot rate.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as the central monetary authority; the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) is the designated financial intelligence unit and AML/ATF supervisor for cross-border reporting and foreign currency transaction oversight.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

PayPal Fit: High

Medium feasibility. Offshore electronic money institutions (EMIs) such as Wise and PayPal are operationally viable as settlement rails for low-value panel payouts and cross-border transfers, but they are not licensed as Canadian banks or authorized deposit-taking institutions. Fund safeguarding and last-mile clearing still require a Canadian-licensed payment service provider or a bank partner. Legal constraints include the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) registration regime administered by the Bank of Canada for payment service providers operating in Canada, the requirement for FINTRAC registration as a Money Services Business (MSB) for certain foreign exchange dealing and remitting activities, and the limitation that offshore EMIs generally cannot provide Canadian IBANs or clear direct debits in CAD. Treasury concentration risk must remain with tier-1 Canadian banks.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound investment into an Ontario limited partnership is handled through a bank-controlled escrow account with a code 'CA' account for receipt of foreign investments. Wire transfers use SWIFT MT103 or the Canadian Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) for domestic clearing, with the caller references 'LP CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION' and a Remittance Purpose Code of 'Corporate finance - capital contribution to a partnership' as referenced by the OECD' s FKT code framework. The receiving bank must verify the beneficial owner and source of funds; any wire above CAD 10,000 is subject to FINTRAC Large Value Transaction Reporting (LVTR). Non-resident contributions in CAD or foreign currency are permitted without exchange control restrictions; however, where the contribution is made from a jurisdiction with an anti-money-laundering concern, an enhanced due-diligence file including a legal opinion and account sign-off approval from the General Counsel is required.Trade settlements and disbursements are executed through SWIFT MT103 single-customer credit transfers for cross-border supplier and panel-payout settlements, and Commercial Letters of Credit (L/C) for documentary trade where goods are part of survey-fulfillment incentives or procurement. Domestic recurring payouts to panel participants are batched through Automated Clearing Settlement System (ACSS) direct deposits or real-time rails via Payments Canada. All outbound payments above CAD 10,000 or equivalent must be captured by FINTRAC reporting; bank references must include the beneficiary legal name, ISO country code, and treasury memo in alphanumeric format. Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

A Canadian limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and is not itself subject to federal or Ontario corporate income tax. Taxable income is allocated to partners. For a taxable Canadian corporation, the federal general rate is 15% and the Ontario general corporate rate is 11.5%, producing a combined general rate of 26.5%. A qualifying Ontario-controlled private corporation may generally access a combined small-business rate of approximately 12.2% on eligible active business income within the applicable business limit.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The standard Canadian Part XIII withholding tax rate on taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is 25%. A treaty commonly reduces the rate to 5% for qualifying corporate shareholders meeting ownership thresholds and to 15% for other qualifying treaty residents, subject to the applicable treaty. An LP distribution is generally not a dividend merely because cash is distributed; the Canadian tax treatment follows the underlying partnership income and capital characterization.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general monetary threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is automatically unnecessary. Under Income Tax Act section 247, taxpayers must make reasonable efforts to determine arm's-length terms and prepare contemporaneous documentation by the filing due date. T106 reporting generally applies where aggregate reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents exceed CAD 1,000,000 or another prescribed reporting condition is met. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to an ultimate parent entity or surrogate parent of an MNE group with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year. Canada does not impose a universal OECD-style Master File and Local File filing regime, but CRA may require detailed local-file-equivalent support during an audit.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no separate general Economic Substance Regulations comparable to the regimes in some offshore jurisdictions. Substance is nevertheless relevant under the Income Tax Act, the common-law sham and beneficial-ownership principles, treaty anti-abuse provisions, the principal-purpose test where applicable, and the general anti-avoidance rule in section 245. The CRA may challenge arrangements lacking commercial purpose, genuine functions, people, risk control, decision-making, assets, or economically coherent pricing. A limited partnership with non-resident partners must document genuine operations, partner rights, capital accounts, allocation methodology, Canadian activities, and the commercial rationale for related-party charges.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and fraudulent reporting

Statutory sanction: Conviction under the Income Tax Act may result in a fine ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment for up to two years, reassessment of tax, gross-negligence penalties, and interest. A false statement or omission made knowingly or under circumstances amounting to gross negligence can attract a penalty generally equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit.

Director personal liability: A corporate general partner, its directors, officers, and responsible individuals may face prosecution where personal involvement or authorization is proved. Directors may also face derivative liability for unremitted source deductions and GST/HST under applicable statutory provisions. A Canadian tax conviction can create significant immigration, entry, reputational, and commercial consequences, but imprisonment and travel restrictions are not automatic for every tax assessment.
Partnership misclassification and abusive allocation

Statutory sanction: CRA may recharacterize the arrangement, deny allocations or deductions, apply the general anti-avoidance rule, reassess partners, impose interest and penalties, and deny treaty benefits. A sham arrangement can be disregarded for tax purposes.

Director personal liability: Limited partners generally benefit from statutory limited liability if they respect the applicable partnership statute and do not act as general partners. The corporate general partner bears partnership obligations, and individuals may incur personal liability for fraud, knowing participation, fiduciary breaches, unpaid source deductions, GST/HST, or conduct outside the statutory liability shield.
Transfer-pricing non-compliance

Statutory sanction: The CRA may adjust income under section 247, deny deductions, impose a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the relevant adjustment where reasonable efforts were not made and the statutory conditions are met, and assess withholding tax, interest, and related penalties. Recharacterization may affect T106, GST/HST, and treaty positions.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers can face personal exposure where they knowingly approve false records, fraudulent transfer-pricing positions, or unremitted payroll, withholding, or GST/HST amounts. Ordinary transfer-pricing reassessment does not automatically pierce the corporate veil.
Failure to withhold and remit non-resident tax

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for the unremitted amount, generally together with a 10% penalty for failure to deduct or remit and a 20% penalty for repeated failures, plus arrears interest and information-return penalties. Treaty relief may be denied where residency or beneficial ownership evidence is inadequate.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally assessed for certain unremitted source deductions under the Income Tax Act where the corporation failed to remit and the statutory director-liability conditions are satisfied. Limited-partner status does not protect a person who actually controlled or authorized the withholding default.
GST/HST fraud or improper cross-border treatment

Statutory sanction: The CRA may deny credits, assess unremitted tax, apply gross-negligence or other penalties, charge interest, and prosecute tax fraud. Failure to remit collected GST/HST can be treated as a serious compliance breach.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate general partner may be jointly and severally liable for specified unremitted GST/HST where statutory due-diligence defenses fail. Fraudulent conduct may generate separate criminal liability.
Shell entity, sham residence, or treaty shopping

Statutory sanction: The CRA may deny treaty benefits, apply the principal-purpose test or general anti-avoidance rule, recharacterize income, assess withholding tax, and impose penalties and interest. Beneficial-ownership and residence assertions unsupported by facts are high-risk.

Director personal liability: Individuals involved in deliberate false residence, beneficial-ownership, or treaty certifications may face civil assessments, penalties, and criminal prosecution for fraud. Travel bans are not a routine Canadian tax sanction, although criminal proceedings can create court-imposed restrictions and broader immigration consequences.
AML, sanctions, and suspicious payment activity

Statutory sanction: The market-research LP is not automatically a FINTRAC reporting entity merely because it makes or receives cross-border payments. Obligations can arise if the business conducts a regulated money-services, financial, securities, accounting, or other listed activity. Violations can result in administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, seizure or forfeiture, and criminal prosecution under Canadian anti-money-laundering and sanctions legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, general partners, and persons directing transactions may incur personal criminal or regulatory exposure for knowing participation, proceeds-of-crime laundering, sanctions evasion, or obstruction. Travel restrictions may be imposed by a court in criminal proceedings, but are not an automatic consequence of an ordinary AML deficiency.
Record destruction and obstruction

Statutory sanction: The CRA may impose penalties, extend reassessment periods, draw adverse inferences, issue requirements for information, and pursue obstruction or tax-evasion charges where the conduct is deliberate.

Director personal liability: Individuals who direct destruction, concealment, or obstruction may be prosecuted personally. Corporate or limited-partner status does not shield deliberate obstruction or destruction of records.
