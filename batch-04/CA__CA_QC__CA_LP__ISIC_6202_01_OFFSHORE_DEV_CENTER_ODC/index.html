Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – Business Registration Online (BRO) portal, in coordination with Revenu Québec – Mon dossier d'entreprise portal and the Registraire des entreprises du Québec (REQ). The CRA administers the federal Business Number (BN), GST/HST, and Part I income tax; Revenu Québec administers the Quebec Sales Tax (QST) and Quebec corporate/partnership tax filings.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Register the limited partnership (LP) with the Registraire des entreprises du Québec to obtain the Quebec Enterprise Number (NEQ). Step 2: Request a federal Business Number (BN) from the CRA through BRO using the LP's legal name and NEQ; this BN operates as the primary tax identifier for federal purposes. Step 3: Register for GST/HST if the LP's worldwide taxable supplies exceed the CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold; the registration is in the partnership's name, not in the partners' names. Step 4: Register for QST with Revenu Québec; registration is mandatory where the LP is a GST/HST registrant and makes taxable supplies in Quebec. Step 5: Non-resident partners that are not otherwise required to have Canadian presence may apply for a CRA non-resident account using Form RC1293; the LP must still file partnership information returns identifying each partner. All applications require formation documents, registered office address, general partner details, and expected revenue projections.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible. For federal and Quebec income tax purposes, a limited partnership is fiscally transparent: income, gains, losses, and credits flow through to the partners according to the partnership agreement. Non-resident partners are taxable on Canadian-source business income allocations and are subject to Part XIII withholding on certain partnership distributions. For GST/HST and QST, however, the partnership is treated as a separate 'person' and, once registered, must account for tax on its taxable supplies in its own name.

Available election forms

Canada has no US-style check-the-box election. The principal available elections for a limited partnership are: (i) the partnership rollover election under subsection 97(2) of the Income Tax Act, allowing property to be transferred into the partnership at an agreed amount between cost and fair market value; (ii) a joint election under subsection 96(3) to have a partnership fiscal period deemed to end on a specified basis; and (iii) certain provincial QST/GST small-supplier elections. The hybrid mismatch rules in sections 18.1 to 18.4 of the Income Tax Act operate as mandatory anti-avoidance rules: where a foreign jurisdiction treats the Canadian LP as an opaque entity, any deduction claimed by a Canadian partner for an amount paid by the partnership may be denied to the extent there is no corresponding ordinary inclusion, and no elective opt-out is permitted.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual worldwide taxable supplies, measured over the last four consecutive calendar quarters. Once GST/HST-registered, the LP must also register for QST if it makes taxable supplies in Quebec, irrespective of the CAD 30,000 threshold. Zero-rated exports count toward the threshold but do not attract tax.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods are zero-rated if the exporter satisfies all documentary requirements proving the goods were exported from Canada as part of a commercial transaction. Services supplied by a Quebec LP to a non-resident client are zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act where the recipient is a non-resident and the service is not supplied to a person in Canada, is not performed in relation to Canadian real estate or tangible personal property, and is not part of a Canadian business carried on by the supplier in the ordinary course. Offshore software development and maintenance services delivered to a foreign parent or client generally qualify as zero-rated exports if they are not consumed by a Canadian resident and the contract clearly designates the non-resident recipient. The burden of proof is on the LP to demonstrate foreign nexus.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada Customs Export Declaration, CCED) or equivalent electronic export declaration (ED) for goods movement, if any
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical export of goods (not applicable for pure software services)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / bank proof of foreign currency receipt from the non-resident client
  • Signed offshore service agreement identifying the non-resident recipient, scope of work, and delivery location
  • Detailed commercial invoice in the prescribed GST/HST and QST form showing zero-rated status under Schedule VI
  • Technical delivery evidence (e.g., server logs, VPN access records, or acceptance reports) proving that services were rendered to offshore recipients and not consumed in Canada

No real-time fiscal clearance, API-based clearance, or digital stamping regime exists in Canada or Quebec. E-invoicing is permitted without prior authorization, but each GST/HST invoice must contain the prescribed information under subsection 223(1) of the Excise Tax Act (e.g., seller name, BN, date, description, amount, tax payable), and each QST invoice must meet parallel requirements under the Quebec Taxation Act. There is no statutory PEPPOL mandate, although CRA and Revenu Québec accept standardized electronic records for audit purposes.

CRA and Revenu Québec may reassess within four years of a taxation year in the case of ordinary review, and more than four years where misrepresentation, fraud, or gross negligence is established. A false or unsupported zero-rating claim triggers immediate re-assessment of uncollected GST/HST and QST, interest, and penalties. The Excise Tax Act provides for penalties of up to 25% of the tax amount plus a gross-negligence penalty of the greater of CAD 100 and 25% of the amount disallowed (sections 280.1 and 285.1). Revenu Québec imposes parallel penalties under the Quebec Taxation Act. Deliberate circular transactions, forged export documentation, or failure to secure foreign exchange remittance within the expected 180-day commercial cycle may be recharacterized as taxable domestic supplies and exposed to criminal prosecution under the income tax and excise acts.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is freely convertible for ordinary commercial and investment transactions. Canada generally permits unrestricted conversion between Canadian dollars and foreign currencies, and there is no general capital-account approval requirement for foreign investment, partnership contributions, dividend-equivalent distributions, loan repayments, or repatriation of lawful profits. A Quebec limited partnership should nevertheless maintain partnership agreements, contribution records, source-of-funds evidence, tax filings, transfer-pricing support where applicable, and banking records. Cross-border payments remain subject to sanctions screening, AML requirements, beneficial-ownership verification, withholding-tax rules, non-resident reporting, transfer-pricing rules, and the Canadian income-tax treatment of partnership allocations. Payments to non-residents may require withholding under the Income Tax Act, including the general 25% rate where no treaty reduction or statutory exemption applies; treaty eligibility and beneficial ownership must be documented. Foreign accounts and certain foreign assets may generate Canadian information-reporting obligations for Canadian residents and Canadian-controlled entities.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement, mandatory conversion ratio, or prescribed deadline requiring a Canadian exporter or software-services provider to convert foreign-currency receipts into Canadian dollars. Export proceeds may generally remain in foreign-currency accounts or be converted at the holder's discretion. The entity should retain contracts, invoices, proof of service delivery, remittance records, and accounting support, and should reconcile foreign-currency receipts under applicable Canadian accounting and tax rules. Financial institutions may request transaction purpose, counterparty, invoice, beneficial-owner, and source-of-funds information under AML, sanctions, and risk-based onboarding procedures.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada. Canada has no general exchange-control administration or mandatory foreign-exchange surrender authority. The Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions supervises federally regulated deposit-taking institutions, while the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada administers anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing requirements for reporting entities. Quebec financial-sector supervision may involve the Autorité des marchés financiers for provincially regulated financial activities.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Medium for supplemental collections and payouts; Low as a substitute for a primary Canadian operating bank. Wise Business, Payoneer, and comparable providers may support eligible Canadian businesses, subject to product availability, local entity verification, transaction limits, restricted-activity rules, and source-of-funds review. An offshore electronic-money institution generally cannot be assumed to provide a Canadian deposit account, Canadian deposit insurance, unrestricted CAD clearing, or a legally sufficient primary treasury relationship. Use of a foreign fintech does not eliminate Canadian AML, sanctions, tax, beneficial-ownership, books-and-records, or payment-services obligations. Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Route capital through the Canadian operating bank by wire in CAD or an accepted foreign currency, preferably from an account held in the name of the contributing partner or its verified parent. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

The Quebec limited partnership is generally fiscally transparent and is not subject to corporate income tax at the partnership level. A taxable Canadian corporation generally bears a combined federal and Quebec rate of 26.5% at the general rate, comprising 15% federal tax and 11.5% Quebec tax. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may access an approximately 12.2% combined small-business rate on eligible active business income, comprising 9% federal tax and 3.2% Quebec tax, subject to the applicable business-limit rules.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Canada's statutory Part XIII withholding tax rate on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is 25%. A treaty may reduce the rate, commonly to 5% for a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting ownership tests and 15% for other qualifying portfolio dividends. A Quebec limited partnership does not pay dividends in its own capacity; distributions generally retain their underlying tax characterization, while corporate partners may distribute after-tax profits as dividends.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general monetary threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is automatically unnecessary. Canadian taxpayers must make reasonable efforts to determine arm's-length terms and maintain documentation under Income Tax Act section 247. Form T106 generally applies to reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents where the aggregate transaction categories meet the statutory reporting threshold, commonly CAD 1,000,000. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to qualifying multinational enterprise groups with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year. Canada does not generally impose a separate universal Master File and Local File filing obligation using the OECD terminology, although equivalent supporting documentation is expected.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone regime called Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance is evaluated through transfer-pricing rules, the general anti-avoidance rule, beneficial-ownership principles, treaty anti-abuse rules, residence and central-management facts, specific anti-avoidance provisions, and judicial doctrines. An offshore development centre should maintain Canadian personnel, decision-making authority, technical supervision, premises or operational infrastructure, customer contracts, risk control, and contemporaneous evidence consistent with the functions, assets, and risks for which it earns remuneration.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Upon conviction under the Income Tax Act, a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment of up to five years, or both, in addition to the tax, interest, and civil penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face criminal prosecution where they personally participated, authorized, acquiesced in, or were wilfully blind to the conduct. Tax debts do not ordinarily create automatic personal liability, but source deductions, GST/HST, and certain other remittances can trigger director liability subject to statutory due-diligence defences. Civil restitution may be ordered in criminal proceedings. A general tax-debt travel ban is not an ordinary Canadian tax sanction.
Gross Negligence and Civil Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: The Income Tax Act generally imposes a penalty equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit attributable to the false statement or omission, plus tax and arrears interest.

Director personal liability: The penalty is ordinarily assessed against the taxpayer, but individuals may be assessed or prosecuted where they separately committed an offence or are liable for source deductions or indirect taxes. Professional discipline and director disqualification risks may arise from fraudulent conduct.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: The CRA may adjust income under Income Tax Act section 247, impose transfer-pricing penalties generally equal to 10% of the relevant adjustment where statutory conditions are met, and assess withholding, interest, and consequential penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure for knowingly participating in fraudulent arrangements or for unremitted source deductions. Ordinary transfer-pricing reassessments normally remain corporate or partnership-level liabilities.
Failure to Remit Non-Resident Withholding

Statutory sanction: The payer may be liable for the unwithheld tax, interest, and statutory failure-to-remit penalties. The payer generally cannot transfer the statutory withholding obligation to the recipient after payment.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for unremitted source deductions and certain withholding obligations if the corporation failed to remit and the statutory conditions are met, subject to the director's due-diligence defence and limitation periods.
GST/HST and Quebec QST Fraud or Non-Remittance

Statutory sanction: Assessments may include the tax, interest, gross-negligence penalties, false-statement penalties, and criminal fines or imprisonment for tax fraud. Civil penalties vary according to the return, conduct, and amount involved.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unremitted GST/HST and QST under the applicable statutes, subject to due diligence and statutory limitation rules. Criminal participation can result in imprisonment and asset-recovery orders.
Shell Company and Sham Substance

Statutory sanction: The CRA or Revenu Québec may deny deductions, treaty benefits, credits, or partnership allocations; apply the general anti-avoidance rule; reassess tax and interest; and impose gross-negligence or evasion penalties where conduct warrants.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly implement sham arrangements may face civil claims, professional sanctions, director liability for remittances, and criminal prosecution for fraud or tax evasion. Corporate separateness is not a defence to personal criminal conduct.
Anti-Money Laundering and Beneficial-Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: Consequences may include FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, transaction restrictions, seizure or forfeiture, criminal fraud or money-laundering charges, fines, and imprisonment of up to ten years for indictable money-laundering offences.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, beneficial owners, and controllers may be prosecuted personally. Conviction may result in imprisonment, forfeiture, restitution, and prohibition orders. Travel restrictions may arise from criminal bail or court orders, but are not an automatic consequence of an ordinary AML administrative penalty.
Records, Books, and Electronic Evidence Failure

Statutory sanction: The taxpayer may face record-keeping penalties, denied deductions or credits, adverse audit inferences, compliance orders, reassessments, interest, and criminal penalties for obstruction or destruction of evidence.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be prosecuted for obstruction or authorization of record destruction. Personal liability for the underlying tax generally requires a specific statutory basis or personal wrongdoing.
