Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital; capital is established by the partnership agreement and may be contributed in cash, property or services. For a non-financial data center colocation business, no escrow account is required; capital calls and contribution schedules are recorded in the partnership register.

Funding & unlock path: Fire safety certification requires compliance with the Québec Construction Code (Chapitre I, Bâtiment) and the National Fire Protection Association codes applicable to data centers (NFPA 75 and NFPA 110 for emergency generators), including an SIM pre-operational inspection of alarm, standpipe/sprinkler, and generator systems. Structural load inspections must be sealed by a Quebec professional engineer for raised access floors (minimum 8 kPa), battery containment, and rack loading zones. Occupational clearance requires CNESST workplace registration and, where employees are present, an up-to-date prevention program for electrical safety and confined-space entry in cooling and power rooms.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) for federal GST/HST and income tax; Revenu Québec for Quebec QST and provincial income tax matters. Portals: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services.html) and Revenu Québec CLIC (https://www.revenuquebec.ca/en/).

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity not resident in Canada must first determine whether it carries on business in Canada. To obtain a GST/HST registration number, it must file Form RC1 (Request for GST/HST Registration), either through the CRA’s Business Registration Online portal or by written application, providing legal entity documentation, proof of foreign registration, and the intended Canadian permanent establishment details. For Quebec, the parallel QST registration is made with Revenu Québec using Form LM-1-V (Registration Application for GST/HST and QST), which allows a combined registration for both federal GST/HST and Quebec QST. The entity must also obtain a Quebec Establishment Number (NEQ) if it has a physical presence in Quebec; otherwise, a non-resident may register directly for QST where a supply is made in Quebec in the course of a business carried on in Quebec. All registrations require a Canadian mailing address or a designated agent in Canada for service of documents.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a check-the-box classification regime. A limited partnership is a fiscally transparent person for income tax purposes; the partnership itself does not pay federal or Quebec income tax, but each partner, including a non-resident limited partner, is subject to Canadian tax on its share of partnership income attributable to a Canadian permanent establishment. For GST/HST and QST, a partnership is a separate ‘person’ and must register in its own name if its taxable supplies exceed the registration threshold; no formal pass-through election applies, but the partnership and its members may enter into joint and several liability arrangements for indirect taxes. There is no hybrid mismatch election mechanism available in Canada; hybrid mismatch rules apply compulsorily under Canadian domestic law and are not elective.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies (other than zero-rated supplies) worldwide within the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters or in a single calendar quarter in Canada; the same CAD 30,000 threshold applies separately for QST registration in Quebec. Once registered, a non-resident may claim input tax credits and input tax refunds for GST/QST paid on expenses attributable to its taxable supplies.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies to supplies of goods that are exported by the supplier, supplied to a non-resident recipient for use outside Canada, or supplied in the course of transporting goods to a destination outside Canada. For data center colocation services, the supply of server rack space, power, cooling, and related hosting services to a non-resident who uses the services partly or wholly outside Canada may be zero-rated only where the service is supplied to a non-resident and is not performed in whole or in part in Canada; colocation services actually rendered at the Quebec data center are generally subject to GST/HST and QST at the place of performance, and the exporter must obtain documentary evidence proving the service is performed outside Canada if zero-rating is claimed. For goods zero-rating, the supplier must retain evidence establishing that the goods were exported within a prescribed period and that the supplier did not make the goods available to the purchaser in Canada. Services that are ancillary to the supply of exported goods may also be zero-rated if the primary supply is zero-rated.

  • Canada Customs Invoice / Customs Export Declaration (Canada Border Services Agency – CSA approved form, eManifest or ACI export data)
  • Commercial invoice or service agreement indicating foreign destination and non-resident recipient
  • Bill of Lading (ocean, air, rail or truck waybill) showing final destination outside Canada
  • Proof of exportation issued by CBSA or a bonded carrier, including the customs export declaration number
  • CRA Form GST/HST Transfer or Foreign Recipient Certificate (if applicable, to support zero-rating of exported services or intangible property)
  • Bank transfer or wire record demonstrating payment by the non-resident recipient (not a mandatory condition for zero-rating, but used as supporting evidence)

Canada has no real-time fiscal clearance or mandatory digital stamping system. The CRA requires that registrants retain electronic records in a readable and accessible format, and GST/HST invoices must contain specified particulars (supplier name, registration number, date, description, amount and tax charged). Quebec has progressively mandatory electronic invoicing for large enterprises and public-sector entities under Revenu Québec’s electronic invoicing initiative; however, this mandate is being phased and does not require an API-based transaction clearance or government pre-approval of invoices. A registered person may need to use a Revenu Québec-certified e-invoicing service provider when required.

The CRA and Revenu Québec may audit an exporter’s books for six years from the end of the reporting period. If zero-rated exports are claimed without proper supporting documentation, the tax authorities may reassess the supply as taxable, requiring GST/QST to be remitted on the full sale price with penalties and arrears interest, and may claw back any input tax credits claimed in connection with the supply. Willful participation in a false export claim can lead to penalties under the Excise Tax Act (e.g., subsection 285.1) of up to $25,000 plus fines up to $100,000, or a percentage of the tax evaded, and the Tax Court of Canada may impose a gross negligence penalty not exceeding 50% of the tax evaded. Criminal prosecution is available for fraudulent export schemes under section 380 of the Criminal Code, and the Quebec tax authority may impose separate penalties under the Tax Administration Act (CQLR c. A-6.002).

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility of the Canadian dollar on both current-account and capital-account transactions. There are no non-resident conversion restrictions, no prior-authorization hurdles, and no blocked-funds mechanism for profit remittances, LP distribution remittances, loan repayments, or repatriation of contributed capital. The practical frictions for the Quebec LP are: (i) FINTRAC electronic-funds-transfer reporting for transfers at or above CAD 10,000; (ii) Canadian tax exposure of non-resident limited partners on Canadian-source business income allocations through the partnership (Part I of the Income Tax Act) and Part XIII withholding on treaty-relevant amounts such as interest, rents, and royalties, with rates normally reduced under the Canada-US treaty for US partners; (iii) the Investment Canada Act notification or review obligation where non-Canadian partners establish or acquire control of a Canadian data-center business; and (iv) settlement and pricing discipline, because the CAD/USD market is offshore and deep, and the LP should transact FX only with a regulated Canadian dealer or a CLS-participant bank to ensure compliant execution and settlement.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canadian law imposes no statutory repatriation timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no surrender requirement for export proceeds to the central bank or any government authority. Export receipts in USD or other currencies may be held indefinitely in foreign-currency operating accounts at a Canadian Schedule I bank. For income-tax purposes, every settlement of foreign currency must be translated into CAD under section 39(1) of the Income Tax Act, and the LP should adopt and document a consistent translation policy (Bank of Canada noon rate or contractual spot rate) to support auditable FX gain or loss computation. Cross-border revenue from colocation rack-space licensing, bare-metal hosting, and managed services is generally zero-rated for GST/HST when supplied to a non-resident and not connected to real property situated in Quebec; to the extent a rack or cage arrangement is recharacterized as a right over Quebec real property, GST at 5% and QST at 9.975% may apply to that portion of the supply.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as the central bank and sole issuing authority for bank notes; prudential regulation and supervision of Schedule I chartered banks by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); anti-money-laundering and terrorist-financing supervision by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC); cross-border currency and trade declaration reporting by the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA). Canada has no dedicated foreign-exchange-control agency because the Foreign Exchange Control Act was repealed in 1951 and the Canadian dollar is fully free-floating.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: MEDIUM. Offshore digital EMIs (Wise, Airwallex, Currencycloud, and similar) are viable as supplementary collection and disbursement rails and for multicurrency treasury float, but they cannot replace a Schedule I operating account or the capital escrow account of the Quebec LP. Legal constraints: (i) an EMI providing payment services to Canadian residents is generally required to register as a money services business (MSB) with FINTRAC and to file suspicious-transaction and large-EFT reports; (ii) retail payment functions performed in Canada trigger Bank of Canada registration under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA), which came into force in 2024, and most offshore EMIs access the Canadian market through agent arrangements with regulated processors; (iii) CAD-denominated settlement ultimately clears through Payments Canada (Lynx or ACSS) or through correspondent banks, and an unregulated EMI cannot obtain direct clearing membership; and (iv) the LP remains primarily responsible under PCMLTFA for the accuracy of beneficial-ownership and transaction information furnished to the EMI, and the EMI's foreign license (for example, FCA authorization in the United Kingdom) must be evidenced, monitored, and reverified periodically.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound partner capital injections should be executed by SWIFT MT103 customer credit transfers in the partner's home currency or by Payments Canada ACSS or Lynx domestic EFT where the remitter holds a Canadian account. Each transfer must state a clear purpose-of-payment description in SWIFT field 70 or the bank's EFT reference field, such as CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION - CA_LP or CAPITAL CALL - DATA CENTER, and the receiving bank will map the transaction to the applicable Bank of Canada balance-of-payments classification code for an inward direct investment. The receiving bank files a FINTRAC large electronic-funds-transfer report for inflows at or above CAD 10,000, so the LP must concurrently provide the capital-call notice, the partner-register excerpt, and the remitter's bank statement to evidence source of funds. Where a non-Canadian partner makes an inward direct investment through the LP, the Investment Canada Act requires a notification within 30 days after the establishment of a new Canadian business or the filing of a notification or review application before implementation where an existing Canadian business is acquired, and the escrow release schedule should be conditioned on compliance with that filing. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: MEDIUM. Offshore digital EMIs (Wise, Airwallex, Currencycloud, and similar) are viable as supplementary collection and disbursement rails and for multicurrency treasury float, but they cannot replace a Schedule I operating account or the capital escrow account of the Quebec LP. Legal constraints: (i) an EMI providing payment services to Canadian residents is generally required to register as a money services business (MSB) with FINTRAC and to file suspicious-transaction and large-EFT reports; (ii) retail payment functions performed in Canada trigger Bank of Canada registration under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA), which came into force in 2024, and most offshore EMIs access the Canadian market through agent arrangements with regulated processors; (iii) CAD-denominated settlement ultimately clears through Payments Canada (Lynx or ACSS) or through correspondent banks, and an unregulated EMI cannot obtain direct clearing membership; and (iv) the LP remains primarily responsible under PCMLTFA for the accuracy of beneficial-ownership and transaction information furnished to the EMI, and the EMI's foreign license (for example, FCA authorization in the United Kingdom) must be evidenced, monitored, and reverified periodically.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined general corporate rate (15% federal Part I + 11.5% Quebec provincial; the LP itself is fiscally transparent and this rate applies to corporate general partners on their allocable share)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% statutory Part XIII rate (ITA 212(2)) on Canadian-source dividends; for the LP, distribution of profits to a non-resident limited partner is NOT a dividend subject to Part XIII — it is a flow-through allocation of Canadian business income reportable on the partner's Part I return. Where a corporate partner later declares a dividend from Canadian earnings, the 25% (or treaty-reduced) rate applies.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: T106 (Information Return of Non-Arm's Length Transactions with Non-Residents) must be filed when the aggregate value of transactions with a non-resident related party exceeds CAD 50,000 in a reporting period. CRA contemporaneous TP documentation (master/local file-style) is administratively required for 'large' taxpayers (gross revenue over CAD 50M and non-arm's-length cross-border transactions over CAD 5M); all taxpayers must maintain reasonable records under ITA 247(4).

Economic substance scope: Canada has no Economic Substance Regulations in the BVI/offshore sense. Substance is nevertheless central: (i) GAAR (ITA 245) and Quebec's anti-avoidance provisions can recharacterize a Quebec LP that lacks commercial substance; (ii) treaty relief requires the non-resident partner to be the beneficial owner with a real Canadian PE (attribution of profits under OECD Article 7); (iii) a 'shell' LP with no premises, employees, or business activity in Quebec is exposed to reassessment of income, denial of treaty benefits, and penalties.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (Income Tax Act s. 239 / Quebec Tax Administration Act)

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution: fine of 50%-200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years (ITA 239(1)); Quebec: minimum fine of 50% and up to 200% plus imprisonment; CRA 'Diligence' program referrals to the Public Prosecution Service can freeze assets and require an audit.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers are jointly and severally liable under ITA 227.1 for unremitted withholdings (payroll, Part XIII); where fraud is proven, the corporate veil is pierced, exposing personal assets; travel is restricted (CRA can obtain a court order and the Canada Border Services Agency detains at the border for outstanding warrants; serious evasion draws criminal travel bans and extradition risk).
Unfiled Partnership / Information Returns & Repeat Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Escalating civil penalties: ITA 162(7) CAD 25-250/day per partner; ITA 247(3) transfer-pricing penalty of 10% of the net adjustment where the adjustment exceeds the greater of CAD 5M or 10% of gross revenue; Quebec civil penalties under s. 56 of the Tax Administration Act; interest accrues on all balances.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate general partner can be held liable for the LP's unremitted withholdings and penalties; a 'failure to file' can result in Revenu Quebec issuing a certificate of prosecution, leading to a travel ban against the responsible partner and seizure of personal holdings.
Shell Company / GAAR Recharacterization (ITA 245; Quebec General Anti-Avoidance Rule)

Statutory sanction: The CRA/Revenu Quebec disregards the partnership, reassesses on a 'look-through' basis, denies the tax holiday and treaty reductions, imposes gross-negligence penalties of 50% of the understated tax (ITA 163(2)), and charges interest from the original filing date.

Director personal liability: Promoters and directors may be named as 'planners' under GAAR with third-party liability; courts have lifted the veil to impose restitution on shareholders who stripped the LP's assets; serious cases carry criminal fraud charges under the Criminal Code (s. 380) with imprisonment up to 14 years and travel bans.
AML / FINTRAC Non-Compliance (PCMLTFA)

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 500,000 for entities and CAD 100,000 for individuals per violation; CRA can freeze accounts linked to unreported virtual-currency proceeds; criminal proceedings for knowingly dealing with proceeds of crime under Criminal Code s. 462.31.

Director personal liability: Directors and compliance officers are personally liable for AML failures (PCMLTFA s. 74.1); violations can result in Canadian travel bans for foreign directors, denial of visa applications, and extradition for predicate offences; the corporate veil is pierced where the LP is a facade for money laundering.
