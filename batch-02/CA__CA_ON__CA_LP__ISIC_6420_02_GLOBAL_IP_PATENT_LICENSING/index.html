Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); federal GST/HST and income tax administration. Ontario provincial corporate tax is administered by the CRA under the Ontario-CRA Tax Administration Agreement. Self-service portal: CRA My Business Account at canada.ca (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-businesses/business-account.html).

E-government filing portal

A qualifying Ontario limited partnership that makes taxable supplies in Canada or is a non-resident earning Canadian-source income must obtain a CRA Business Number (BN). Procedure: (1) ensure the LP is duly registered under the Ontario Limited Partnerships Act (ServiceOntario). (2) Apply for a BN and GST/HST account through CRA Business Registration Online, or file Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) by mail/fax; a non-resident LP must provide its foreign registration certificate and appoint a Canadian agent/representative. (3) Register the GST/HST account if mandatory (or elect voluntary registration to claim input tax credits on Canadian operating costs and to zero-rate exported royalty supplies). (4) The CRA may require security for a non-resident registrant under ETA s. 299. (5) Upon approval, the LP receives a BN plus a GST/HST account identifier (format BN + 'RC0001') and must file T5013 partnership information returns where it carries on business in Canada; each partner reports partnership income under ITA s. 96.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada has no domestic 'check-the-box' election; classification is juristic. The Ontario LP is statutorily fiscally transparent for income tax (ITA s. 96), and the partners are directly chargeable. Available elections and hybrid considerations: (a) partnership fiscal-period election under ITA s. 96(2); (b) the SIFT partnership rules under Part IX.1 tax publicly traded LPs at the entity level, with an election for a SIFT wind-up entity in limited restructurings; (c) foreign partners may make a US check-the-box election on IRS Form 8832, effective only for foreign law, and the Canada-US Tax Convention fiscally transparent entity provision applies; (d) GST/HST related-party election for supplies among qualifying closely related registrants where applicable; (e) ETA security deposit procedures for non-resident registrants.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies made in Canada over the trailing four calendar quarters (or exceeding CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter) before mandatory registration; a non-resident making only zero-rated exported supplies (e.g., off-shore patent rights) is not required to register, though voluntary registration is generally advisable to claim input tax credits.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A Canadian GST/HST registrant may zero-rate exported goods under ETA Schedule VI, Part V, s. 5 (goods exported by the supplier) and may zero-rate supplies of intangible personal property (patent, trademark, know-how licensing and sub-licensing) made to non-resident licensees under Schedule VI, Part V, s. 10 where the IP right is granted for use exclusively outside Canada. Services supplied to non-residents are zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V, s. 7 unless they are personal services performed in Canada or relate to Canadian land/goods. For the Ontarian IP HoldCo LP, the supply of the right to use the patent and the related licensing services will qualify for 0% HST if the licensee is a non-resident, the contractual territory and commercial use are outside Canada, and the licence does not confer a right to use the IP in Canada.

  • Customs Export Declaration / Canada Border Services Agency export report via the Canadian Export Reporting System (for physical goods only, not for pure IP royalties)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill (for physical goods only; replaceable by e-certificates of export for cross-border services)
  • Fully executed global patent/trademark licence and sub-licence agreements designating the non-resident territory
  • Royalty invoices describing the zero-rated supply, the BN, date, and HST treatment
  • Certificate of non-resident status of the licensee (foreign tax identification number, certificate of incorporation or equivalent)
  • SWIFT MT103 inward remittance certificate and bank statements evidencing receipt of foreign royalty consideration
  • T106 Information Return (non-arm's length transactions) and executed intercompany documentation where the licensee is related
  • Legal opinion or IP registration extracts verifying the Canadian ownership and foreign enforcement scope of the licensed patent/trademark

Canada does not currently impose real-time e-invoicing clearance, digital stamping, or continuous transaction controls for B2B GST/HST supplies. The CRA mandates electronic GST/HST filing (GST/HST NETFILE, CRA web services/XML) and electronic remittance where annual taxable supplies exceed CAD 1.5 million. The Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing framework adopts the Peppol network for government procurement through the federal Vendor Invoice Management Program (phased participation by Ontario); e-invoicing is voluntary for commercial B2B. Taxable invoices must state the vendor BN, supply date, HST charged, and terms; no API stamping is required for private B2B invoices.

The CRA may disallow zero-rating and assess 13% HST plus interest and penalties if export documentation is deficient. The standard GST/HST reassessment window is four years under ETA s. 298, with no time limit in cases of fraud, wilful misrepresentation, or neglect (ETA s. 298(4); ITA s. 152(4) analog). Civil penalties for false or grossly negligent claims may be as high as 50% of the additional tax assessed, together with interest and criminal prosecution under the Criminal Code where zero-rating is fraudulently claimed. Under ETA s. 272, partners of a partnership are jointly and severally liable for the LP's unremitted HST; the CRA may require a security deposit from non-resident registrants and can claw back paid input tax credits and refunds where zero-rating evidence is not contemporaneous. For income tax, the CRA can reassess beyond the normal three-year limitation period if a T106 for the related-party royalty was not filed, and transfer-pricing penalties of 10% of the adjusted taxable income may apply. There is no specific statutory deadline for currency repatriation; the 180-day benchmark is customary commercial tenor, while income must be reported in CAD and the CRA may insist on contemporaneous transfer-pricing and financial evidence if repatriation is deferred.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a fully convertible and liberalized currency regime under IMF Article VIII. Both current-account and capital-account transactions are generally unrestricted and require no exchange-control approval. Profit remittances, royalties, dividends, loan repayments, and capital repatriations may be made freely in any convertible currency, subject only to income tax withholding requirements under the Income Tax Act and standard anti-money-laundering verification at the distributing bank.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory obligation to surrender export proceeds to the central bank or any official agency. Exporters and licensing entities may hold foreign-exchange receipts in domestic or offshore accounts, with reporting obligations limited to tax filings (T1134/T1135), FATCA, and CRS disclosure where applicable.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada; foreign exchange control regime administered federally under the Currency Act and the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA), with supervisory oversight by FINTRAC and OSFI.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Medium-to-High for cross-border payment routing and intercompany royalty settlements, but Low for regulatory 'bank-like' deposit-taking. Legal constraints: any non-bank entity seeking to offer payment services in Canada must comply with the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) and register with FINTRAC as a Money Services Business (MSB); it cannot accept deposits in the regulated banking sense under the Bank Act. An Ontario LP engaged in global IP licensing may use a licensed offshore EMI as a payment-clearing utility, but should maintain a primary Tier-1 Canadian bank account for capital injections, legal capital, and shareholder distributions to satisfy corporate substance and KYC expectations.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant wire transfer protocols require the originating entity to send a SWIFT MT103 to the Ontario LP's Tier-1 bank account, clearly indicating the beneficiary LP name, account number, and ABA/SWIFT/BIC identifier. For Bank of Canada international-transactions reporting, the remitter and beneficiary should classify the payment in the wire field as 'Intercompany equity capital contribution' or 'Direct investment injection' (capital-account category) and include the underlying LP agreement reference and IP acquisition purpose. The receiving bank may require identification of the ultimate source of funds and standard certification of beneficial ownership; no exchange-control authorization is required.Clearing rails for trade settlements include SWIFT MT103 wire transfers for direct intercompany royalty payments (field 70/77B should reference the royalty invoice and licensing agreement), Commercial Letters of Credit (SWIFT MT700/MT707) for documentary trade settlement, and SWIFT Global Payments Innovation (GPI) for same-day tracking and settlement. For US-dollar disbursements, the Canadian bank will route through correspondent banks using CHIPS or Fedwire; for other major currencies, the bank will use local clearing systems (e.g., TARGET2 for EUR, CHAPS for GBP) via SWIFT. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined federal-provincial general corporate rate for Canadian resident corporations in Ontario (federal 15% + Ontario general rate 11.5%). The Ontario Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent; its income is allocated to partners and taxed at the partner level unless a partner is itself a corporation or the LP is subject to specific deemed-corporation or anti-avoidance rules.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% (Part XIII statutory rate for dividends paid by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident). For the Ontario LP itself, distributions to partners are not dividends; the tax character of the partner’s allocated income is preserved. Non-resident partners are therefore taxed under Part I on Canadian-source business income or under Part XIII on gross passive income, as applicable.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T5013 return must be filed no later than five months after the partnership’s fiscal period-end. A calendar-year Ontario LP with a December 31 year-end must file by May 31 of the following calendar year.

Deadline: · Frequency:

T106 must be filed by the same deadline as the related T5013 or corporate T2 return, generally five months after the fiscal period-end for a partnership and six months after year-end for a corporate partner.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: No statutory monetary threshold mandates a Local File or Master File in Canada; however, Form T106 must be filed when the total value of reportable non-arm’s length transactions with non-residents exceeds CAD 1,000,000. CRA expects contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation reflecting OECD principles for all material controlled transactions involving the Ontario LP.

Economic substance scope: Canada has not enacted Caribbean-style Economic Substance Regulations. Nevertheless, the CRA will apply the General Anti-Avoidance Rule under s.245 and the jurisprudence on sham, beneficial ownership, and substance-over-form. An Ontario IP HoldCo that lacks personnel, premises, decision-making functions, and real economic risk in the licensing activities is vulnerable to reassessment and treaty-benefit denial.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Prosecution under ITA s.239 carries fines of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to five years; CRA may also apply civil penalties and gross-negligence penalties of 50% of the understated tax.

Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, and controlling officers who authorize or participate in false filings can be criminally liable. Civil veil-piercing may attach where the LP is found to be a mere sham or alter ego, exposing individual wealth to collection.
Transfer Pricing Noncompliance and Missing Documentation

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess royalty income on an arm’s length basis, deny Part XIII treaty relief, and impose a s.247(3) penalty equal to 10% of the transfer-pricing adjustment where documentation is absent and the adjustment threshold is met.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate general partner can be assessed for corporate penalties only in limited remittance contexts; however, individuals who knowingly sign false transfer-pricing declarations may be exposed to gross-negligence and criminal sanctions.
Non-Resident Withholding Tax Failure

Statutory sanction: The withholding agent is liable for the full withholding amount plus interest. Failure-to-remit penalties are 10% of the unremitted amount for a first offence and 20% for a repeat offence under ITA s.227(8).

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner or any person responsible for remittances may be jointly and severally liable under s.227.1 for unremitted Part XIII tax. CRA may issue Director’s Liability assessments, register civil judgments, and obtain travel-restriction orders where departure from Canada would frustrate collection.
GAAR, Sham Transactions, and Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: The CRA may deny the tax benefit under s.245(2), reassess beyond the normal limitation period in cases of misrepresentation, and impose a GAAR penalty under s.245(4) of 25% of the tax benefit where the arrangement is not clearly tax-neutral.

Director personal liability: Courts may pierce the corporate/partnership veil where the LP is an artificial intermediary, making general partners personally liable for resulting taxes and penalties; responsible officers may also be named as defendants in injunction proceedings.
AML and Beneficial Ownership Non-Disclosure

Statutory sanction: Administrative penalties under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act can reach CAD 500,000 for corporate non-compliance; criminal money laundering carries imprisonment of up to 10 years. Ontario corporate and partnership registries require current beneficial ownership disclosure and impose sanctions for false declarations.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners who knowingly conceal beneficial ownership risk personal criminal liability, civil penalties, disqualification orders, and seizure of personal assets where the entity structure is used to launder proceeds.
