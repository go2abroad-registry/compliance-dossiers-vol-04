Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), including Business Registration Online and My Business Account; Revenu Quebec administers Quebec sales tax (QST) and related provincial tax registrations through clicSÉQUR and its business portal.

E-government filing portal

A Canadian corporation obtains a nine-digit Business Number (BN) from the CRA through Business Registration Online, telephone registration, or incorporation-related registration channels. The corporation must open a GST/HST program account (RT) when required or voluntarily elected. A Quebec registrant generally must separately register for QST with Revenu Quebec and obtain a QST identification number. A non-resident entity carrying on business in Canada, making taxable supplies in Canada, importing commercial goods, or becoming subject to specified digital-economy rules must obtain the applicable CRA BN and GST/HST account; it may also require QST registration where Quebec registration rules apply. Registration generally requires legal formation documents, registered office and business address, director and ownership information, business activity details, expected revenues, fiscal year-end, and authorized representative information. Non-residents may be required to provide additional evidence of commercial activities and Canadian tax nexus.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canadian corporations are generally taxed as separate legal entities and have no United States-style check-the-box election. Relevant Canadian elections include corporate tax elections for transactions such as tax-deferred transfers under Income Tax Act section 85, reorganizations, capital-dividend treatment, fiscal periods, and affiliated transactions. A corporation may qualify as a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) or another prescribed corporate category based on ownership and control facts, but classification is determined by statute rather than an elective entity-classification form. Canada has enacted anti-hybrid mismatch rules that can deny deductions, inclusion benefits, or treaty outcomes; there is no general election that converts a Canadian corporation into a fiscally transparent entity for Canadian tax purposes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, excluding zero-rated supplies for certain threshold calculations, in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters generally triggers GST/HST registration under the small-supplier rules. Separate QST registration generally applies at a CAD 30,000 threshold for taxable supplies made in Quebec, subject to special rules. Non-resident suppliers of digital services, including electronically supplied services and certain platform supplies, may be required to register under Canada's specified non-resident digital-economy regime when the applicable CAD 30,000 threshold is exceeded. Voluntary registration is generally available before the threshold is reached.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Canada may generally be zero-rated for GST/HST where the statutory export conditions are satisfied and the supplier retains evidence that the goods were exported. Services supplied to a non-resident may be zero-rated where the relevant place-of-supply and zero-rating provisions apply, including qualifying services performed for or in respect of a non-resident outside Canada. Electronically supplied AI inference or API access must be analyzed under the specific GST/HST and QST place-of-supply rules, customer-status requirements, performance-location rules, and statutory exclusions. Zero-rating is not automatic merely because the customer pays from abroad or the contract is denominated in foreign currency. The supplier must verify customer identity and non-resident status, document the contractual recipient, establish the nature and location of the supply, and retain evidence supporting the applicable zero-rating provision. Services connected with Canadian real property, tangible personal property, events, or other statutory exclusions may not qualify.

  • Commercial invoice identifying the supplier, purchaser or consignee, description, value, currency, and export destination
  • Bill of lading, airway bill, courier record, or equivalent transport documentation
  • Packing list and export permit or certificate where required for controlled or regulated goods

Canada and Quebec do not generally impose a nationwide real-time invoice-clearance, mandatory tax-device stamping, or continuous transaction-reporting requirement for ordinary corporations. Electronic invoices are generally permitted if they contain the prescribed information and can be retained and reproduced in an accessible form. GST/HST and QST registrants must issue compliant invoices or receipts, maintain books and records, and file electronically where required by applicable filing rules or administrative directions. CRA and Revenu Quebec may require electronic filing, but there is no general obligation for an AI API provider to transmit each invoice through a government clearance API before issuance.

Canada has no general mandatory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for export proceeds. A zero-rating claim may be reassessed if the supplier cannot prove exportation, non-resident status, the applicable statutory provision, or the required place of supply. The reassessment can recover under-collected GST/HST or QST, applicable interest, and civil penalties. Penalties may apply for false statements, gross negligence, repeated non-compliance, late registration, late filing, failure to remit, or failure to maintain adequate records. Fraudulent or knowingly false claims may attract higher penalties and prosecution under the Excise Tax Act, the Quebec Tax Administration Act, customs legislation, or other applicable laws. CRA and Revenu Quebec may generally reassess within the ordinary limitation period, commonly four years for a corporation, with extended or unlimited periods where there is misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, willful blindness, fraud, or waiver. Improper input-tax-credit or export-refund claims may be clawed back together with interest and penalties. Customs violations may additionally trigger Administrative Monetary Penalty System sanctions, seizure, or other enforcement measures.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible on both current and capital accounts. There are no exchange control permits required for inbound or outbound remittances, including dividend distributions and profit repatriation. Withholding taxes under the Income Tax Act apply to certain cross-border payments (e.g., dividends, royalties, interest) but do not restrict convertibility. Businesses must comply with record-keeping and the $10,000 cross-border transaction reporting rule to FINTRAC for cash or monetary instruments; electronic wire transfers are also subject to FINTRAC reporting thresholds.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory timeline or mandatory conversion ratio for export proceeds. Exporters may hold, freely convert, or repatriate export earnings without regulatory restriction. All settlement must be recorded for tax purposes under the Income Tax Act and the Excise Tax Act, and any FX gains/losses are taxable/treatable as ordinary income or capital as applicable.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (foreign exchange oversight shared with the Department of Finance Canada; AML/FX transaction reporting enforced by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada – FINTRAC, created under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act – PCMLTFA)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Offshore digital Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) have no standalone licensing recognition in Canada. They are treated as non-bank money service businesses (MSBs) and must register with FINTRAC with a Canadian program coordinator, maintain a physical presence in Canada, and adhere to the Retail Payment Activities Act (federal supervision by the Bank of Canada) if providing payment services exclusively. Feasibility is Low-Medium for a fully offshore EMI structure; the typical operational path is to partner with a Canadian Schedule I bank for settlement rails and custody of client funds, while the EMI acts only as a payment technology gateway.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined general corporate rate in Quebec, comprising 15% federal tax and 11.5% Quebec tax. Eligible Canadian-controlled private corporations may generally obtain a combined rate of approximately 12.2% on qualifying active business income within the applicable CAD 500,000 business limit, comprising 9% federal tax and 3.2% Quebec tax, subject to business-limit reductions and eligibility restrictions.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Canadian Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident, subject to a reduced rate under an applicable tax treaty. Under the Canada-United States treaty, the rate is generally 5% where the beneficial owner is a company that owns at least 10% of the voting stock, and 15% in other ordinary cases. Treaty rates may differ for other jurisdictions.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due six months after the end of every taxation year. Any balance is generally due two months after year-end. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally obtain a three-month balance-payment deadline if it meets the statutory conditions, including the applicable taxable-income and association requirements. Instalments are due monthly or, where permitted, quarterly.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Monthly instalments are generally due on the last day of each month. Quarterly instalments may be available to qualifying corporations and are generally due on the last day of each fiscal quarter. Instalment requirements are based on estimated current-year, prior-year, or second-prior-year tax under the applicable statutory method.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not impose a single general CAD threshold below which section 247 contemporaneous documentation is categorically unnecessary. A taxpayer must maintain reasonable contemporaneous documentation for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions and provide it within three months of a CRA request. T106 reporting generally applies where the aggregate reportable transactions with a non-arm's-length non-resident exceed CAD 1,000,000 for the year, subject to the statutory rules. A mandatory country-by-country report generally applies to a multinational enterprise group with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million, or the applicable Canadian-dollar equivalent. Canada does not generally impose a separate statutory Master File filing requirement for every taxpayer, but OECD-style master-file information may be required to support the arm's-length analysis.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a separate general Economic Substance Regulation regime equivalent to the regimes of certain offshore jurisdictions. Economic substance remains relevant through the general anti-avoidance rule, sham and legal-characterization principles, beneficial-ownership analysis, treaty principal-purpose and anti-treaty-shopping rules, specified foreign tax-credit rules, interest limitation rules, thin-capitalization rules, foreign-affiliate rules, and the requirement that transfer-pricing arrangements reflect functions performed, assets used, and risks assumed. A Canadian AI API company should maintain Canadian decision-making, personnel, technical functions, contractual authority, operational records, intellectual-property rights, and risk control consistent with the profits attributed to Canada.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Reporting

Statutory sanction: Criminal tax evasion may result in fines of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to five years under the Income Tax Act. Civil reassessments, interest, gross-negligence penalties, and denial of deductions may apply independently.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorize, assent to, or participate in the conduct may face personal criminal exposure, director-liability assessments for certain source deductions, restitution orders, and asset-recovery proceedings. Travel restrictions are not an automatic tax penalty but may follow criminal proceedings, bail conditions, or enforcement orders.
Failure to File or Late Filing

Statutory sanction: Late-filing penalties, information-return penalties, arrears interest, instalment penalties, and repeated-failure penalties may apply. CRA or Revenu Québec may issue a demand to file and assess tax based on available information.

Director personal liability: Directors may become personally liable for unremitted source deductions and certain statutory remittances if they failed to exercise due diligence. Ordinary corporate income tax is generally not automatically a director debt absent statutory grounds or wrongdoing.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust income under section 247, impose a 10% transfer-pricing adjustment penalty where statutory conditions are met, deny deductions, assess withholding tax, and apply interest. Quebec may make corresponding provincial adjustments.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure where deliberate misrepresentation, fraud, or failure to remit withholding taxes is established. Corporate veil piercing is not automatic merely because a transfer-pricing adjustment is assessed.
Improper Treaty Relief and Withholding Failure

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed the unpaid Part XIII tax, arrears interest, penalties, and information-reporting penalties. The recipient may lose treaty relief and face reassessment in its residence jurisdiction.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be assessed personally for unremitted source deductions where the statutory director-liability conditions are satisfied and due diligence is unavailable.
Shell Company, Sham, or Treaty-Conduit Arrangement

Statutory sanction: Treaty benefits may be denied under domestic anti-avoidance rules, treaty anti-treaty-shopping provisions, or the principal-purpose test. Reassessments, withholding tax, interest, penalties, and GAAR adjustments may follow.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly implement sham or abusive arrangements may face civil recovery, criminal tax-fraud exposure, director-liability assessments, and disqualification consequences under applicable corporate law.
Unremitted Payroll, GST/HST, or Quebec Source Deductions

Statutory sanction: Trust-like remittances can attract assessments, interest, penalties, garnishment, director-liability proceedings, and criminal prosecution in serious cases.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for certain unremitted source deductions and trust amounts unless they demonstrate statutory due diligence. Liability can survive dissolution in accordance with applicable limitation and enforcement rules.
Crypto-Asset and Payment-Rail Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Taxable income, GST/HST, QST, information-reporting, interest, gross-negligence, and anti-money-laundering consequences may apply. Crypto transactions may also create barter, financial-service, foreign-exchange, or taxable disposition issues.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal exposure for knowing participation in tax evasion, proceeds-of-crime transactions, misleading records, or failure to maintain required books and records.
Books, Records, and Beneficial-Ownership Deficiency

Statutory sanction: Authorities may disallow deductions, impose records penalties, reassess on a net-worth or estimated basis, deny treaty relief, and apply adverse evidentiary inferences.

Director personal liability: Directors may face sanctions for knowingly approving false records or failing to maintain statutory corporate records. Criminal exposure requires the applicable mental element and statutory proof.
Proceeds of Crime and AML

Statutory sanction: Criminal Code, Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, sanctions, and FINTRAC-related consequences may include significant fines, forfeiture, and imprisonment. Reporting-entity obligations depend on the business activities actually conducted.

Director personal liability: Individuals may be prosecuted personally, with imprisonment, forfeiture, compliance orders, and restrictions on operating businesses. Corporate liability does not shield knowing participants.
Artificial Intelligence Data and Intellectual-Property Misconduct

Statutory sanction: Civil damages, injunctions, statutory damages where available, contractual claims, tax deduction denial, capitalization adjustments, and potential criminal consequences for deliberate infringement or fraud may arise.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal liability for knowing infringement, fraudulent representations, or tortious conduct outside the ordinary protection of the corporate form.
