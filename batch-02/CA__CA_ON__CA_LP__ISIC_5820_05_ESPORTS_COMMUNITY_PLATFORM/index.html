Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – Business Registration Online portal at canada.ca/cra/business; provincial registration through Ontario Business Registry for extra-provincial LP licensing.

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity establishes a federally issued Business Number (BN) by filing Form RC1 with CRA or completing online business registration. The BN serves as the general tax identification number. For GST/HST purposes, the applicant must open a separate registration account under the BN (RT prefix) by indicating taxable activity, including commercial gaming platform services. The LP itself does not obtain a separate Ontario tax identification number; Ontario income tax is administered federally as part of the CRA personal/corporate tax filing system. Foreign partner ownership does not preclude registration, but the non-resident partner must obtain a Canadian BN for certain reporting obligations.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not offer a federal 'check-the-box' entity classification regime. A limited partnership is statutorily fiscally transparent under Income Tax Act (Canada) s. 96, with income/loss computed at partnership level and allocated to partners in accordance with the partnership agreement. Available elections are limited to schedule-based elections such as the requirement to file the TIP-501 partnership information return, statutory elections under s. 216 for non-resident partners, and optional life/other elections for capital cost allowance and recapture. No hybrid mismatch election is available; Canadian anti-hybrid rules under s. 94A and s. 18.6 apply to prevent deduction/non-inclusion outcomes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide annual taxable supplies (small supplier threshold under Excise Tax Act s. 148). Non-resident persons carrying on business in Canada must register if their taxable supplies made in Canada exceed this threshold, including supplies made through digital commerce. Registration is also mandatory regardless of threshold for non-residents that solicit orders and import goods, or that supply services delivered electronically to Canadian customers in certain circumstances.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Excise Tax Act s. 6, tangible personal property sold and exported from Canada in accordance with the regulations is zero-rated for GST/HST if the supplier exports the property directly, or the recipient exports it as soon as reasonably practical after delivery. With respect to esports community platform services, supplies of services made to a non-resident recipient are zero-rated under ETA s. 22(2) unless the service is rendered to an individual who is physically present in Canada at the time the service is rendered, or the service relates to real property situated in Canada or tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time the service is rendered. Accordingly, platform access fees and tournament services provided to non-resident gamers located outside Canada generally qualify for 0% HST/GST treatment, provided the non-resident status and place-of-performance evidence is documented. CRA may apply a subjective 'place of enjoyment' test for digital services; adequate records are required to substantiate the foreign recipient status and non-Canadian delivery.

  • CBSA Export Declaration (Form B13A) or electronic export declaration through CARS/ARR for goods exported from Canada
  • Bill of Lading (original or electronic surrender) or Air Waybill evidencing carrier receipt for international transport
  • Commercial Invoice and packing slip, including supplier/recipient identity, HS codes, and terms of sale
  • Inward Remittance Certificate/bank wire receipt evidencing collection of export proceeds in Canadian or foreign currency
  • Internal zero-rating substantiation file containing non-resident status evidence (e.g., foreign address, IP logs, residence certificates) and records of digital service delivery

There is no Canadian national real-time fiscal clearance or API-based digital stamping mandate. CRA permits electronic invoicing and electronic records provided that prescribed GST/HST invoice details are maintained (supplier name, BN, registration number, invoice date, amounts, GST/HST charged). Tax invoices and supporting records must be legible and available at CRA request. Ontario imposes no additional provincial e-invoicing clearance. For export transactions, no digital stamping is required; documentary evidence in electronic form is accepted.

CRA is authorized to reassess GST/HST returns within the normal four-year limitation period, and the period is indefinitely extendable where the taxpayer has made a misrepresentation or has committed fraud. Denial of export zero-rating can result in immediate reassessment of 13% HST as applicable, plus payment of daily compound interest. A gross negligence penalty may be imposed at 50% of the additional or improperly claimed refund amount under ETA s. 285. Fraudulent claims, forged export documentation, or abusive artificial transactions may also trigger criminal prosecution under the Excise Tax Act and income tax misrepresentation penalties. Input tax credit refunds or export rebates are subject to full clawback upon characterization of the export zero-rating as ineligible.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full convertibility on both current and capital account transactions under the Currency Act and its WTO and OECD obligations. No exchange-control license, quota, prior approval, or profit-remittance bloc is required for a non-resident-owned Ontario limited partnership. Partnership distributions to non-resident partners are subject to federal Part XIII withholding tax at 25% unless reduced by an applicable income tax treaty; no additional FX or balance-of-payments gate applies. For practical treasury purposes, CAD, USD, EUR, GBP and JPY are freely traded, and funds may be converted through any Schedule I chartered bank without Bank of Canada clearance. The only material compliance layers relate to AML/CFT reporting through FINTRAC (suspicious transaction reports, large cash transaction reports, large virtual currency transaction reports, and cross-border reporting) and to record-retention obligations under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada has no statutory timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no surrender obligation for export proceeds derived from software licensing, esports tournament platform fees, sponsorship revenues, or prize management services. Exporters and licensed treasury entities may retain all receipts in foreign currency, maintain multi-currency accounts with Canadian banks, and convert at their commercial discretion. The only administrative requirement is that cross-border wire transfers exceeding CAD 10,000 must be reported to FINTRAC by the bank, while physical cross-border currency movements of CAD 10,000 or more must be declared to the Canada Border Services Agency.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada / Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); no separate foreign-exchange-control authority exists in Canada

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Medium feasibility for an Ontario eSports community platform. Offshore digital EMI providers (e.g., Wise commercial accounts, Payoneer distribution accounts, Revolut Business, Airwallex) are structurally viable for inbound sponsor receipts, foreign-vendor payouts, prize disbursement rails, and FX conversion; however, they are not licensed banks in Canada and cannot offer Canadian cheque clearing, Interac settlement, or FINTRAC-registered local banking services unless they hold a money-services-business authorization in Ontario. Key legal constraints: (1) foreign EMIs do not benefit from Canadian deposit insurance, (2) their account numbers are not Canadian settlement accounts, (3) they may face ordinance-level restrictions on receiving certain tournament reward transactions, and (4) they cannot serve as the primary clearing house for a Canadian legal entity if the corporate account is required for tax registration or capital escrow. Rating: feasible as an auxiliary cross-border remittance and conversion rail, but not as a substitute for a Schedule I Canadian bank account holding liquidation-grade funds.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Non-resident sponsor or investor capital must be injected via SWIFT MT103 or MT202/MT203 instruction to the CA_LP operational account held at a Schedule I Canadian bank. The remittance advice must specify all required reference data: beneficiary name component matching the LP legal name, beneficiary account number, and the statement 'Contribution to capital account — Ontario Limited Partnership'. Canadian banks apply a transaction purpose code for inward capital transfers; typical codes include 'C01' for equity capital or 'Other Investment — Participation in Enterprise' fields. An accompanying signed Subscription Agreement, updated Schedule A (capital account ledger), investor KYC package, and source-of-funds statement should be delivered to the bank before the wire to avoid FINTRAC screening delay. There is no mandatory approval for CAD currency conversion on arrival; funds may be parked in CAD or swapped into USD as per the treasury mandate. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% (federal 15% plus Ontario 11.5%; general corporate rate for a corporation or for the corporate general partner where the Limited Partnership is taxable as a corporation; otherwise the LP is a flow-through entity and partners are taxed at their respective rates)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% on dividends paid by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident person (Part XIII tax under subsection 212(2)); for a Limited Partnership that is treated as a flow-through entity, the distribution of income to non-resident partners generally is not a dividend subject to withholding tax, but the partner may be subject to tax under Part I on its share of business income; if the LP elects to be treated as a corporation (or if the distribution is paid out of investment income), Part XIII withholding may apply on the investment income portion at 25% subject to treaty reductions.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

T5013 Partnership Information Return is due within five months after the partnership's fiscal year-end; T2 Corporation Income Tax Return is due within two months of the corporation's taxation year-end for most corporations, or three months for Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPCs) that are qualifying; Ontario LP Annual Return is due within 30 days after the anniversary of the partnership's registration (if required), but the Ontario Limited Partnership Act also requires a return of partners to be filed within 30 days of the initial registration and an annual renewal within 30 days of the anniversary date; if the partnership has no Ontario permanent establishment, the Ontario corporate allocation may be nil but the LP return still must be filed with the provincial registry.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: For Canadian transfer pricing, the mandatory documentation threshold is CAD 5,000,000 in gross revenue for the year (or net book value of assets exceeding CAD 10,000,000, or average number of full-time employees exceeding 100) for the taxpayer to file a T106 Information Return; however, under the Income Tax Act section 247, the CRA requires contemporaneous documentation for any transaction between a taxpayer and a non-resident person with whom it does not deal at arm's length, regardless of revenue; the standard threshold is that the taxpayer must prepare and retain documents acceptable to the CRA, including a Local File and a Master File where applicable, if the aggregate value of controlled transactions exceeds CAD 500,000 in a taxation year (for high-value intercompany services: CAD 1,000,000), and the CRA's T106 return threshold is CAD 5,000,000 of gross revenue for the year or CAD 1,000,000 of intercompany payments or receipts in a taxation year; for a Limited Partnership that is a member of a multinational group, the partnership's general partner (or the Canadian partner) must ensure the T106 is filed for every controlled transaction between a Canadian resident and a non-resident; the CRA's administrative practice requires transfer pricing documentation that includes a comprehensive analysis of the controlled transactions, comparable analysis, and an economic analysis, and the penalty for failing to provide documentation upon request is CAD 500 per day for each day of default, up to a maximum of CAD 10,000 (or CAD 10,000 for one day, CAD 50,000 for repeated failures); the CRA also issues Statement of Transfer Pricing (Form 2629) and T106 Summary and Supplementary forms.

Economic substance scope: Ontario and Canada apply the Economic Substance Doctrine through several mechanisms: (i) section 247 of the Income Tax Act includes a reasonableness test that requires transactions or series of transactions to be consistent with what would have been reasonable if the parties were dealing at arm's length; a transaction lacking economic substance can be recharacterized under the anti-tax-avoidance rule (GAAR, section 245) if it results in a misuse or abuse of the Income Tax Act; (ii) the Canada Revenue Agency's GAAR analysis evaluates whether the transaction has substance, purpose, and economic reality; (iii) the Ontario Limited Partnership Act imposes a limited liability shield only if the limited partner does not take part in the control of the partnership; if the limited partner submits to the business or registers a management role, it can be deemed to be a general partner and lose limited liability, which also impacts economic substance for tax purposes; (iv) under the OECD Pillar Two (Global Minimum Tax, implemented in Canada for fiscal years beginning on or after December 31, 2023), a domestic top-up tax applies to groups with at least EUR 750 million in revenue; (v) for a Canadian Limited Partnership that is not a 'taxable Canadian entity' under the domestic reporting requirements, the CRA will still scrutinize the substance of the LP in relation to treaty shopping, agency PE attributions, and the artificial creation of a permanent establishment.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Misrepresentation of Income

Statutory sanction: Under subsection 163(2) of the Income Tax Act, knowingly or under circumstances amounting to gross negligence making a false statement on a T5013 or T2 return is subject to a penalty equal to the greater of 50% of the understated tax and 50% of an overstatement of a refund; under subsection 163(1), a false declaration for tax evasion carries a 50% penalty; upon conviction under section 239 of the Income Tax Act, a person is liable to a fine of not less than 100% and not more than 200% of the tax evaded, up to a maximum of CAD 200,000 and imprisonment of up to two years (tax evasion is an indictable offence); subject to a summary conviction on the first offence, a fine of 50% to 100% of the tax evaded and up to one year imprisonment; CRA may also assess GAAR under section 245, resulting in a 25% penalty on the amount of reassessed taxes; and under Criminal Code section 380, fraud against the government is punishable by up to 14 years imprisonment where the subject matter exceeds CAD 5,000.

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner are jointly and severally liable under subsection 227.1(1) for unremitted withholding tax (including employee source deductions and non-resident Part XIII withholding), unpaid GST/HST, and unpaid payroll deductions; a director who knowingly signs a false return may be liable to civil fraudulent misrepresentation, restitution of the tax evasion amount, and criminal charges under section 380 of the Criminal Code; travel bans may be imposed by the CRA under the Income Tax Act's collection provisions (enforcement of foreign travel prohibition is through the Federal Court's collection process); for a limited partner who controls the partnership, the limited liability shield is pierced and the partner becomes liable personally and without limitation for partnership debts and taxes; a direction of forfeiture of 100% of the evaded amount plus restitution and a mandatory order to pay interest can be ordered under the Criminal Code.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Under subsection 247(3), a transfer pricing adjustment can be made to income, and the related adjustment to taxable capital is a secondary adjustment; if the transfer pricing adjustment exceeds the arm's length amount and a documentation deficiency exists, a penalty of 10% of the net adjustment (ceil at 5% of gross revenue) may apply; failure to file T106 carries a penalty of CAD 1,000 per month for each month that the return is late, up to a maximum of 24 months (that is CAD 24,000); a failure to provide documentation upon request results in a penalty of CAD 500 per day per failure, limited to CAD 1,000 per day for multiple failures and a maximum of CAD 10,000 per request; if the CRA determines that the transfer pricing arrangement is a sham, the GAAR penalty of 25% of the reassessed tax may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner are not automatically liable for transfer pricing penalties, but are jointly liable for the resulting corporate income tax once the transfer pricing adjustment is assessed and the corporation fails to pay; if the transfer pricing scheme is a sham to mislead the CRA, the directors who signed false returns or participated in the scheme may be charged with tax evasion under section 239, leading to a 200% fine of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to 2 years; personal liability arises under the Income Tax Act for unremitted withholdings related to intercompany fees and for director negligence if the fee is considered a dividend distribution in disguise.
Shell Company / Economic Substance Failure

Statutory sanction: GAAR reassessment can deny the benefit of the LP's passive pass-through status and tax the full platform income to the Canadian entity, with a 25% penalty and 5.3% compounded late tax interest; if the arrangement is considered a sham, section 239 imposes a 50-200% penalty and imprisonment up to 2 years; the CRA can also apply the doctrine of estoppel to assess the general partner personally; the Ontario Registrar can strike the LP from the register for failure to maintain a registered head office or for administrative revocation, which results in the partners no longer enjoying limited liability and exposes them personally; under the Limited Partnership Act, if a limited partner's name appears in the name of the partnership or if the limited partner takes part in the control of the business, the limited partner is not liable for the debts of the limited partnership, but the exemption is lost if a limited partner participates in management (as per the Act), and that loss of limited liability also enables creditors and tax authorities to pursue the partner's personal assets.

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner are liable for unpaid taxes and transfers; if the LP is deemed a sham, the director who managed the platform's finances is personally responsible for the full amount of unpaid corporate income tax, GST/HST, and source deductions, plus penalties and interest; a court can issue a prohibition order, a travel ban in a fraud investigation, and an order of restitution; imprisonment is possible under Criminal Code section 380 (up to 14 years) for defrauding the government of property exceeding CAD 5,000; directors may also be disqualified from acting as directors under the Ontario Business Corporations Act.
Withholding Tax and Dividend Evasion

Statutory sanction: The CRA will assess the payer under subsection 215(1), making the payer jointly and severally liable for the unwithheld amount plus 10% penalty (subsection 227(9)) and interest (subsection 227(10)) from the date the amount was required to be remitted; additionally, a penalty of 5% for failure to withhold plus 10% if the failure occurred knowingly or under circumstances of gross negligence; the failure to remit a required amount is a criminal offence under section 239(2), punishable by fine up to 200% of the amount and imprisonment up to two years; a director of the general partner may be held personally liable for the unremitted amount and interest.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for all unremitted Part XIII withholdings under subsection 227.1(1) and for withholding on dividends and interest; a director who knowingly fails to withhold triggers civil liability (the CRA can collect the full amount from the director) and criminal liability; the CRA commonly issues a director's liability assessment to the director personally, and the onus shifts to the director to prove due diligence; travel bans and seizures of personal assets are possible under income security provisions.
AML/CTF and Unlicensed Payment Aggregation

Statutory sanction: Failure to comply with the PCMLTFA can result in administrative monetary penalties of up to CAD 500,000 for a corporation (first offence) and CAD 1,000,000 for a subsequent offence; criminal prosecution can lead to fines on conviction and imprisonment up to 5 years; not filing a suspicious transaction report or prescribing that an attempted transaction above CAD 10,000 is not reported leads to fines and penalties; the Ontario Registrar may refuse or cancel an MSB licence; and if the platform processes payments without being a registered MSB, the CRA can impose a penalty of up to CAD 100,000 for a corporation and the key individuals are subject to 12 months in prison (the Proceeds of Crime Act provisions); also, the payment processors (Stripe/PayPal) may terminate the account and report to the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), which can lead to criminal referral.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers are liable to regulatory penalties under the OSFI AML regime (administrative monetary penalties can be against directors for failing to report); the CRA/FINTRAC can issue a monetary penalty directly against directors who knowingly failed to implement the compliance program; under the PCMLTFA, a director who participates in or acquiesces in an offence under CAD 100,000 can be criminally liable; imprisonment up to 5 years; a director may be subject to an order to pay restitution, a lifetime disqualification from acting as a director, and a travel ban as part of the investigation process.
