Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); primary electronic service portal: CRA My Business Account (Mon dossier d'entreprise), available at canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/e-services-businesses/business-account.html; federal Business Number registration also available through the Business Registration Online (BRO) service. The Ontario Ministry of Finance remains the provincial counterpart for Ontario-specific accounts (e.g., Employer Health Tax, fuel tax, tobacco, alcohol), but Ontario's HST is assessed and collected federally under the Ontario Comprehensive Tax Agreement of 2009 and an Ontario tax collection agreement.

E-government filing portal

Foreign entities without a Canadian place of business apply for a federal Business Number (BN) by submitting CRA Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) to the Sudbury Tax Centre, Non-Resident Accounts Section, or, where the entity has a Canadian presence, online through Business Registration Online. The BN consists of a 9-digit base number plus program account suffixes: 'RT' for the GST/HST program, 'RM' for import/export reporting, and 'RC' for corporate income tax. GST/HST registration under Part IX of the Excise Tax Act (ETA) is mandatory when a non-resident makes taxable supplies in Canada exceeding CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or CAD 30,000 in the previous four consecutive calendar quarters (the small-supplier threshold); registration is voluntary below the threshold, though voluntary registration confers input tax credit eligibility only where the registrant is engaged in a commercial activity. The CRA issues a confirmation letter and the BN certificate; the registrant must enrol in CRA electronic services (My Business Account and GST/HST NETFILE) to file and remit. Ontario accounts (where applicable) are opened through the shared federal-provincial BN with the Ontario Ministry of Finance. For the present scope (wholesale remarketing of used hydraulic earthmovers and rollers, NAICS 423810 / ISIC 4659), no supplementary Ontario sectoral licence is a precondition to tax registration.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation; a Canadian Business Corporation (federal letters patent or Ontario Business Corporations Act) is a separate taxpayer under the Income Tax Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. 1 (5th Supp.)); the corporation is subject to Part I tax on worldwide income if resident in Canada, while a non-resident corporation is taxed only on Canadian-source business and property income, gains from dispositions of taxable Canadian property, and Part XIII withholding on certain outbound payments. No partnership pass-through analogy applies to a corporation; distributions are taxed under the dividend gross-up and dividend tax credit regime.

Available election forms

Canada has no US-style check-the-box election permitting an entity to be treated as transparent for income tax purposes; a federal or provincial corporation cannot elect flow-through status. Available elections relevant to a used heavy-machinery trading company include: fiscal-period election under s. 249.1 of the Income Tax Act (non-calendar taxation year); the GST/HST Quick Method election under s. 220 and Part 5 of the GST/HST Regulations (for qualifying small suppliers); the s. 216(1) election for non-residents deriving income from Canadian real property; and the associated-corporations allocation election under s. 256(4) respecting the small business deduction. Canada's hybrid mismatch rules in ss. 18.2 and 18.3 of the Income Tax Act (implementing BEPS Action 2, generally applicable to payments made or payable on or after the legislated application date) operate as automatic denial-of-deduction measures for deduction-with-no-inclusion outcomes, not as elective transparency regimes; therefore, no hybrid mismatch election permits a CA_CORP to be recharacterised as an offshore-taxable transparent entity.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of taxable supplies made in Canada in a calendar quarter or in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters; for non-residents, only supplies for which the place of supply is in Canada are counted and threshold monitoring follows the ordinary small-supplier rules of s. 148 ETA. Consumption of the equipment in Ontario by the buyer attracts HST at 13%; goods shipped out of Canada may be zero-rated as set out below.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating under Schedule VI, Part V of the ETA, particularly s. 1 (supply of goods delivered to a carrier or consignee for export outside Canada in commercial quantities) and s. 4 (supply by a registrant of goods exported in accordance with the regulations). Eligibility for used construction excavators and rollers under HS subheading 8429.52 or 8430 requires: (a) the exporter is a GST/HST registrant; (b) the recipient is a foreign buyer or the goods are destined for a foreign jurisdiction; (c) the seller delivers the goods to a common carrier or consignee, or otherwise ships them out of Canada; (d) export takes place within 60 days after the supply (subject to ministerial discretion), as substantiated by the export transaction records; and (e) services integral to the export (freight, inspection, demobilisation, handling) may also qualify for zero-rating when performed in respect of exported goods. The exporter must retain proof of export status and report zero-rated supplies in the 'Other revenue' flow of the GST/HST Return (GST34) to claim input tax credits on attributable acquisition, logistics, and storage costs.

  • Customs Export Declaration: CBSA B13A export declaration or the approved electronic export declaration/EDI (eManifest carrier-level message) filed with the Canada Border Services Agency or Global Affairs Canada in authorized software, showing the machinery's value, HS subheading (8429.52 or 8430, as applicable), destination country, and exporter's BN (RM program account)
  • Bill of Lading (ocean, rail, or truck bill issued at the Canadian port of exit) or air waybill evidencing physical movement of the used hydraulic excavator or roller out of Canadian territory
  • Inward Remittance Certificate evidenced by the exporter's bank SWIFT/MT103 settlement records or letter-of-credit payment documents demonstrating receipt of foreign-currency proceeds from the non-resident buyer within established commercial terms

Canada does not impose a real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, SAF-T, or continuous transaction control mandate. Suppliers must issue invoices meeting the minimum content requirements of Regulation 6 of the GST/HST Regulations (ETA): supplier identity and BN, recipient identity, invoice date, description and quantity, consideration, GST/HST rate, and tax amount stated separately; electronic invoices (EDI, PDF, secure portals) are valid and may be transmitted through API-connected accounting systems without pre-clearance. GST/HST registrants with more than CAD 1 million in annual taxable supplies must file returns electronically through GST/HST NETFILE or an approved e-filing software; CRA maintains e-services access via My Business Account and electronic messaging for notices of assessment and remittance documents. Books and records must be retained for six years and, if held electronically, must be kept in a legible, accessible format in Canada or abroad subject to CRA retention rules. Ontario relies entirely on the federal collection infrastructure for HST; no provincial clearance portal or supplementary invoicing stamp applies.

A zero-rating claim will be disallowed and the full 13% ON-HST reassessed where documentary evidence does not establish export within the statutory timeline; the CRA may apply the normal four-year reassessment period, unbounded where misrepresentation, neglect, or fraud is established, plus interest under s. 280 ETA on unremitted amounts. False or negligent claims trigger the subsection 280.1(1) ETA gross-negligence penalty equal to 50% of the amount by which tax was understated or a rebate/refund was overclaimed, in addition to penalties under s. 322 ETA for deliberate evasion (fines from 50% to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment), and CBSA Administrative Monetary Penalties under the Customs Act for inaccurate export reporting. Disallowance also revives the exporter's liability for the input tax credits previously claimed in connection with the export transaction; internal audit procedures should retain title-passage certificates, used-equipment condition reports, locomotive/trailer numbers, and full banking records for at least six years from the transaction date.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible on both current and capital account for residents and non-residents; Canada does not operate a foreign-exchange control system, and the Bank of Canada imposes no currency conversion quotas, licensing, or prior vetting of inward or outward remittances. Capital account transactions, including foreign direct investment, equity injections, intercompany loans, profit repatriation, and dividend payments, are permitted without exchange-control authorization, although large asset acquisitions may be notifiable or reviewable under the Investment Canada Act. Profit remittance hurdles are tax-driven rather than FX-driven: non-resident investors face Canadian Part XIII withholding tax (statutory 25% before treaty relief) on dividends, interest, royalties, and management fees, and the paying corporation must complete applicable CRA forms such as NR4 and, where applicable, obtain an NR6 authorization. Banks will process outbound dividend and intercompany remittances freely, but will require board resolutions, source-of-funds explanations, and may apply FINTRAC electronic-funds-transfer reporting for transactions equal to or greater than CAD 10,000.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory export-proceeds surrender, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no timeline for repatriation of foreign-currency export receipts. A Canadian wholesaler of used construction excavators may lawfully retain export proceeds offshore or hold them in foreign-currency accounts with Canadian banks, provided all commercial invoices, contracts, bills of lading, customs filings, and tax obligations are accurately documented. Export shipments are reported to the Canada Border Services Agency and Statistics Canada for trade-statistical purposes, not for exchange-control purposes; such customs declarations do not create an obligation to convert foreign currency into Canadian dollars.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) - Canada has no dedicated foreign-exchange administration authority; the prudential banking regulator is the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and the anti-money laundering regulator is the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility: Constrained but usable at the margin. Foreign fintech electronic-money institutions and offshore digital payment platforms that lack a Canadian bank charter cannot serve as the primary deposit-taking or clearing account provider for a Canadian Business Corporation, because they are not authorized under OSFI to accept deposits or to settle through Canadian retail payment systems. Such platforms may be registered as money service businesses and, since 2024, registered under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) if they act as payment service providers in Canada. They can therefore facilitate cross-border receivables, foreign exchange conversion, and lower-value supplier disbursements, but all capital injections, trade-document settlement, payroll, and corporate treasury movements should ultimately be routed through a Schedule I Canadian bank to ensure access to SWIFT, Lynx, Real-Time Rail, and regulated trade finance instruments.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inward equity capital must be routed exclusively through the corporate bank account at a licensed Canadian Schedule I bank. The recommended method is a SWIFT MT103 wire from the foreign shareholder's bank account, clearly referencing the share-subscription or equity-injection agreement in the remittance information field. Canada does not operate a centralized central-bank purpose-code registry, but large Canadian banks require a purpose-code or structured instruction that distinguishes a capital contribution from a trade payment; using a goods-trade code for equity capital is a red flag. The receiving bank will issue mandatory FINTRAC electronic-funds-transfer reporting for transactions of CAD 10,000 or more, and the company must provide a verification package consisting of the board resolution authorizing share issuance, the signed share-subscription contract, the inward remittance advice, the updated securities register, and, if the asset or investment value exceeds the applicable threshold, an Investment Canada Act filing. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility: Constrained but usable at the margin. Foreign fintech electronic-money institutions and offshore digital payment platforms that lack a Canadian bank charter cannot serve as the primary deposit-taking or clearing account provider for a Canadian Business Corporation, because they are not authorized under OSFI to accept deposits or to settle through Canadian retail payment systems. Such platforms may be registered as money service businesses and, since 2024, registered under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) if they act as payment service providers in Canada. They can therefore facilitate cross-border receivables, foreign exchange conversion, and lower-value supplier disbursements, but all capital injections, trade-document settlement, payroll, and corporate treasury movements should ultimately be routed through a Schedule I Canadian bank to ensure access to SWIFT, Lynx, Real-Time Rail, and regulated trade finance instruments.

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Dual federal-territorial system: Federal base rate of 15% after the general rate reduction on the taxable portion of active business income earned in Canada, plus Ontario provincial corporate income tax at a general rate of 11.5%; combined headline effective general corporate rate is approximately 26.5%, whereas the combined small business rate on eligible active business income up to the Ontario provincial limit is approximately 12.2% as reduced by federal and provincial abatement rules.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Canada statutory withholding tax on dividends paid to non-resident shareholders is 25%, subject to reduction to 15% under the Canada-US tax treaty and to 5% where the recipient is a US corporation that owns at least 10% of the voting shares; for other treaty partners the rate is typically 15% on portfolio dividends and 5% on substantial shareholdings where the applicable DTA provides; Ontario does not levy an additional provincial dividend withholding tax.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Schedule 48 and Form T106 must be filed by Canadian corporations in respect of reportable cross-border non-arm's-length transactions exceeding CAD 1,000,000 in aggregate per taxation year; a transfer-pricing documentation (Local File) penalty is exigible where complete contemporaneous documentation has not been prepared by the filing deadline and a transfer-pricing mispricing adjustment exceeds 10% of gross revenue or CAD 5,000,000, whichever is less; the CRA's administrative guidance requires a Master File (general enterprise-wide transfer pricing documentation) for any Canadian entity with total gross revenue from intercompany transactions exceeding CAD 20,000,000; the Local File must be prepared by the earlier of the tax return filing date or the end of the taxation year; a separate Country-by-Country Report is required only where the ultimate parent's consolidated group revenue exceeds CAD 750,000,000, which is not typically relevant for this bilateral trading entity.

Economic substance scope: The federal Income Tax Act does not contain a general economic substance doctrine, but the general anti-avoidance rule (section 245) denies a tax benefit from avoidance transactions lacking genuine economic substance where the transaction is not primarily business-oriented and defeats the object, spirit, or purpose of the Act; in Ontario, the Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) are not applicable because Ontario is not a zero-tax or preferential-regime jurisdiction; the Canada Revenue Agency may nonetheless apply the statutory anti-avoidance rules to a wholesale remarketing business that lacks real physical presence, actual employees in Canada, or meaningful decision-making in Ontario, resulting in reassessment of the Canadian permanent establishment or a denial of loss deductions; substance indicators for the used heavy machinery trading entity include a local Ontario warehouse, inspection and refurbishment staff, trading inventory risk, purchase/sale contracts negotiated in Ontario, and actual invoicing and payment flows through Canadian bank accounts.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Section 238 and subsection 239 of the Income Tax Act: tax evasion conviction is an indictable offence punishable by a fine of not less than CAD 50,000 and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, and/or imprisonment for up to two years less a day on summary conviction, or up to five years if the Crown proceeds by indictment; the Tax Court of Canada will also impose civil penalties of 50% of the understated tax for gross negligence (subsection 163(2)); the Ontario Corporations Tax Act provides separate penalties capped at CAD 100,000.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who participate in, acquiesce to, or authorize the evasion may be jointly and severally liable under subsection 227.1 of the Income Tax Act for unremitted withholding amounts; criminal liability attaches personally under section 239, including imprisonment and restitution orders to the Crown; Canadian courts often impose travel bans, seizure of passports, banking freezes, and personal bankruptcy exposure where the corporate veil is pierced for fraud.
Shell Company and Sham Trust Arrangement

Statutory sanction: The arrangement may be disregarded under the anti-avoidance rule (section 245) and the sham transaction doctrine; penalties under section 163.2 apply for false statements or omissions causing a tax liability shortfall—up to CAD 100,000 in the case of a corporation or partnership and CAD 50,000 for individuals; the CRA may reassess the non-resident conduit as a sham and impose withholding on deemed distributions of CAD 25% plus interest.

Director personal liability: Directors who signed false financial statements, certifications, or returns are personally liable for civil penalties and interest; under the Canada Business Corporations Act, the corporate veil is not a shield for fraudulent activity; directors may face professional negligence claims by shareholders, bankruptcy claw-back actions by trustees, and in the most egregious cross-border fraud cases, extradition proceedings under mutual legal assistance treaties.
Anti-Money Laundering and Sanctions Violations

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties (AMPs) of up to CAD 1,000,000 for corporations and CAD 500,000 for individuals on a first violation, increasing to CAD 2,000,000 for repeat violations; criminal enforcement in partnership with the Royal Canadian Mounted Police may achieve fines up to CAD 2,000,000 plus imprisonment for up to ten years; a Province of Ontario order under the Ontario Business Information and Protection Act can suspend registration, revoke licenses, and permanently bar directors.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers may be named personally in FINTRAC compliance orders; the Supreme Court of Canada has endorsed personal liability for directors who fail to ensure corporate compliance with anti-money-laundering legislation where they knew of the reporting obligations or recklessly turned a blind eye; travel bans are imposed by Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada under the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act on directors of corporations tied to sanctioned persons; personal criminal convictions lead to imprisonment, full asset restitution, and prohibition from serving as a company director for up to five years.
Transfer Pricing and Permanent Establishment Misreporting

Statutory sanction: Under section 247 of the Income Tax Act, transfer pricing adjustments are assessed with a penalty equal to 10% of the adjustment amount in respect of the transaction if the corporation does not have complete contemporaneous records and did not use a reasonable arm's-length method; total penalties are capped at CAD 25,000,000; if the CRA deems an Ontario permanent establishment exists, the reallocation of net income to Ontario yields reassessment of federal tax and Ontario tax plus arrears interest at the prescribed rate and compounded daily surcharges.

Director personal liability: Directors who approve intercompany pricing based on an artificial allocation that results in tax evasion may be held personally liable for CRA debts under subsection 227.1 where the corporation has remitted the taxes to the CRA but the underlying penalty remains unpaid after a payment demand; travel bans under the Tax Equity and Fiscal Responsibility Act may be sought by the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) against directors refusing to comply with reassessment notices; imprisonment is reserved for criminal wilfulness under the Income Tax Act.
