Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

      
Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

    

    

      
Tax ID Application Infrastructure

      

        
Competent tax authority
Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) for federal GST/HST registration, Business Number administration, and federal income tax matters (portal: CRA My Business Account / Business Registration Online at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/business-registration-online.html); Revenu Québec for provincial income tax, QST registration, Quebec enterprise number (NEQ), and provincial sales tax administration (portal: Revenu Québec 'Mes Services' at https://www.revenuquebec.ca/en/).

        
E-government filing portal
The Quebec limited partnership must first obtain a federal Business Number (BN) from the CRA through Business Registration Online or by filing Form RC1, disclosing the partnership, its general and limited partners, the principal place of business, and the ISIC 6420 / NAICS 533110 licensing activity. The BN is then expanded with a GST/HST program account (RT) once the partnership makes taxable supplies exceeding CAD 30,000 per four consecutive calendar quarters or if voluntary registration is elected. In parallel, the partnership registers with the Registraire des entreprises du Québec to obtain the NEQ for the Quebec establishment, then registers with Revenu Québec for QST and receives a QST registration number. Foreign partners and non-resident partners may be required to provide a Canadian address for service, powers of attorney, prescribed identification, and, in certain cases, nomination of a Quebec mandatary or representative for service of process. For a Canadian limited partnership, there is no minimum capital or share capital filing requirement at the tax registration stage, but the partnership agreement and partner declarations must be maintained as CRA and Revenu Québec records.

      

      
Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible — The Canadian limited partnership is fiscally transparent under subsection 96(1) of the Income Tax Act; it is not an entity-level taxpayer, and income, losses, deductions, and credits of the partnership are allocated to its partners in accordance with the partnership agreement. The LP has a territorial scope as a Canadian-resident partnership: it is taxable on Canadian-source allocations in the hands of resident and non-resident partners, with non-resident partners subject to Part I tax on their Canadian-source allocable partnership income and potentially Part XIII withholding tax on certain partnership payments. For GST/HST and QST purposes, however, the partnership itself is a 'person' that may be required to register and to collect tax if it carries on commercial activity in Canada. No part of the operation is in a tax-exempt zone; the sole relevant classification is pass-through eligibility at the income tax level and registrant status at the indirect tax level. — The Quebec limited partnership must first obtain a federal Business Number (BN) from the CRA through Business Registration Online or by filing Form RC1, disclosing the partnership, its general and limited partners, the principal place of business, and the ISIC 6420 / NAICS 533110 licensing activity. Default Tax Classification & Elections

      

        
Default tax classification
Pass_Through_Eligible — The Canadian limited partnership is fiscally transparent under subsection 96(1) of the Income Tax Act; it is not an entity-level taxpayer, and income, losses, deductions, and credits of the partnership are allocated to its partners in accordance with the partnership agreement. The LP has a territorial scope as a Canadian-resident partnership: it is taxable on Canadian-source allocations in the hands of resident and non-resident partners, with non-resident partners subject to Part I tax on their Canadian-source allocable partnership income and potentially Part XIII withholding tax on certain partnership payments. For GST/HST and QST purposes, however, the partnership itself is a 'person' that may be required to register and to collect tax if it carries on commercial activity in Canada. No part of the operation is in a tax-exempt zone; the sole relevant classification is pass-through eligibility at the income tax level and registrant status at the indirect tax level.

        
Available election forms
Canada does not operate a 'check-the-box' system for Canadian partnerships; the flow-through classification of an LP is mandatory, not elective. Relevant elections available to the IP-licensing LP include: (i) a partnership fiscal-period election under Income Tax Act subsection 96(8) and applicable Regulations to adopt or change a fiscal period; (ii) the GST/HST closely related registrant election under subsection 156(2) of the Excise Tax Act, and the corresponding Quebec QST election under the Act respecting the Québec Sales Tax, which allows qualifying related registrants to make specified taxable supplies for nil consideration and thereby avoid GST/HST and QST leakage on intercompany patent royalties and administrative recharges; (iii) voluntary GST/HST and QST registration elections for small suppliers; and (iv) elections relating to the deemed remittance or self-assessment of GST/HST/QST on imported intangible property and services. Hybrid mismatch rules under Canadian income tax law may deny deductions with respect to arrangements using an LP or a foreign flow-through classification; such rules cannot be overridden by a domestic entity classification election.

      

    

    

      
VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

      

        
Standard VAT / GST rate
14.975%

        
Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)
CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies per four consecutive calendar quarters for both GST/HST and QST. A person who is not a small supplier must be registered; for GST/HST, once taxable worldwide supplies exceed CAD 30,000, registration is mandatory unless an exception applies. The QST registration threshold under Revenu Québec is also CAD 30,000 of taxable supplies, and a registrant is required to disclose the registration number on invoices and annual filings. For a Quebec LP earning global patent-licensing royalties, invoicing non-resident licensees from Canada may create Canadian receipts for threshold purposes, and the LP should typically register voluntarily if the threshold has not yet been reached but input tax credits on acquisition costs are material.

      

      
      

        Export 0% zero-rating conditions
        
A supply of a patent licence, patent right, or other intellectual-property right made by the Quebec LP to a non-resident licensee may be zero-rated for GST/HST purposes under Schedule VI, Part V, of the Excise Tax Act when the supply is made to a non-resident person and the right is not for use in Canada at the time of the supply or is not supplied to a non-resident's Canadian permanent establishment. The corresponding QST zero-rating provision under the Act respecting the Québec Sales Tax applies to the same supply where the intellectual-property right is exploited wholly outside Quebec. Pure patent licensing fees for rights used entirely offshore therefore attract a 0% GST/HST and 0% QST rate rather than exemption, preserving the LP's entitlement to input tax credits and input tax refunds on Canadian overhead and acquisition costs connected with the zero-rated export supply. Zero-rating is not automatic: the LP must obtain and retain contemporary documentary evidence proving the non-resident status of the licensee, the offshore place of use, the commercial basis of the royalty, and the absence of any right to use the patent in Canada. Royalties attributable to Canadian market exploitation remain taxable at 14.975%. Where the patent licence is delivered through tangible storage media exported from Canada, the export of the physical goods must also satisfy the applicable customs zero-rating and direct-export tests.

Canada does not currently impose a mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control, or digital stamping regime. There is no national requirement to transmit each patent royalty invoice to the CRA or Revenu Québec before issuance. Electronic invoicing is permissible if the electronic records are accurate, complete, readable, and accessible to CRA and Revenu Québec on audit. Registrants with annual taxable supplies above specified federal/Quebec filing thresholds must file GST/HST and QST returns electronically using CRA GST/HST NETFILE and the Revenu Québec electronic filing service; large businesses may be required to transmit certain data files, but no national API clearance mandate exists. Invoices relating to taxable supplies must show the supplier's Business Number and GST/HST registration number, and QST invoices must show the QST registration number; failure to display the number can jeopardize the customer's input tax credit and the supplier's compliance position.

      
The CRA and Revenu Québec may reassess a GST/HST/QST return within the normal four-year limitation period; the period is unlimited where a taxpayer has made a misrepresentation attributable to neglect, carelessness, wilful default, or fraud. Zero-rating claims that are not substantiated by contemporaneous export or non-resident-use evidence will be denied, resulting in reassessment at the full 14.975% combined GST/HST and QST rate, plus interest and the recapture of input tax credits or input tax refunds previously claimed in connection with the export. A taxpayer who knowingly, or under circumstances amounting to gross negligence, makes a false statement or omission in a return or supporting document may be liable to a gross-negligence civil penalty under the Excise Tax Act and the corresponding Quebec QST legislation, generally calculated as 50% of the understated net tax or of the excess refund or rebate claimed. Deliberate schemes to fabricate export documentation or to route Canadian-use royalties through non-resident licensees may be prosecuted as tax evasion, with fines, imprisonment, seizure of goods or records, and revocation of registration. The audit file should therefore include the full statutory export-documentation bundle, a written transfer-pricing and royalty-rate justification, and a documented internal control trail showing that the 180-day repatriation benchmark was met or formally waived in the intercompany agreement.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

      

        
Current-account convertibility
The Canadian dollar is fully convertible on both current and capital accounts; Canada is an IMF Article VIII member with an open capital account and no exchange-control permits, quotas, or licensing for royalty or intercompany payments. Capital account restrictions are limited to Investment Canada Act screening for high-value acquisitions or new businesses and to Canadian sanctions/OSFI directives; they never attach to currency convertibility itself. Profit remittance hurdles are tax-driven only: Part XIII withholding on payments to non-residents (25% statutory rate, treaty-reduced to 0-15%) and parallel Quebec withholding; once withholding, NR4 filings, and clearance certificates are handled, remittance is unrestricted.

        
Export proceeds surrender rules
None. Canada imposes no statutory surrender or repatriation timeline on export proceeds or royalty receipts, no mandatory conversion ratio into CAD, and no obligation to sell foreign currency to a designated institution. Exporters and licensing entities may hold foreign-currency accounts freely; the only mandatory obligations are the financial institution's FINTRAC reports on international electronic transfers of CAD 10,000 or more and the entity's normal tax record-keeping.

      

      
Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) is the central bank; however, Canada does not maintain a foreign-exchange control administration. FX policy is market-determined; OSFI supervises bank FX risk, FINTRAC enforces AML/EFT reporting, and the Canada Border Services Agency enforces cross-border currency and monetary instrument declarations at or above CAD 10,000.

    

    

      
Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

      

        
        

          

            Airwallex
            Fit: High
          

          
Feasibility: Low. Offshore digital EMIs including Wise, Payoneer, and Airwallex are not authorized to accept deposits in the legal sense under the Bank Act (Canada); they operate in Canada only as Money Service Businesses registered with FINTRAC under the PCMLTFA. Consequently, an offshore digital EMI cannot serve as the LP's primary operational or escrow clearing rail for royalty movements. It may be used as a secondary inbound collection vehicle only where the EMI is FINTRAC-registered, the account is payable to the LP's name, and funds are swept daily to a tier-1 Canadian bank account.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility: Low. Stage 06
Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

      
Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

    

    

      
Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)
Not generally applicable at the Quebec limited partnership level because an ordinary Canadian limited partnership is fiscally transparent. Income and losses are allocated to partners. The combined general corporate income tax rate for a Quebec corporation is generally 26.5%, comprising 15% federal tax and 11.5% Quebec tax, subject to eligibility, permanent-establishment status, taxable-income allocation, and applicable incentives.

      
Dividend withholding tax (WHT)
25% on taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident, subject to treaty reduction. An ordinary limited partnership does not pay dividends; distributions are generally partnership distributions, and their Canadian tax treatment depends on the character and source of the underlying income, the partner's status, and applicable anti-avoidance rules.

The T5013 partnership information return is generally due within five months after the partnership's fiscal period-end. T5013 slips and partner information are generally due within 90 days after the partnership year-end. The Quebec partnership information return is generally due within five months after the fiscal period-end. Partner-level income tax returns and instalments follow the applicable partner rules and do not replace the partnership information return.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

      
Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not use a universal transaction-value threshold that eliminates the arm's-length requirement. Contemporaneous documentation is required under the Income Tax Act when non-arm's-length cross-border transactions are relevant, and CRA may request it within the statutory response period. A T106 reporting threshold generally applies where the total value of reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents exceeds CAD 1,000,000, subject to statutory exclusions and aggregation rules. Canada does not impose a separate OECD-style Master File and Local File filing requirement in the same form used by some jurisdictions, although CRA expects documentation covering the functions, assets, risks, method selection, comparables, and financial results.

      
Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone regime formally called Economic Substance Regulations. Substance remains material under the arm's-length transfer-pricing rules, the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty interpretation, beneficial-ownership analysis, principal-purpose and treaty-abuse analysis, corporate-residence rules, and Canadian-source and permanent-establishment principles. An IP holding partnership should demonstrate genuine control over economically significant functions, decision-making, risk management, licensing strategy, valuation, enforcement, and personnel or service-provider capacity. Legal ownership alone is not sufficient to support residual royalty returns where development, enhancement, maintenance, protection, and exploitation functions are performed elsewhere.

      

    

    

      
Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

      
      

        Tax evasion and fraudulent tax reporting
        
Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act, conviction for an offence can result in a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment for up to five years, or both. Civil reassessment, gross-negligence penalties, arrears interest, and denial of deductions may apply independently.

        
Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, authorized officers, and persons who participated in or directed the conduct may face criminal prosecution, restitution or tax recovery, and personal assessment where statutory director-liability rules apply. A travel ban is not an automatic tax penalty but may arise through criminal process, bail conditions, enforcement measures, or immigration consequences.

      

      
      

        Part XIII withholding failure
        
Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for the entire unwithheld amount, statutory penalties, and prescribed interest. Late remittance penalties may generally range from 10% to 20%, with enhanced consequences for repeated failures. Incorrect information returns may attract additional penalties.

        
Director personal liability: Directors and responsible officers may be assessed personally for unremitted source deductions under statutory trust-liability provisions if the corporation or other legal person fails to remit and the statutory conditions are satisfied. A general partner may face direct contractual and statutory exposure distinct from a limited partner.

      

      
      

        Transfer-pricing manipulation and treaty abuse
        
Statutory sanction: CRA may reallocate income, deny deductions, impose transfer-pricing adjustments and penalties, deny treaty relief, assess withholding tax, and apply the general anti-avoidance rule. Transfer-pricing penalties can apply where the statutory conditions are met, including inadequate documentation or failure to make reasonable efforts.

        
Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, and controlling persons may incur civil tax and interest exposure, professional-regulatory consequences, and criminal exposure where the conduct is deliberate or fraudulent. Limited liability does not protect a person from personal wrongdoing, statutory trust liability, or a guarantee.

      

      
      

        Sham partnership, nominee arrangement, or abusive shell company
        
Statutory sanction: The arrangement may be recharacterized or disregarded under Canadian anti-avoidance principles, with reassessment of tax, withholding, interest, penalties, and possible denial of partnership treatment. False records or concealment may lead to prosecution under tax and criminal laws.

        
Director personal liability: The general partner, directors of corporate partners, signatories, advisers, and beneficial owners may face civil restitution, personal assessments under applicable statutes, asset-tracing orders, and criminal prosecution. A court may deny reliance on limited liability for personal fraud or misuse of the structure; travel restrictions may arise only through applicable court or criminal procedures.

      

      
      

        AML, sanctions, and suspicious royalty-payment activity
        
Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties can be substantial and may reach up to CAD 1,000,000 for serious individual violations and CAD 5,000,000 for serious entity violations, depending on the violation and classification. Criminal money-laundering offences can result in imprisonment of up to ten years for indictable offences, in addition to forfeiture and fines. Sanctions breaches can trigger prosecution and significant penalties under Canadian sanctions legislation.

        
Director personal liability: Individuals who authorize, facilitate, or knowingly participate may face imprisonment, forfeiture, civil recovery, director and officer disqualification consequences, and personal criminal liability. Travel restrictions may follow arrest, bail, forfeiture, or court orders; they are not an automatic consequence of an administrative AML penalty.

      

      
      

        Books, records, and beneficial-ownership non-compliance
        
Statutory sanction: CRA or Revenu Quebec may impose document-production penalties, disallow deductions, extend reassessment exposure where permitted, and reassess tax based on available evidence. Corporate and partnership statutes may impose additional record-keeping and disclosure penalties.

        
Director personal liability: Directors, general partners, and officers may be personally liable for statutory record-keeping breaches, obstruction, false statements, or failure to preserve trust and source-deduction records. Civil restitution and prosecution are possible where deficient records conceal tax evasion or proceeds of crime.
