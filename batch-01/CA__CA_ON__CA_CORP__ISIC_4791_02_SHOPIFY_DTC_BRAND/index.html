Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — A Canadian Business Corporation is a separate legal taxpayer under the Income Tax Act (Part I) and the Ontario Corporations Tax Act; no pass-through or flow-through status is available for corporations. The CRA may nonetheless treat a non-resident corporation as carrying on business in Canada, creating Canadian-source tax exposure on a territorial basis. Domestic e-commerce fulfilment in Ontario secures a permanent establishment, attracting federal and Ontario corporate tax on Canadian-source business income. GST/HST registration is entity-level; branch registrations are generally not permitted.

Available election forms

Canada has no US-style 'check-the-box' election; corporate entity classification is conclusive for income tax purposes. Relevant elections for a Shopify DTC corporate group include: (i) related-corporation election under s. 156, Excise Tax Act, permitting zero-rated intercompany supplies for GST/HST; (ii) GST/HST joint election for regularly purchased property and services (s. 176) and section 179 joint election for capital personal property; (iii) ITA s. 85 rollover election on incorporation/asset transfers; (iv) hybrid mismatch deduction-denial rules (ITA ss. 18.4–18.6) to be considered for cross-border intercompany financing or Shopify Payments settlement structures; (v) Ontario foreign-trade-zone or international business activity incentives do not alter the corporation's taxable status.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD $30,000 — statutory 'small supplier' threshold measured by total taxable supplies made worldwide (residents) or from Canadian activities (non-residents) in any single calendar quarter or over the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters. Exceeding the $30,000 threshold obliges mandatory GST/HST registration within 30 days; proposed legislation announced in the 2024 Fall Economic Statement would increase the threshold to CAD $50,000 effective April 1, 2025, if enacted. A VAT-grouping or cross-border marketplace rule may deem an online marketplace (e.g., Shopify Marketplace) to be the supplier of a non-resident seller's Canadian supply chain.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies under Part V, Schedule VI, Excise Tax Act for: (i) tangible personal property sold by the registrant and exported by the supplier or on the supplier's behalf 'as soon as is reasonably practicable' after the supply is made (CRA administrative benchmark of 60 days for export documentation) with documentary proof of export per GST/HST Memorandum 4.5; and (ii) services supplied to a non-resident recipient where the service is not supplied in connection with Canadian real property or tangible personal property situated in Canada, and is not a personal service rendered in Canada to an individual present in Canada. A Shopify DTC brand may therefore zero-rate offshore digital services, intellectual-property licences, and goods shipped by a Canadian warehouse to foreign consumers, while domestic Canadian online retail sales remain taxable at 5% GST or 13% HST depending on provincial place-of-supply rules.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA Form B13A or G7 electronic export reporting where the goods are controlled or require export reporting; otherwise carrier/trade evidence of removal from Canada)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill (independent carrier document evidencing physical export and consignee outside Canada)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (bank SWIFT MT103, payment processor settlement statement, or Shopify Payments payout report evidencing foreign-currency consideration received from the offshore customer)

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control (CTC), or XML-stamping regime exists at the federal or Ontario level. The Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Initiative (PCEI) establishes a voluntary Peppol-compliant national e-invoicing framework, but compliance is not yet statutory. Mandatory obligations are periodic: GST/HST returns must be filed electronically (GST/HST NETFILE) for registrants with taxable sales exceeding CAD $1.5 million; remittances must be made by electronic funds transfer above prescribed thresholds; digital books and records supporting e-commerce transactions must be retained in English or French and be readable by CRA systems. Digital services and intangible personal property supplied to Ontario consumers generally follow destination-based place-of-supply rules and attract 13% HST.

The CRA may disallow zero-rating and input tax credits where export documentation is incomplete or where payment is not repatriated within a reasonable commercial cycle; the normal reassessment period is four years for GST/HST (ETA s. 298), with unlimited reassessment in cases of misrepresentation, neglect, or fraud. Penalties and clawbacks include: (i) gross negligence penalty of 25% of the tax sought to be evaded (ETA s. 285.1); (ii) failure-to-file penalties (ETA s. 280) and false-statement penalties (ETA s. 285); (iii) income tax gross negligence penalty of 50% of understated tax (ITA s. 163(2)); (iv) compound daily interest on reassessed amounts and the 2% incremental interest surcharge for large corporations; (v) criminal prosecution under ETA s. 327 and ITA s. 239 for tax evasion, with fines and imprisonment of up to 5 years on indictment; and (vi) retrospective clawback of every zero-rated export sale in the audit sample plus extrapolated reassessments across all foreign-currency payment rails.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada has no exchange-control legislation since the repeal of the Foreign Exchange Control Act in 1951. The Canadian dollar is fully convertible for both current-account and capital-account transactions, including dividend remittances, interest payments, intercompany loans, and repatriation of share capital. No exchange-control approval, quota, or surrender certificate is required for inbound or outbound cross-border fund movements. Economic exposure for a USD-priced Shopify DTC brand arises from settlement conversion of Shopify Payments, PayPal, and Stripe balances into CAD through Canadian Schedule I bank or licensed MSB clearing partners. Profit remittance is administratively unrestricted; the only statutory friction is Part XIII withholding tax (default 25%, treaty-reduced to 5%-15% depending on dividend ownership and applicable double-tax treaty) levied by the Canada Revenue Agency, not the central bank.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no export-proceeds surrender obligation, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no repatriation timeline for export receipts. A Canadian DTC exporter may retain foreign currency offshore or onshore, convert at market rates, or sweep into a CAD operating account at any time. For goods exported out of Canada, the transaction is zero-rated for GST/HST under section 22 of the Excise Tax Act, so no tax payment is trapped on export settlement. Record-keeping practice under the Customs Act and PCMLTFA requires the exporter to retain commercial invoices, airway bills/package tracking, customs and trade partner documentation, and payout records to substantiate the export nature of funds for the bank and income tax purposes, but no surrender certificate must be filed with a central authority.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BOC). Canada maintains no dedicated foreign-exchange control commissioner; prudential supervision of authorized foreign-exchange dealers resides with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), while sanctions and import/export administration sit with Global Affairs Canada and the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA); anti-money-laundering surveillance is administered by FINTRAC. The Bank of Canada sets monetary policy and operates the Lynx high-value payment settlement system.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium; permitted only as ancillary collection/disbursement rails, not as a substitute for a CDIC-insured Schedule I bank anchor. Wise Business, Payoneer, Airwallex, and similar offshore electronic money institutions may open a Canadian client account, but any fintech holding or transmitting funds or settling merchant proceeds preceded by a payment order on behalf of the corporation must register as a Money Services Business (MSB) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act with FINTRAC. Offshore EU/UK EMIs without Canadian MSB registration cannot lawfully on-board a Canadian corporate payer for treasury purposes; even where registered, fintechs remain unsecured creditors of the EMI in liquidation, undertake no guaranteed deposit insurance, and offer limited access to commercial credit, FX hedging, or LC issuance. Recommended routing for a non-resident shareholder capital injection into the Ontario operating entity is a SWIFT MT103 from the shareholder's jurisdiction into the designated Capital Escrow / Subscription account at a Schedule I Canadian bank. The payment narrative should read 'Direct investment - subscription for common shares [or shareholder loan] under Ontario Business Corporation Act' and must include the corporate name and account number. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium; permitted only as ancillary collection/disbursement rails, not as a substitute for a CDIC-insured Schedule I bank anchor. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal corporate income tax rate on taxable income for general Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPCs) after the federal small business deduction; 26.5% combined federal-provincial headline rate for Ontario active business income above the small business limit; 11.5% combined rate within the Ontario small business limit; 15% federal rate and 11.5% Ontario rate for aggregate investment income, subject to refundable dividend tax rules; non-resident-owned corporations and public corporations are subject to a 15% federal base rate plus Ontario general corporate rate of 11.5%, yielding a combined headline rate of 26.5% before provincial abatement adjustments.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% statutory Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder, subject to reduction under an applicable income tax treaty; for a US shareholder under the Canada-US Tax Convention, the reduced rate is 5% for any company that owns 10% or more of the voting stock of the payor company; otherwise 15% for portfolio dividends; for a corporate shareholder resident in a treaty jurisdiction, rates generally range from 5% (significant shareholding) to 15% (portfolio); no Canadian withholding tax on dividends paid to Canadian resident shareholders; the withholding tax is administered by the payor/corporation through the CRA's Part XIII returns and remittance to CRA within the month following the dividend payment.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: For taxation years beginning after January 1, 2023, a Canadian corporation must prepare and contemporaneously document a Local File for transfer pricing if the aggregate value of related-party transactions reported in the tax year exceeds CAD 5 million; a Master File is required if the multinational enterprise group has consolidated group revenue exceeding CAD 60 million for the preceding fiscal year, and the Canadian corporation is required to file a CRA Form T106 (Information Return for Non-Arm's Length Transactions with Non-Residents) if the total annual value of all reportable transactions exceeds CAD 5 million; Country-by-Country (CbC) reporting applies if the multinational group's consolidated revenue equals or exceeds CAD 1 billion, with Form RC5188 required; failure to file T106 carries a penalty of $100 per day up to $25,000; failure to maintain contemporaneous TP documentation carries a penalty of $500 per month up to $24,000 for a failure to provide documentation on or before the CRA's request.

Economic substance scope: Canada applies the Economic Substance Doctrine largely through the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) and the 'bona fide purpose' requirement in transfer pricing rules; the 2018 OECD BEPS Action 6 and Canada's implementation of the Multilateral Instrument (MLI) rely on a principal purpose test (PPT) in tax treaties, denying treaty benefits where one of the principal purposes of the transaction was to obtain treaty benefits and it would be contrary to the object and purpose of the treaty; Section 247(2) of the Income Tax Act applies an arm's length standard and requires 'economic substance' evidenced by the actual conduct of the parties, the functions performed, risks assumed, and assets used; Canada's domestic jurisprudence (CRA folio S4-F3-C1) refuses to recognize transactions lacking economic substance that may result in tax avoidance; for Ontario specifically, no separate Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) exist beyond federal GAAR and the 'effective management and control' doctrine for corporate residence; a Canadian federal or provincial corporation with no real office, no employees, and no operational substance beyond a registered agent is treated as a shell corporation and subject to GAAR recharacterization, potential Canadian source income withholding, and denial of treaty benefits; the 2019 Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) guidance on Transfer Pricing Memoranda adopts OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines as incorporated into Article 9 of the OECD Model Tax Convention, and requires a functional analysis demonstrating that the Canadian entity has the capacity and actual exercise of control over risks and decision-making; for a Shopify DTC brand, economic substance is demonstrated through board meeting minutes, employment records, product inventory ownership and risk assumption, marketing and customer service functions in Canada, and physical bank accounts and transaction records.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (Structured False Reporting)

Statutory sanction: Federal prosecution under subsection 239(1) of the Income Tax Act: fine of not less than 100% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment for a term not exceeding five years, or both; additional civil penalties under subsection 163(2) equal to 50% of the understated tax; CRA interest compounded daily on unpaid taxes; legal costs and restitution orders; Ontario Corporate Tax Act penalties may mirror federal sanctions for provincial tax fraud; a court order may be issued for prohibition on holding corporate directorships.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly assent to or participate in tax evasion are jointly and severally liable under subsection 227.1(1) of the Income Tax Act for unpaid corporate taxes, GST/HST, and source deductions; director 'due diligence' defence under subsection 227.1(3) is narrowly construed; personal assessments can extend to interest and penalties; director disqualification under the Canada Business Corporations Act (CBCA) for up to five years for conviction of an indictable offence in connection with the corporation's business; criminal conviction may result in travel bans (prohibition on obtaining or renewing passports due to outstanding Canadian tax warrants), disgorgement of improperly retained dividends, and imprisonment; the CRA may place a lien on personal assets and garnish bank accounts; a director who fails to supervise treasury operations of a Shopify DTC brand may be personally liable for funds diverted through non-arm's length payments.
Shell Company / Economic Substance Failure

Statutory sanction: CRA may invoke GAAR under Section 245 to deny treaty benefits and recharacterize transactions, resulting in additional tax at up to 73% combined rate for mischaracterized income, interest, and 50% gross-negligence penalties; in the case of an offshore trust or foreign-based corporation that is deemed to be controlled by a Canadian resident, the Canada Revenue Agency may assess the foreign accrual property income (FAPI) under the foreign affiliate rules; administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 25,000 for failure to file T106 information returns; CRA may issue a section 231.5 notice requiring information; the Minister may seek a Federal Court order to compel compliance; for Foreign Limited Liability Companies (LLCs) and equity transfer it is a trap: Canada's unilateral 'GILTI-like' rules do not exist, but Canada's foreign affiliate rules on FAPI impose immediate income inclusion for passive income of a controlled foreign affiliate, with a 5% FAPI inclusion rate and 21% top-up tax in certain cases; the treaty residence certificate will be denied if the CRA determines no beneficial ownership; formal 'shell corporation' classification may attract the CRA's delay or criminal referral.

Director personal liability: Directors may be assessed under section 227.1 for corporate taxes if the corporation is a shell and lacks assets, and the joint and several liability extends to unpaid taxes withheld from employees and GST/HST net tax assessed; directors who sign T2 returns with false economic substance statements can be subject to gross negligence penalties in their personal capacity; in severe cases the Minister may apply to court for a 'compliance order' and ultimately a finding of contempt with imprisonment; the Anti-Money Laundering Act (Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, PCMLTFA) registration failure can carry fines up to CAD 1 million for directors and officers for money laundering offences.
Anti-Money Laundering (AML) and Financial Integrity Breach

Statutory sanction: Under the PCMLTFA: registration offence fine up to CAD 500,000 for individuals and CAD 1 million for companies; unregistered MSB activity: imprisonment up to five years on indictment and fine at the discretion of the court; administrative monetary penalties (AMPs) up to CAD 100,000 per violation for non-compliance with registration, reporting, record-keeping, and compliance program obligations; continuing non-compliance can result in criminal prosecution; FINTRAC administrative enforcement may issue public notice penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly fail to implement an AML compliance program, fail to report suspicious transactions, or conspire to launder the proceeds of crime are personally liable to prosecution under section 7 of the PCMLTFA; senior officers of the DTC entity may be subject to travel bans under court order when charged with money laundering or tax evasion; personal fines and imprisonment up to 10 years for laundering proceeds of crime under section 462.31 of the Criminal Code; the court may order forfeiture of personal and corporate property; director disqualification orders may be imposed under federal corporate law.
Transfer Pricing Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: Penalty under subsection 247(3): 10% of the amount of the transfer pricing adjustment, where the adjustment exceeds the lesser of 10% of gross revenue and CAD 5 million; the penalty is applied without threshold if no contemporary TP documentation is provided; in addition, CRA may disallow interest deduction under thin capitalization rules (Section 78.4 and 18(4) leading to 50% disgorgement), assess a 5% penalty on improperly claimed treaty withholding, and apply the normal reassessment window of 3 to 7 years; gross negligence penalties under section 163(2) may stack on top of TP penalties for failure to correct the return; interest is compounded daily and may be substantial for a multi-year audit.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign tax returns without reasonable care to ensure TP disclosure and documentation are complete may be assessed for gross negligence penalty if the understatement was due to wilful default; CRA has imposed joint liability on directors for corporate taxes that are attributable to TP adjustments where the corporation refuses to pay; in egregious cases, directors of a Canadian DTC brand that is a subsidiary of a US parent may be personally named in a section 227.1 assessment for unpaid Part XIII withholding on royalty payments; criminal tax evasion referral may be initiated if the documentation discrepancy constitutes falsification of records.
Shell Company / Substance Deficiency

Statutory sanction: CRA may disregard the shell entity for tax purposes; a non-resident corporation's Canadian sales may be attributed to a permanent establishment based on economic substance (agent, stock of goods, or substantial roaming activity); GAAR may recharacterize the Canadian entity's income to a full Canadian taxable status at a 26.5% combined rate plus penalties; provincial and federal authorities may strike the corporation for continuous non-filing upon which the directors may face personal liability for taxes under section 227.1; the CRA may apply elevated late-filing penalties and interest; in a ultimate scenario the Canadian entity's treaty residence certificate is revoked, causing a return of withholding taxes and elimination of treaty benefits on all cross-border payments.

Director personal liability: Directors of a dormant shell corporation remain personally liable for the corporation's GST/HST and payroll source deductions even if the corporation has no assets; failure to dissolve a shell properly results in continued personal exposure to CRA assessments and collection actions, including a freeze of director bank accounts; the director may be served a criminal tax evasion charge for assisting in a sham arrangement; the court may issue an order prohibiting the director from managing a corporation; in cross-border cases, the Canadian director may be subject to a passport denial while an arrest warrant for tax evasion is outstanding.
