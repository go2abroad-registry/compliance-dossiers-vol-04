Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — both federal corporate income tax and Ontario corporate income tax (administered by the CRA under the Ontario Corporations Tax Act) are managed through the Business Number (BN) system. The central online registration portal is Business Registration Online (BRO) and account administration is performed through My Business Account (MyBA) at canada.ca. Corporate incorporation filings are made separately with Corporations Canada (federal) or the Ontario Companies Office (provincial).

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Incorporate a Business Corporation under the Ontario Business Corporations Act (OBCA) or the Canada Business Corporations Act (CBCA); the entity is a Canadian-resident corporation and subject to entity-level federal and Ontario corporate income tax. Step 2: Register with the CRA by submitting Form RC1, 'Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts', or by using CRA Business Registration Online. The CRA issues a 9-digit Business Number (BN), which constitutes the Canadian tax identification number (TIN) for corporate entities. Step 3: Activate the relevant BN program accounts: 'RP' for corporate income tax, 'RT' for GST/HST (Ontario HST at 13%), 'RC' for payroll where employees are present, and 'RM' for import/export activity involving goods. Step 4: For a non-resident offshore securities broker with no Canadian permanent establishment, registration may be limited to a voluntary GST/HST program account under section 241 of the Excise Tax Act (ETA) solely to recover input tax credits. Step 5: Ontario does not issue a separate tax identification number for corporate income tax; the Ontario Business Information Number issued by the Companies Office is a corporate registry reference, not a tax account.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canadian corporate income tax is imposed at the entity level and there is no U.S.-style 'check-the-box' election. A Canadian Business Corporation is automatically a taxable Canadian corporation under section 89(1) of the Income Tax Act (ITA) and is not eligible for pass-through classification. Relevant statutory elections and mechanisms that an offshore securities broker may use include: (i) the section 85 rollover election for asset transfers into the corporation; (ii) non-resident branch elections under ITA sections 216 and 219; (iii) the section 217 pension or employment income election for non-residents; (iv) the section 261 functional-currency election; (v) GST/HST closely-related-corporation elections under ETA section 156 and joint election for financial institutions under ETA section 178.1; and (vi) hybrid-mismatch provisions, which operate as mandatory anti-avoidance limitations rather than elective classifications.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (C$30,000) of taxable supplies made in Canada in the current calendar quarter and in the preceding four calendar quarters, determined under the ETA small-supplier threshold in section 148. Registration is compulsory once the threshold is exceeded or, in the case of a non-resident person, immediately upon carrying on business in Canada while making taxable supplies, unless the small-supplier exemption applies. An offshore broker supplying only zero-rated financial services to non-residents may nevertheless elect registration under section 241 of the ETA in order to claim input tax credits.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A Canadian-resident offshore securities broker may zero-rate exported financial services under Part IX of Schedule VI to the ETA (and under section 23 for services supplied to non-residents) where: (1) the recipient of the supply is a non-resident of Canada; (2) the supply is a 'financial service' as defined in section 123(1) of the ETA, including brokerage, dealing, clearing, or settlement of equity, CFD, derivative, or securities contracts; (3) the financial service is not supplied in connection with real property situated in Canada or tangible personal property situated in Canada and is not for consumption, use, or supply in Canada; (4) the benefit of the service is enjoyed wholly outside Canada; and (5) the broker maintains contemporaneous documentation establishing the non-resident status of the recipient. A financial service supplied to a Canadian resident, or to a non-resident for use in Canada, is generally exempt rather than zero-rated. A supply of an intermediary financial service made by a non-resident person to another non-resident person falls outside the ETA entirely. Zero-rating will be denied where the brokerage activity relates to Canadian-resident risk, Canadian-listed securities issued by Canadian issuers, or property located in Canada.

  • Customs Export Declaration (B13A) and, where tangible goods are shipped, the carrier bill of lading or air waybill
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or SWIFT/MT103 wire transfer confirmation evidencing foreign-currency settlement
  • Signed brokerage agreement and client AML/KYC file establishing the recipient's non-resident status
  • GST/HST invoice issued at 0% and referencing Part IX of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act
  • CRS or FATCA tax residency self-certification form (or equivalent) obtained from the offshore client
  • Internal working papers documenting that the supply qualifies as a financial service and has no Canadian consumption nexus

Canada does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or API-based tax stamping for GST/HST invoices. E-invoicing is voluntary under the Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing Standard using the PEPPOL network and PEPPOL BIS 3.0 format. Registrants must, however, issue valid GST/HST invoices under section 221 of the ETA and the provincial/territorial regulations, retain all underlying books and records in electronic format on demand, and e-file GST/HST returns (Form GST34) where annual taxable revenue exceeds C$1 million. During audit, the CRA may require machine-readable electronic records under section 87 of the ETA and section 230 of the ITA, and may demand real-time access to brokerage accounting systems where high-risk zero-rating is claimed.

Export zero-rating claims by securities brokers are a stated CRA audit focus. Where substantiation is inadequate, the CRA will reassess the supply at the Ontario HST rate of 13%, add compounded daily arrears interest, and may impose civil penalties under ETA sections 280(1), 281, and 285, generally up to C$25,000 plus 25% of net tax. Gross negligence in claiming zero-rating may attract penalties under ITA section 163(2) of up to 50% of the understated tax, and criminal evasion sanctions under ITA section 239. The normal four-year reassessment limitation period is extended indefinitely where the failure to substantiate arises from neglect, carelessness, or wilful default. Input tax credits associated with the ineligible zero-rated supplies are subject to clawback, and the CRA can deny refunds until the required export and FX-repatriation evidence is produced. Non-tax securities and AML penalties under the Ontario Securities Act and the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act may also be engaged on the same facts.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada has accepted IMF Article VIII obligations and operates a fully convertible, free-floating currency regime without capital controls. Current-account payments, capital injections, and profit remittances are unrestricted. Repatriation of dividends and branch profits is subject only to Part XIII Income Tax Act withholding tax (25% statutory rate, reduced to 5–15% under applicable income tax treaties) and, for non-resident vendors disposing of taxable Canadian property, a section 116 clearance certificate. No prior exchange-allocation approval or repatriation licence is required; settlement risk is minimal.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory surrender deadline, mandatory conversion ratio, or forced-sale obligation for export, brokerage, or services receipts. Export and brokerage proceeds may be retained indefinitely in any freely convertible currency. The only applicable requirement is passive FINTRAC reporting of cross-border currency and monetary instruments movements of CAD $10,000 or more pursuant to PCMLTFA; there is no exchange-control restriction.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) is the central monetary authority; the Department of Finance Canada (Ministère des Finances Canada) retains residual exchange-control policy authority under the Currency Act; the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) enforces cross-border funds monitoring under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA); the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) supervises all Schedule I banking counterparties. Canada has no dedicated foreign-exchange-control administration.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Low for primary clearing, Medium for ancillary pay-in collection. Legal constraints: a non-Canadian electronic money institution (EMI) must be registered as a money-services business (MSB) with FINTRAC before offering digital payment services in Canada; the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) imposes licensing, risk-management, and settlement-account requirements on payment-service providers, requiring foreign EMIs to maintain segregated client funds at a Canadian bank or credit union. Foreign EMIs without Canadian MSB registration are prohibited from holding Canadian client funds; therefore the operating entity must rely on OSFI-regulated Schedule I banks for core clearing and settlement.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on taxable dividends paid or credited to non-residents, reduced by an applicable income tax treaty (e.g., 15% under the Canada–US treaty for portfolio dividends; 5% for a US corporate shareholder owning at least 10% of voting stock).

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return must be filed within 6 months after the corporation's fiscal year-end; tax balance-due payment is due 2 months after year-end for non-CCPCs and 3 months after year-end for CCPCs eligible for the small business deduction.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: There is no monetary threshold for preparing contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation; documentation should be prepared for every non-arm's length cross-border transaction to support penalty relief. The s.247(3) penalty is triggered only if the net transfer-pricing adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 or 10% of the corporation's gross revenue for the year. Country-by-country reporting (CbC) is mandatory for multinational groups with consolidated revenue of CAD 750 million or more.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate a standalone Economic Substance Regulation (ESR) in the offshore jurisdictional sense. Instead, the Income Tax Act GAAR (s.245) disallows tax benefits from transactions lacking genuine economic substance or a bona fide business purpose, and the CRA may use substance-over-form, sham trust, and anti-treaty-shopping analyses. An offshore securities brokerage maintaining only a Canadian nominee presence without Canadian employees, management, offices, client-facing operations, and real market risk will be recharacterised and its benefits denied.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Statements

Statutory sanction: Under Income Tax Act s.239(1), conviction incurs a fine of not less than 50% and not more than 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, and/or imprisonment for up to 2 years. Civil penalties under s.163.2 for false statements can reach 50% of the understated tax; CRA Quiet Disclosure cannot insulate deliberate evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors who direct, authorise, or acquiesce in fraudulent filings may be prosecuted as parties to the offence under the Criminal Code; courts may lift the corporate veil to impose personal restitution orders, grant Mareva asset freezes, issue travel bans pending sentencing, and file criminal fraud charges with imprisonment in high-severity cases.
Transfer Pricing and Thin Capitalisation Abuse

Statutory sanction: IMA s.247(3) penalty equal to 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment if the adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 or 10% of gross revenue; disallowance of excessive thin-capitalisation interest under s.18(4); GAAR unwind of the transaction plus 20% gross negligence penalty in aggravated circumstances; reassessment with interest.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false TP documentation or indemnify losses for the shell may be personally assessed under directors' liability provisions for unremitted withholding and, where the vehicle is found to be a sham or an agent of a foreign principal, the corporate veil is pierced placing personal liability for all unpaid tax, penalty, and client restitution arising from the offshore brokerage.
Shell Company / Economic Substance Deficiency

Statutory sanction: CRA can disallow treaty relief under GAAR and anti-avoidance rulings; assess tax on a non-arm's-length basis; revoke nominee corporate status under Ontario Business Corporations Act; impose gross negligence penalties equal to 50% of the tax attributable to the sham arrangement; securities regulators may revoke registration, fine the firm, and disqualify the directors.

Director personal liability: Courts will pierce the corporate veil where the company is found to be a mere striker for the foreign brokerage; directors are personally liable for civil restitution to Canadian clients, corporate tax debts, and axing penalties. Travel bans under fugitive proceedings and extradition requests can arise when the director-managed shell facilitates fraud or tax evasion.
AML/CFT and Client Segregation Violations

Statutory sanction: The Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) imposes up to CAD 1,000,000 for infractions, CAD 500,000 for third-party non-compliance on each non-compliance, and administrative monetary penalties; unconscionable client-false trade terms trigger IIROC/OSFI penalty orders and civil disgorgement.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly permit breaches can be named in PCMLTFA proceedings, held jointly and severally for restitution to clients for misallocated funds, banned from registration under securities legislation, subjected to travel bans in the context of a criminal investigation, and, where fraud is established, sentenced to imprisonment upon conviction.
