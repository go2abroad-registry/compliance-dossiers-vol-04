Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), Canada.ca/revenue-agency, including Business Registration Online, My Business Account and GST/HST program administration; Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) for customs and export declaration processes; Ontario Ministry of Finance, Ontario.ca/finance, for Ontario provincial tax and Employer Health Tax administration.

E-government filing portal

A foreign-owned Ontario limited partnership obtains its Canadian tax identity by: (1) applying for a 9-digit Business Number (BN) through CRA Business Registration Online or Form RC1, providing the LP legal name, Ontario Limited Partnerships Act registration number, registered office address, non-resident partner information and Canadian contact/agent if required; (2) electing to open or being assigned a GST/HST program account under the BN; CRA may require non-resident registrants to post security before registration becomes effective; (3) registering payroll and Ontario Employer Health Tax accounts if the LP has employees above the Ontario statutory threshold; (4) registering with the CBSA Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) for export reporting where shipments or controlled goods must be declared; and (5) maintaining the BN for federal income tax information reporting, noting that the LP itself is not an income tax taxpayer and each partner reports its allocable share of income under the partnership pass-through rules.

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible — A foreign-owned Ontario limited partnership obtains its Canadian tax identity by: (1) applying for a 9-digit Business Number (BN) through CRA Business Registration Online or Form RC1, providing the LP legal name, Ontario Limited Partnerships Act registration number, registered office address, non-resident partner information and Canadian contact/agent if required; (2) electing to open or being assigned a GST/HST program account under the BN; CRA may require non-resident registrants to post security before registration becomes effective; (3) registering payroll and Ontario Employer Health Tax accounts if the LP has employees above the Ontario statutory threshold; (4) registering with the CBSA Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) for export reporting where shipments or controlled goods must be declared; and (5) maintaining the BN for federal income tax information reporting, noting that the LP itself is not an income tax taxpayer and each partner reports its allocable share of income under the partnership pass-through rules.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

No check-the-box election exists in Canada. An Ontario limited partnership is mandatorily fiscally transparent for federal and Ontario income tax under Income Tax Act section 96: no entity-level income tax is levied and each general or limited partner includes its allocable share. For GST/HST purposes, the LP is statutorily treated as a person and must be registered and account for HST in its own name when it makes taxable supplies in Canada in the course of commercial activity. Available elections are limited to prescribed GST/HST elections, such as the closely-related registrant nil-consideration election under Excise Tax Act section 156, only where all statutory conditions are met. Hybrid mismatch consequences are self-executing and cannot be altered by a domestic entity classification election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000: the small-supplier threshold for mandatory GST/HST registration is crossed when taxable supplies made by the LP and closely related persons exceed CAD 30,000 in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters or in the current calendar quarter; zero-rated exports count as taxable supplies for the threshold. CRA may impose registration security on non-resident registrants. Ontario Employer Health Tax registration is separately required only if Ontario payroll exceeds the applicable C$1,000,000 employer threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods: exports of tangible personal property, including bulk soybeans and yellow corn sold as principal goods, qualify for 0% GST/HST under Excise Tax Act Schedule VI, Part I when the property is delivered to an address outside Canada, is actually exported from Canada, is not further processed or used in Canada, and is exported within the prescribed time. Services: a bulk-vessel brokerage or freight-arrangement service supplied to a non-resident charterer, seller, buyer, or shipper may qualify as a zero-rated offshore service under Schedule VI, Part V if the service is not in relation to real property or tangible personal property located in Canada and the recipient is a non-resident. The LP should document that the brokerage service is performed for non-resident principals and that no taxable brokerage step is performed for a person resident in Canada. If the LP acts as an intermediary rather than as owner of the grain, the HST treatment turns on the place of supply and recipient status of the commission service.

  • Customs Export Declaration — CBSA/CERS electronic export report and applicable B13A/B13B or commercial export declaration evidencing export value, date, and destination
  • Bill of Lading — ocean/multimodal through bill showing load port in Ontario/Canada, vessel identity, and foreign discharge port
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / SWIFT MT103 or bank payment confirmation evidencing export proceeds received in Canada from the non-resident counterparty
  • Commercial invoice, final vessel settlement account, charter-party, and brokerage/commission contract identifying the non-resident principal
  • Grain terminal evidence — weight-out certificates, loading tallies, inspection/certification documents, vessel stowage plans, and shipping manifests proving the physical exit of soybeans and yellow corn from Canada

Canada and Ontario do not impose mandatory real-time fiscal e-invoicing clearance, continuous transaction controls, API tax clearance, or digital stamping. Electronic invoicing is permitted without CRA pre-approval, but the LP must retain all records and invoices in an accessible, legible format and produce them on request. A valid GST/HST invoice must display the supplier's Business Number, date, recipient name or identifier, description, consideration, HST rate and HST amount, and total amount. For zero-ratable exports, the LP must maintain electronic and documentary proof of export; no CRA digital stamp is required.

CRA and CBSA scrutiny of zero-rated exports is stringent. If export documentation is incomplete, false, or inconsistent, CRA may reassess the supply as taxable in Canada and impose 13% HST on the full consideration, deny corresponding input tax credits, and claw back any previously claimed GST/HST refund. Retroactive reassessment is generally available within the ordinary four-year limitation period and without limitation where there is fraud, misrepresentation, or negligence. Penalties include civil penalties for failure to file or remit, gross negligence penalties for false statements or omissions, daily compound interest on unpaid tax, and potential criminal liability for tax evasion under the Excise Tax Act and Income Tax Act, including fines up to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment. The CBSA may also impose penalties, require bonding, and pursue forfeiture or seizure of goods for false or misleading export declarations.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a fully convertible currency regime under the Bank of Canada Act and Exchange Control Regulations. Capital account transactions face no prior approval requirements for most corporate entities; current account transactions, including profit remittances, are unrestricted subject to standard tax filing obligations with the Canada Revenue Agency. Large-scale capital flows above CAD 10,000 per transaction or annually must be reported to the Bank of Canada.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No mandatory surrender or forced conversion of export proceeds is required. Receipts may be retained in foreign currency accounts and converted at prevailing market rates. All export transactions exceeding CAD 10,000 must be reported to the Bank of Canada; no statutory conversion ratios apply.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Offshore electronic money institutions face regulatory constraints under FINTRAC and OSFI oversight. Viability is limited; integration with Canadian correspondent banking rails is mandatory for full AML compliance. Digital platforms may support limited cross-border services but cannot replace licensed domestic accounts for LP operational requirements.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% (federal 15% + Ontario provincial 11.5% combined general corporate rate; reduced rates apply for Canadian-controlled private corporations on active business income up to the provincial limit)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act for dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder, subject to reduction under an applicable income tax treaty (e.g., Canada-U.S. tax treaty: 5% or 15% depending on ownership threshold; Canada-China tax treaty: 10%). For a Canadian LP, direct distributions of income to a non-resident corporate partner are not dividends but are taxable as business income under Part I, not Part XIII; however, a Canadian corporate subsidiary of the LP paying dividends upward would trigger Part XIII WHT.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

T2 return must be filed within six months after the entity’s fiscal year-end (e.g., December 31 year-end: due June 30). Tax payments (installments and balance) are due three months after fiscal year-end for Canadian-controlled private corporations (two months for other corporations; one month for non-resident-owned corporations without Canadian payroll), subject to modified rules.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canadian dollars (CAD). Canadian transfer pricing documentation is required for transactions exceeding CAD 1 million (gross revenue threshold) in the case of a taxpayer who is not a “specified” taxpayer; also, Canadian multinationals with revenue over CAD 50 million must file a Country-by-Country (CbC) report and the Master File (Form RC4648) for fiscal years beginning on or after January 1, 2020. The Local File requirement is part of the CRA T106 information return (Form T106 – Information Return of Non-Arm’s Length Transactions with Non-Residents). The threshold for Form T106 is any transaction with a non-resident non-arm’s length party where the total fair market value exceeds CAD 1 million per transaction or series for the year.

Economic substance scope: Ontario/Canada federal law does not have a statutory Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime like the UAE, but the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under section 245 of the Income Tax Act applies to abusively avoid Canadian tax through synthetic transactions or shell arrangements. Furthermore, the Canadian partnership regime requires the LP to be a genuine active partnership (with real commercial activity in the grain trading business) to flow tax attributes; otherwise the CRA may treat the entity as a pure conduit and apply the anti-avoidance rules under section 245, the sham transaction doctrine, and the “business purpose” test. For Canadian purposes, the LP must have a real fixed place of business in Ontario where the broker activities (contract negotiation, risk management, shipping documentation) actually occur. Additionally, under the Multilateral Instrument (MLI), the Principal Purpose Test (PPT) applies for treaty benefits, so a non-resident partner must show bona fide business reasons and substance for claiming reduced treaty withholding rates.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion – False T2 Return / Misreported Brokerage Income

Statutory sanction: Under subsection 239(1) Income Tax Act, a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and/or imprisonment for up to 2 years; under subsection 239(1.1), a higher fine up to 200% of the evaded tax for more serious indictable offences; plus gross negligence penalty under subsection 163(2) equal to the greater of $100 and 50% of the understated tax; interest on unpaid tax and penalties compounded daily.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners of the LP are jointly and severally liable under subsection 227.1(1) for unremitted source deductions and GST/HST withholdings; directors may be personally liable for civil assessments of unpaid corporate tax, penalties, and interest (no jail time for civil debt but imprisonment for willful evasion under section 239); travel bans and Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) enforcement hold may apply where a director flees the jurisdiction; the CRA may seek a court order prohibiting a director/principal from leaving Canada under section 240 if an offence under the Act has been committed.
Transfer Pricing Penalty – Invoicing Offshore Affiliates at Non-Arm’s Length Prices

Statutory sanction: Under subsection 247(3) Income Tax Act, the CRA may reassess by applying the arm’s length price and reduce deductions or include amounts in income. Under subsection 247(3)(b), a penalty of 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment to which a false or misleading statement contributed, if the taxpayer failed to make reasonable efforts to determine arm’s length price, plus interest. Failure to file Form T106: $100 per non-resident, per transaction, per failure to file (maximum $200,000 per year) under section 162; additional penalties for false statements on the T106.

Director personal liability: A general partner who signs the T106 warranting comprehensive accuracy may be personally liable for fraudulent misrepresentation if the partner knowingly certifies false documentation; corporate directors see only if a special assessment is issued against the corporation and common law fraud is proven; the CRA may pierce the corporate veil where the LP partnership is a sham or the general partner has acted with intent to evade; personal liability for 10% transfer pricing penalty may attach to a general partner who is the sole shareholder/principal of a corporate general partner under negligent misrepresentation and fiduciary duty theory; travel prohibition may be sought for the director who is the directing mind of the tax evasion.
Shell Company / Fictitious Substance – Canadian LP as Pure Conduit

Statutory sanction: CRA may disallow the LP’s losses and income allocations under the general anti-avoidance rule (section 245) and impose a penalty under subsection 245(4) equal to the greater of $10,000 and 10% of the value of the tax benefit; the entity may be deemed a sham, and all income is reallocated to the non-resident principals; under the MLI PPT, treaty benefits are denied; a non-resident corporate partner's Canadian branch tax is imposed under Part XIV on the taxable income as if it had a permanent establishment; fines under the Income Tax Act for carrying on a fictitious tax arrangement may reach 200% of the tax evaded.

Director personal liability: Where the general partner is a shell Canadian corporation without assets and the CRA assesses the partnership, the CRA will not pierce the LP’s veil unless the general partner is an actor of the foreign principal and the court finds the corporate general partner is an agent or sham; if pierced, the foreign parent is liable for all taxes and penalties; the general partner's officers may be personally liable for remitting GST/HST and payroll, and for gross negligence penalties under section 163; travel bans and seizure of passports are available under the Income Tax Act if the principal is charged with tax evasion; court-authorized injunctions and civil fraud claims allow seizure of personal assets of the directing mind where the LP was used to perpetrate a fraud.
AML / Anti-Money Laundering Failure – Bulk Grain Payments via Irrevocable LC and Swift Wire

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) and regulations: administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1,000,000 per violation for corporations (FINTRAC enforcement); criminal penalties for knowingly contravening the Act: up to CAD 500,000 and/or up to 2 years’ imprisonment for summary conviction, or up to CAD 1,000,000 and/or up to 5 years’ imprisonment on indictment; the CRA may seize books and records and issue a stop-communication order; no tax-specific penalty, but the CRA has audit powers to identify unreported income from hidden wire transfers.

Director personal liability: Corporate directors and operating officers of the Canadian LP are personally liable for fines only if they have caused, authorized, permitted, or acquiesced in the offence under the Criminal Code (section 400) – with penalties on conviction: fine in the discretion of the court, imprisonment up to 5 years; FINTRAC does not impose civil AML penalties directly on individuals, but a director can be convicted of money laundering under Criminal Code section 462.31 carrying up to 10 years’ imprisonment for laundering proceeds of crime; travel bans and arrest warrants may be issued; the Ontario court may issue an asset freeze/seizure order under the Civil Remedies for Organized Crime Act for proceeds of unlawful activity.
Part XIII Withholding Failure – Undistributed Distributions to Non-Resident Partners

Statutory sanction: Under section 227(8), the payer (the LP partnered with the non-resident partner) is liable to pay the full amount of the withholding tax plus interest and penalties; the CRA can reassess the LP for the unremitted Part XIII tax; a penalty under subsection 227(10) of 10% of the unremitted amount for failure to withhold, plus an additional penalty of 10% of the amount not remitted if a subsequent demand is not complied with; interest compounded daily at prescribed rates (currently 8%–10% annually); the CRA may issue a garnishment of the LP’s accounts receivable.

Director personal liability: A Canadian resident director/general partner (or the director of the corporate general partner) is personally liable under subsection 227.1(1) for the unremitted Part XIII tax and penalties if the corporation fails to remit; the CRA can require a director’s certificate and pursue the director without first exhausting collection against the LP; personal liability also attaches for failure to file NR4 returns under subsection 162(7); no imprisonment for non-withholding but the director faces civil judgment, garnishment, seizure of personal property, and possibly a travel ban if the director attempts to leave Canada with significant outstanding liabilities.
CBC / International Information Return Failure (Multinational Grain Group)

Statutory sanction: Failure to file CbC report: penalty of CAD 2,500 per month of delinquency (up to CAD 25,000 total) under subsection 162(7.1); failure to file T106 series: CAD 100 per non-resident per transaction per month (maximum CAD 200,000); failure to provide documents within 90 days after CRA demand: penalty of CAD 1,500 per month per documentation piece (maximum CAD 15,000 per instance); the CRA may also assess a 10% transfer-pricing penalty under section 247 on any adjustment resulting from the absence of documentation.

Director personal liability: No direct statutory personal liability for CbC information return failure, but the senior accountant and the general partner’s director(s) are responsible for filing official returns; a director who knowingly fails to file with intent to deceive may be liable for criminal fines and imprisonment under section 239(1); an officer who signs the T106 certificate knowingly wrongfully may face a civil claim by the CRA and the Ontario Securities Commission if public issuers; the CRA can obtain a court order compelling the director to file the CbC report and may impose contempt sanctions that could include imprisonment for up to 2 years.
