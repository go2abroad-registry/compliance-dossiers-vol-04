Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — GST/HST Program and Business Registration; Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) for customs; Ontario Ministry of Finance for provincial HST administration via CRA; portal: cra.gc.ca and Business Registration Online (BRO).

E-government filing portal

A non-resident foreign entity carrying on business in Ontario must obtain a CRA-issued 9-digit Business Number (BN). Registration is completed through Business Registration Online (BRO) or by filing Form RC1 'Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts'. Because the entity is a limited partnership, the partnership itself registers as a GST/HST person under the Excise Tax Act if it makes taxable supplies in Canada in excess of the registration threshold, or voluntarily to recover input tax credits. CRA may require a designated Canadian representative or agent for electronic authorization and ongoing compliance.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada has no U.S.-style check-the-box entity classification election; partnership treatment and fiscal transparency are mandatory under the Income Tax Act. For GST/HST, a partnership may elect to have the partnership recognized as the registrant, provided all partners meet the registration conditions. Canada's hybrid mismatch rules under the Income Tax Act operate as mandatory anti-avoidance provisions, not elective check-the-box mechanisms.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual worldwide taxable revenues; a non-resident supplier carrying on business in Canada must register once it exceeds CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies exclusive of zero-rated supplies in any calendar quarter or across the trailing four consecutive calendar quarters.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A wholesale supply of SSD and CPU chips qualifies for 0% GST/HST under ETA Schedule VI Part V when the supplier is a registrant, the goods are shipped to a destination outside Canada, delivered to a non-resident recipient, or placed with a common carrier for export, and the transfer of possession for export is documented. The supply must not be consumed or resold in Canada before exportation. Supplies of goods that remain in Canada, even to a non-resident purchaser, are taxable at 13%.

  • Customs Export Declaration — CBSA eExport/B3 electronic export declaration or eManifest export report
  • Bill of Lading or equivalent multimodal transport document evidencing physical carriage from Canada to the foreign buyer
  • Inward Remittance Certificate — bank wire, SWIFT/TT, escrow-inspection release receipt, or other proof of payment from the overseas counterparty

Canada does not impose a mandatory real-time fiscal clearance or digital stamping system for B2B wholesale invoices. Invoices must contain the prescribed information under ETA sections 169 and 223, including supplier name, GST/HST registration number, date, description, and tax payable. Electronic invoicing is permitted, but records must be retained in CRA-accessible format. Customs export and import filings are made electronically through CBSA eManifest, eExport, and related systems; GST/HST returns are filed electronically through GST/HST NETFILE or registered intermediaries.

CRA may reassess the partnership for unremitted 13% HST plus interest and penalties if zero-rating is claimed without contemporaneous export and remittance evidence. Civil penalties under ETA sections 285.1 and 327.1 can reach up to 25% of the net tax evaded, and gross negligence or wilful participation may attract criminal sanctions, fines, and imprisonment. The normal four-year reassessment period is extended indefinitely for misrepresentation or fraud. The general partner may be held jointly and severally liable, and input tax credits previously claimed on related costs become retrospectively clawed back where the export claim is denied.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a fully convertible current account regime with unrestricted repatriation of trade-related profits and dividends. Capital account transactions are subject to review thresholds (CAD 1 million per investor per year for portfolio investments); profit remittance from legitimate export and wholesale activities faces no surrender or prior approval requirements.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory mandatory surrender or forced conversion of export proceeds under Canadian law. Exporters must report large foreign currency receipts via Form T1134 to the Bank of Canada and CRA; banks may request voluntary conversion for AML/CTF compliance when transaction value exceeds CAD 10,000.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined general corporate rate for Ontario-resident corporations (Federal 15% + Ontario 11.5%); the Limited Partnership itself is fiscally transparent, and each partner is taxed on its allocated share of partnership income at the partner's applicable rate.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% for dividends paid by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident recipient under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act. Distributions by a Canadian limited partnership to its non-resident partners are generally not characterized as dividends but as partnership income; the LP must withhold 15% under Regulation 105 for services rendered in Canada and Part XIII tax on any deemed dividend or passive income component.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Form T106 (Information Return of Non-Arm's Length Transactions with Non-Residents) is mandatory for any transaction with a non-resident related party exceeding CAD 1,000,000 in aggregate annual value. Contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation (Local File / Master File equivalent) is required for any controlled transaction where the aggregate value exceeds CAD 1,000,000, and CRA's transfer pricing penalty applies only where the transaction exceeds CAD 5,000,000.

Economic substance scope: Ontario does not maintain a separate Economic Substance Regulations regime. However, the Canada Revenue Agency applies the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) and treaty Limitation-on-Benefits / Principal Purpose Test provisions to deny treaty benefits to a Canadian LP that lacks genuine economic substance. The LP must demonstrate real business activities, including an active trading desk, actual inventory or logistics arrangements, local employees or contracted agents, and a valid Ontario registered office, to avoid being characterized as a conduit or shell arrangement.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Wilful Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Gross negligence penalty equal to 50% of the understated tax under subsection 163(2) of the Income Tax Act; on conviction for evasion, a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, or imprisonment for up to five years, or both (section 239).

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner are personally liable under section 227.1 of the Income Tax Act for unremitted withholdings and source deductions; criminal prosecution may extend to directors who authorize, acquiesce or participate in evasion, resulting in personal fines, restitution orders, travel restrictions and imprisonment.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty benefits under the Canada-US Limitation-on-Benefits article; recharacterization of the structure under GAAR, yielding a 50% penalty on the tax benefit plus interest; assessment of the general partner for all partnership tax liabilities of the LP.

Director personal liability: Directors of a shell corporate general partner face personal liability for the LP's unpaid taxes and for professional fees and penalties incurred by the CRA, and may be prohibited from leaving Canada pending an evasion prosecution.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: CRA adjustment of the transaction price to an arm's length price; a 10% transfer pricing penalty under subsection 247(3) of the Income Tax Act applied to the adjusted net amount where the transaction value exceeds CAD 5,000,000; interest on the additional tax; and extension of the normal reassessment period by three years (to seven years) for misrepresentation.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly sign false or incomplete T106 returns or who direct intercompany pricing without a functional analysis may be assessed personally under the gross negligence provisions and may be subject to criminal liability for participation in a false return.
Anti-Money Laundering / FINTRAC Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 500,000 for a first contravention under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act; criminal penalties of up to CAD 500,000 and/or five years imprisonment for non-registration, and seizure of the escrow funds.

Director personal liability: The PCMLTFA imposes personal liability on directors and officers who knowingly participate in, or are grossly negligent regarding, a contravention; directors may be prosecuted personally and face fines and imprisonment.
Ontario Annual Return and Corporate Transparency Failure

Statutory sanction: Late filing fees of CAD 2 per day up to CAD 200; administrative dissolution or revocation of the LP's Ontario registration; under the federal corporate transparency regime, a fine up to CAD 10,000 and/or imprisonment up to six months for non-compliance.

Director personal liability: Directors of the general partner are personally liable for the LP's failure to maintain registration; a court may order the director to restore the company to compliance and may issue a travel ban where an active CRA collection or criminal investigation is ongoing.
Withholding and Remittance Default

Statutory sanction: The payer is liable for the full amount of the unwithheld tax, including 10% per annum interest and penalties; repeated default is an offence under section 227 of the Income Tax Act and may result in a fine and/or imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for unremitted withholding taxes under section 227.1 of the Income Tax Act; the CRA may pursue a director's personal assets, obtain a civil judgment, and seek a court order prohibiting the director from leaving Canada.
