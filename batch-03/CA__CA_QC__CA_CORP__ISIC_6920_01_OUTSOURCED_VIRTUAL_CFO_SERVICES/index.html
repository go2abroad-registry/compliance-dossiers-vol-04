Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible for both current and capital account purposes. There is no foreign-exchange licensing, surrender, or prior-approval requirement for treasury payments, shareholder capital injections, procurement disbursements, or profit repatriation. Inbound foreign investment may be subject to review under the Investment Canada Act only when the transaction involves acquisition of control of a Canadian business above statutory thresholds. Profit remittance is tax-driven rather than FX-driven: dividends, interest, royalties, and certain management fees paid to non-residents are subject to Part XIII withholding tax under the Income Tax Act, generally 25% statutory, reduced under applicable tax treaties, with Quebec parallel rules under the Tax Administration Act where relevant. In addition, Quebec corporate law solvency tests must be satisfied before any dividend or capital distribution.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion timeline, and no required conversion ratio apply to export receipts in Canada. Exporters may hold foreign currency in Canadian bank accounts without conversion. Obligations are limited to tax and trade reporting: export declarations and customs documentation through the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA), corporate income tax and Quebec tax filings, GST/QST and customs compliance, and FINTRAC reporting by financial institutions for international electronic funds transfers of C$10,000 or more.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); exchange-rate policy is coordinated with the Department of Finance Canada, and cross-border financial-transaction reporting is administered by FINTRAC. Canada has no standalone foreign-exchange control authority requiring transaction-level FX permits.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Medium-High as a payment adjunct, Low as a standalone clearing or escrow rail. Foreign digital EMIs such as Wise are not chartered banks under the Bank Act, are not members of Payments Canada, and cannot issue Canadian demand-deposit instruments, commercial letters of credit, or provide CDIC-insured balances. They may operate as FINTRAC-registered money services businesses (MSBs) and, when fully licensed, support payment routing such as Stripe billing or Wise payouts, but funds must be backed by a regulated Canadian Schedule I bank account for statutory treasury operations, CRA remittances, payroll, and FX clearing. A Quebec business corporation should use such platforms solely as software-enabled payment conduits, never as the primary account holder of corporate liquidity.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Foreign-parent equity injections and shareholder loans must be routed by SWIFT MT103 or high-value telegraphic transfer to the corporation's Canadian bank account, with beneficiary field referencing the Quebec business corporation and account number; CAD settlement occurs through Payments Canada Lynx for high-value transactions. Bank correspondence must include the remittance purpose in the SWIFT field 70 narrative, for example 'Capital contribution', 'Shareholder loan', or 'Service fee under contract', together with invoice or board-resolution references. No exchange-control permit is required, but the bank will verify the source via supporting documents: share subscription agreement, board resolution, loan agreement, and beneficial-owner identification. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Combined federal and Quebec general corporate rate 26.5% (federal 15% after the general rate reduction; Quebec 11.5%); CCPC small business rate 12.2% on first CAD 500,000 of active business income (federal 9% + Quebec 3.2%)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% federal Part XIII on all dividends paid or credited (ITA s.212(2)); no separate Quebec dividend withholding tax. Reduced to 5% under the Canada–U.S. treaty for a corporate resident of the United States that beneficially owns at least 10% of the voting shares; 15% in all other treaty cases. Under the Canada–China DTA, dividend withholding is reduced to 10% (or 5% for a 10%+ corporate shareholder).

Balance-due date: two (2) months after fiscal year-end for most corporations; three (3) months for an eligible CCPC claiming the SBD on active business income. U.S.-domiciled non-resident corporations filing on a non-calendar year must align Canadian fiscal period to treaty-adjusted reporting.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: No statutory dollar safe harbor for contemporaneous documentation. Mandatory Form T106 'Information Return of Non-Arm's Length Transactions with Non-Residents' where the aggregate fair market value of reportable transactions in a taxation year exceeds CAD 1,000,000; Country-by-Country Report (CbCR) mandatory for MNE groups with annual consolidated revenue of at least CAD 750,000,000 (OECD BEPS Action 13). Canada has not enacted a separate Master File/Local File statutory threshold, but CRA transfer-pricing review letters request the OECD-aligned documentation and penalties apply under ITA s.247(3).

Economic substance scope: Offshore-style Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) do not apply in Canada. Canadian residency follows 'central management and control' (ITA s.250); a Quebec-incorporated virtual CFO corporation is resident in Canada and subject to worldwide taxation. The CRA attacks artificial 'letter-box' companies through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR, ITA s.245) and Quebec's parallel GAAR in the Taxation Act; excessive management fees, back-to-back royalty arrangements, and intercompany invoicing without real employees or premises in Quebec are recharacterized. A pure tax-residency certificate without operational substance in Quebec does not protect against CRA reassessment.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Dividend / Royalty WHT Evasion (Part XIII)

Statutory sanction: ITA s.215: the Canadian payer is jointly and severally liable for the entire unwithheld Part XIII amount, plus daily compound interest and penalties; gross negligence penalties of 50% of the unremitted amount under ITA s.163(2); CRA may waive the treaty reduction back and assess the 25% rate for up to three years of amendments plus a further three under the Part XIII omission rules

Director personal liability: Directors of the Canadian payer are personally, jointly, and severally liable for unremitted Part XIII amounts (ITA s.227.1) even after the corporation is dissolved; criminal prosecution under ITA s.242 for willful evasion carries imprisonment up to five (5) years; non-resident directors convicted of tax fraud become inadmissible to Canada under IRPA s.36(1)(c) and subject to removal and travel bans
Transfer Pricing Mispricing / Management Fee Shifting

Statutory sanction: ITA s.247(2) permits the CRA to recharacterize non-arm's length transactions to the arm's length price; s.247(3) imposes a 10% penalty on the net adjustment where the adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 or 10% of the taxpayer's gross revenue; Quebec reassessment follows the federal transfer pricing adjustment automatically; interest accrues from the original due date

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly signed false T106 schedules or who materially misrepresented the intercompany pricing face gross negligence penalties under ITA s.163.2 (preparer/planner penalty of CAD 1,000 to CAD 100,000 plus 100% of the planning fees); the CRA's 'intermediary penalty' reaches CFO consultants, and convicted directors face imprisonment of up to two years; the penalties are not dischargeable in bankruptcy (BIA s.178(1)(a))
GAAR Abuse / Shell Company Substance Deficiency

Statutory sanction: GAAR (ITA s.245) nullifies the tax benefit and the CRA re-prices the full profit to Canada; as amended by the 2024 legislation, a special 50% penalty applies on the GAAR benefit for transactions entered into after June 12, 2024, in addition to the reassessed tax and daily compound interest; Quebec applies its own GAAR and can assess an additional 10% penalty for false declarations

Director personal liability: Directors and shareholders who caused the corporation to enter the abusive scheme are assessed personally for the 50% GAAR penalty to the extent they were 'participants' in the avoidance transaction; corporate veil is pierced for tax debts owed to the Crown; for non-resident directors, a finding of willful participation in tax evasion triggers inadmissibility to Canada and a travel ban, plus potential extradition for fraud over CAD 5,000 under the Criminal Code
