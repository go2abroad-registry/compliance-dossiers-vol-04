E-government filing portal

A foreign entity establishing an Ontario limited partnership as a re-invoicing hub must first obtain a Canadian Business Number (BN) from the CRA by submitting Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number). For GST/HST purposes, the partnership must apply for a separate RT (GST/HST) program account through RC1 or via the CRA's Non-Resident GST/HST Registration process. The registration requires the partnership's legal name, Ontario registration documents, names and addresses of all general and limited partners, the Canadian general partner or agent for service, and estimated annual taxable supplies. The BN is issued immediately for online applications; the RT account may be activated at the same time. Non-resident partners do not require personal Canadian SINs for the partnership's registration, but the partnership must appoint a Canadian resident representative or rely on the general partner's Canadian nexus if applicable.

Canada has no elective federal 'check-the-box' regime equivalent to the US. Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible for both current account and capital account transactions. There are no central bank approvals, exchange licensing requirements, or quantitative limits on the conversion of CAD for trade invoicing, profit repatriation, capital injections, or liquidation proceeds. The Investment Canada Act may subject certain foreign investments in Canadian businesses to pre-closing review, but this does not restrict remittance of profits or capital. Re-invoicing profits realized by an Ontario limited partnership are distributable to non-resident partners without Bank of Canada authorization. The principal compliance overlay is tax-based: payments to non-resident partners may be subject to Canadian withholding tax under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act, subject to an applicable tax treaty, and any interest, royalty, or service fee cross-border payments must satisfy substantive and documentation requirements under the Canada Revenue Agency rules. Banking barriers arise from risk appetite and KYC/AML controls, not exchange controls. Funds may be received in CAD or foreign currency and credited to Multi-Currency Corporate Accounts; conversion is effected through banking markets at freely determined rates.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no export proceeds surrender rules. There is no statutory time limit for repatriation and no mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts. An Ontario re-invoicing entity may lawfully retain export proceeds in the invoicing currency, maintain foreign currency bank accounts outside Canada with Bank of Canada approval not being required, and convert funds when commercially convenient. All inbound and outbound wire transfers of CAD 10,000 or more are reportable to FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. For related-party export transactions between a Canadian partnership and a non-resident affiliate, transfer pricing rules under Section 247 of the Income Tax Act require arm's length pricing and contemporaneous documentation; banking records must substantiate the commercial substance of every transaction.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada). Canada maintains a liberalized, floating-rate regime without a dedicated foreign exchange administration. Prudential supervision of Schedule I banks is exercised by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), while anti-money laundering and cross-border transaction reporting is overseen by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC). Exchange control authority, if any, is derived from the Currency Act and the Bank of Canada Act; there are no unilateral restrictions on current or capital account convertibility.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Low for primary treasury operations; ancillary, non-deposit-taking payment services are only moderately viable. Offshore electronic money institutions (EMIs) do not hold a Canadian banking licence and cannot accept 'deposits' as defined under the Bank Act. They may provide payment execution services only if registered with FINTRAC as a money services business (MSB) and, in some cases, authorized under provincial money-services laws. An Ontario limited partnership engaged in high-volume commercial re-invoicing should not use an offshore EMI as the primary settlement account because offshore EMIs lack Canadian deposit insurance, may not participate in Canadian clearing rails such as Lynx or Payments Canada, and present onboarding and operational friction for trade-finance documentary transactions. Regulated Canadian Schedule I banks remain the only reliable clearing infrastructure for SWIFT MT103, documentary credits, and cross-currency liquidity.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Capital injections from non-resident partners or parent entities should be routed exclusively through clear SWIFT MT103 payment instructions to the Ontario LP's Canadian bank account. The remitter must include a precise payment purpose in SWIFT field 70, such as 'INCREASE IN CAPITAL' or 'CONTRIBUTION TO PARTNERSHIP CAPITAL', and execute the transfer to the beneficiary bank's SWIFT BIC. CAD-denominated large payments settle through Lynx (the Canadian high-value payment system); USD-denominated injections settle through correspondent banking channels such as CHIPS/Fedwire. The receiving bank will verify source of funds, the partnership agreement, the capital account contribution schedule, and beneficial ownership declarations. Inbound transfers of CAD 10,000 or more must be reported to FINTRAC by the financial institution. No Bank of Canada approval is required. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Combined federal 15% general corporate rate plus Ontario provincial 11.5% general corporate rate = 26.5% aggregate headline rate. The Ontario Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent (s. 96 ITA): the LP pays no Part I tax; each partner includes its allocable share of business income and is taxed at its own rate. A resident corporate partner pays the 26.5% combined rate on active business income; a non-resident corporate partner is taxable on its Canadian-source partnership business income and is subject to provisional Part IX.1 remittance at 36.83% unless it elects to file a Part I return.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding (s. 212(2)) on taxable dividends paid by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder. The target Ontario LP is fiscally transparent and does not pay 'dividends' at the LP level; cash distributions to a non-resident partner representing the partner's share of previously taxed Canadian business income are not subject to Part XIII withholding. Instead, the non-resident partner is subject to Part I tax on its allocable Canadian-source business income, with provisional Part IX.1 remittance at 36.83% if the partner has not elected to file a Part I return. Where the LP has a corporate general partner that receives a service fee or interest, the fee is subject to Part I tax and the interest may be subject to Part XIII at 25% unless treaty-reduced.

T2 must be filed within six months after the end of the taxation year; balance due tax must be remitted within two months after year-end (three months for CCPCs eligible for the SBD). Non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada through the LP are subject to the same six-month T2 deadline and must pay installments under s. 157.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 aggregate value of non-arm's length cross-border transactions in a taxation year triggers mandatory T106 reporting and the obligation to prepare contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation under s. 247(4) and Information Circular IC 87-2R. Canada does not impose standalone OECD Master File/Local File filings; the functional equivalent is the contemporaneous documentation set retained and produced to CRA on audit. Country-by-Country Reporting (Form RC4649 via the G20/OECD XML schema) is mandatory where the MNE group's consolidated revenue is at least C$750 million. The Ontario re-invoicing LP must maintain function, risk, and asset analyses, intercompany agreements, and benchmark studies.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not operate an OECD ESR registration regime comparable to the UAE. However, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s. 245 ITA) denies any tax benefit arising from an avoidance transaction where it is established that the benefit is not consistent with the object, spirit, and purpose of the ITA; the Supreme Court of Canada's Deans Knight (2023) decision confirms a two-stage textual/contextual/purposive inquiry. Conduit re-invoicing entities lacking employees, premises, business functions, and risk-bearing capacity will be re-characterized as shams or avoidance transactions; treaty benefits are denied where the entity is not the beneficial owner and where the principal purpose test under the MLI applies. For Canada-US treaty claims, Article XXIX A (Limitation on Benefits) must be satisfied by each non-resident partner claiming treaty relief.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Wilful False Statements

Statutory sanction: s. 239(1) ITA: fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded plus imprisonment up to 5 years; s. 163(2) gross negligence penalty: the greater of C$100 and 50% of the understated tax plus interest; CRA may also apply the sham doctrine and reassess beyond the normal three-year reassessment period under s. 152(4)(a)(i).

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who directed, assented to, or acquiesced in the false return face criminal fines and imprisonment; civil liability joint and several for unpaid LP-level Part IX.1 or Part XIII taxes under s. 227.1; the court may order restitution, prohibit service as a director, and impose travel restrictions via passport surrender orders upon criminal conviction.
Failure to Withhold and Remit Part IX.1 / Part XIII

Statutory sanction: Withholding tax arrears plus compounded daily interest; penalty of 5% on the unremitted amount plus 1% per month under s. 227; CRA may issue a s. 227.1(4) certificate which is enforceable by filing in the Ontario Superior Court as a judgment.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable without proof of negligence for unremitted Part IX.1/Part XIII amounts under s. 227.1(1); the due-diligence defense is narrow and requires proof of reasonable care in financial management; bankruptcy does not discharge director liability for withholding taxes.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: s. 247(2): upward adjustment of income to the arm's length amount; s. 247(3): penalty equal to the lesser of 10% of the adjustment and C$100,000 per taxation year where reasonable efforts were not made; arrears interest compounds from the understated year; false documentation also attracts s. 163(2) gross negligence penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for TP adjustments, but a director who signs the T106 or T2 with knowledge of the false transfer price is exposed to criminal prosecution under s. 239; where the TP adjustment increases Part IX.1 or Part XIII liability, s. 227.1 director liability applies to the unpaid remittance.
Shell Company / Economic Substance / GAAR

Statutory sanction: s. 245 GAAR denies the entire tax benefit and re-characterizes transactions with full Part I tax, arrears interest, and penalties; treaty benefits denied under the Limitation on Benefits and the MLI principal purpose test; concealment of the scheme attracts s. 163(2) penalties at 50% of the tax benefit.

Director personal liability: Directors who designed or participated in the GAAR planning may be assessed as persons who participated in the transaction and face personal liability for the unremitted Part IX.1/Part XIII amounts; criminal charges for money laundering under s. 462.31 of the Criminal Code apply where the shell is used to disguise the proceeds of fiscal evasion, with imprisonment up to 10 years.
Money Laundering / AML Breach (FINTRAP)

Statutory sanction: Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act: administrative monetary penalties up to C$1,000,000 per violation for corporations, plus compliance orders and potential suspension or revocation of registration where the LP is a money services business or carries on remittance activity; criminal liability under s. 462.31(2) of the Criminal Code with fines up to twice the property value and imprisonment up to 10 years.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers are personally liable where they knowingly fail to implement the compliance program or direct the prohibited transaction; courts may impose criminal fines equal to double the value of the laundered property, order restitution to the Crown and affected parties, prohibit the director from managing a business, and impose imprisonment up to 10 years.
