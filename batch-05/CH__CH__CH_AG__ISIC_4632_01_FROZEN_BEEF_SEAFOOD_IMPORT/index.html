Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

Minimum nominal share capital CHF 100,000; upon incorporation, at least 20% of the nominal value of each share and an absolute minimum of CHF 50,000 in total must be paid in cash or verified as non-cash contribution (Art. 621(2) and Arts. 628–633 CO). Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Federal Tax Administration (FTA/ESTV) – Eidgenössische Steuerverwaltung; online portal: www.estv.admin.ch / VAT e-service portal for registration and filing

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity without a Swiss domicile or permanent establishment must register for Swiss VAT if it performs taxable supplies in Switzerland and annual taxable turnover (excluding exempt supplies) exceeds CHF 100,000. Registration is made via the FTA’s online registration form (e-service), providing details of the foreign entity, proof of foreign TIN, anticipated turnover, business activity, and local agent if required. Upon approval, the FTA issues a UID (Unternehmens-Identifikationsnummer) in CHE-XXXX.XXX.XXX format, which serves as the VAT registration number; the entity must add ‘MWST’ to the UID for VAT transactions.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

No check-the-box election exists under Swiss corporate tax law; an AG is always treated as a taxable separate entity and is subject to unlimited tax liability. Available grouping elections are strictly limited to Swiss VAT groups (Art. 12 of the VAT Act) permitting members to consolidate within a single UID/MWST number, provided they are financially, economically, and organisationally integrated; there is no tax consolidation for federal or cantonal income tax purposes. Anti-hybrid rules under the Swiss implementation of BEPS apply and no elective hybrid mismatch mechanism is available.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

8.1%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CHF 100,000 annual taxable turnover in Switzerland (excluding exempt turnover; for foreign entities with no Swiss domicile, this threshold applies to supplies linked to Swiss territory)

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of physical goods are subject to 0% Swiss VAT provided the goods leave Swiss customs territory as evidenced by an accepted electronic customs export declaration and the exporter retains adequate proof of export within the statutory period (generally 30 days from supply). For services — including transport, commission, and intermediary services directly linked to exports of goods, or for services performed on movable goods which are subsequently exported — Swiss VAT may also be zero-rated if the recipient is abroad or the service relates to immovable property/exported goods. Domestic sales of imported frozen meat and seafood are taxable at 8.1%; only genuine cross-border exports qualify for zero-rating.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Ausfuhranmeldung / e-dec output)
  • Bill of Lading or Airway Bill / CMR consignment note evidencing physical export
  • Commercial Invoice referencing the export declaration and consignee abroad
  • Proof of payment and/or inward remittance certificate for export proceeds (if payment received via banking channel)

No mandate for real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or national API-based electronic invoicing currently exists in Switzerland. A B2B electronic invoice is permitted and legal; since 2020, the Swiss QR-bill standard is the mandatory format for invoice payment slips generated for domestic payment traffic, but this is not a fiscal clearance mandate. Customs export declarations must be submitted electronically via the FTA’s customs declaration system (e-dec / NTCS) as part of the export procedure.

Swiss customs and tax authorities scrutinize export zero-rating claims, requiring robust documentary proof that goods actually left Swiss customs territory. If zero-rating is wrongly applied, the FTA assesses the VAT due with default interest (normally 5% per annum) and may impose administrative fines of up to four times the tax evaded in cases of intentional tax evasion. Where fraudulent documentation is used, criminal penalties (fines and potential imprisonment) may apply; clawback is retroactive to the date of the original supply, and the taxpayer bears burden of proof for all export substantiation.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Switzerland maintains unrestricted currency convertibility for both current and capital account transactions. There are no exchange controls, no central-bank approval gates, and no requirement to license capital flows into or out of the Swiss franc. Profit remittance, dividend distributions, intercompany loan repayments, royalty transfers, and liquidation proceeds may be freely transferred abroad. Dividends paid by a Swiss AG are subject to 35% Swiss withholding tax unless reduced by an applicable double-taxation treaty, but the FX conversion itself is without restriction. Capital accounts: fully liberalized, subject only to annual SNB statistical reporting thresholds for cross-border financial claims and liabilities (e.g., the ‘Maximalforderung/Maximalverbindlichkeit’ survey for non-bank financial institutions). Current accounts: fully convertible; import settlements, customs VAT, and trade payments do not require exchange authorization. In practice, banks apply enhanced source-of-funds checks and obtain a written purpose of payment for every SWIFT or SIC transfer, which can delay settlement by 1–2 days but does not constitute convertibility restriction. Harm to profitability or balance-sheet hedging is managed by standard market-based instruments; repatriation is never blocked by Swiss statutory law.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Swiss law imposes no surrender requirement, no mandatory forced FX conversion, and no statutory repatriation deadline for export receipts. An exporter is free to hold all foreign-currency earnings in any Swiss or offshore account indefinitely. For statistical and surveillance purposes only, resident non-bank entities must report outstanding foreign receivables and payables to the Swiss National Bank on an annual basis when the aggregate balance exceeds CHF 10 million; goods-related trade receivables are reported in the Swiss International Investment Position survey. There is no requirement to convert into CHF, though if an exporter chooses to hedge via the home country’s central-bank forward market, SNB rules permit unrestricted forward transactions. Supporting records (commercial invoice, bill of lading/airway bill, packing list, customs declaration, veterinary and health certificates) must be preserved for at least 10 years under the Swiss Code of Obligations and the VAT Act, and must be produced to the bank or SNB upon request for FATF/AML monitoring.

Central bank supervision: Swiss National Bank (Schweizerische Nationalbank, SNB), acting concurrently with the Swiss Financial Market Supervisory Authority (FINMA) for banking and financial-market conduct; the Federal Administration of Customs (FCA) handles trade-document verification.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Feasibility: Moderate-to-low as a primary treasury channel, but viable only as a secondary execution rail. Swiss FINMA-licensed fintech institutions (e-money licenses under Art. 1b Banking Act) may accept public deposits up to CHF 100 million without the full banking license, and hence cannot provide a full suite of trade finance instruments such as documentary credits, commercial letters of credit, bank guarantees, or SWIFT-sponsored MT700 issuance. Foreign digital EMI wallets (e.g., Wise platform-based accounts, Revolut Business, various UK/EEA electronic-money institutions) are not passportable into Switzerland; Switzerland is not an EEA member, so an EEA EMI cannot establish a branch or offer regulated payment services in Switzerland without a separate Swiss FINMA license or a licensed Swiss bank sponsorship. Legal constraints: FINMA Circular 2018/03, the AMLA (Anti-Money-Laundering Act), and the Swiss Code of Obligations require a Swiss-regulated payment institution to perform full CDD, obtain UBO declarations, screen sanctions (SECO, UN, EU, OFAC), retain records for 10 years, and classify business lines according to the AMLA. A foreign EMI can process FX conversion and provide virtual IBANs with underlying Swiss partner banks, but for a CHF 10 million per month meat/seafood settlement flow, the EMI is operationally brittle: it lacks LC capabilities, cannot provide correspondent banking assurances for large seafood trade, and frequently triggers bank-internal enhanced KYC due to impossible verification of onion-transaction counterparties. Therefore, the blueprint must choose a Tier-1 licensed Swiss bank as settlement anchor and use EMIs only for payroll or minor contingent payments.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Equity and shareholder-loan injections from foreign parent to Swiss AG must be routed via SWIFT MT103, with serial or cover payment instructions as per the remitting bank’s practice. The beneficiary account must be a dedicated capital escrow account or general operational account previously T&I-opened with the Swiss bank. In the payment instruction, the remitter must use a clear purpose code reconcilable with Swiss SNB reporting: for equity injection use ISO 20022 purpose code 'CAPH' (capital owner contribution) or 'FYIN' (initial endowment) – Swiss banks map these to the corresponding SNB Berichtigung code; for intercompany loans use 'LOAN' (loan disbursement) with, for remittance details, the loan agreement number and maturity date. The Swiss bank will not deny the FX transfer, but will retain documentation of: (i) board resolution of the Swiss entity or parent authorizing capital raise; (ii) notarized extract of the commercial register if the capital increase is registered; (iii) a certified shareholder register; (iv) source of funds declaration; and (v) if the inbound remittance is a shareholder loan for tax buoyancy, the arm’s-length loan agreement plus OECD Transfer Pricing file showing admissible interest rate. No SNB pre-approval is required. For amounts above CHF 10 million, the bank will run an additional enhanced CDD review but execution is still immediate. It is also recommended to use a correspondent corridor via a major Swiss bank with clear UMPC fallback to avoid EUR/CHF latency: instruct via the euro-zone clearing through TARGET2 or the Swiss SIC EURO-SIC system.For payment of vendor invoices to foreign exporters of frozen beef and seafood, the authorized clearing rails are: (1) SWIFT MT103 (customer credit transfer) for direct payments, with recommended cover via MT202 or MT205 depending on the correspondent network; (2) Commercial Letters of Credit through MT700 (issue), MT707 (amendment), MT710/MT720 (advised/confirmed transfer) under UCP600 — for bulk shipments a revolving LC is preferred to avoid document re-work; (3) SWIFT MT760 (Standby Letter of Credit / guarantee) when performance bonds are contractually required; and (4) EUR or CHF payments via the Swiss SIC / euro-SIC interoperable with TARGET2 and SEPA Channels; Swiss banks are SEPA participants, so EUR remittances can be sent as SEPA credit transfers, but attention to the BIC/IBAN checksum and reference text is mandatory. Payments for import goods must be supported by a complete trade documentary set (invoice, packing list, bill of lading/AWB, cold-chain temperature log, FSVO sanitary certificates) and must be submitted to the paying bank at least one banking day in advance to allow the compliance team to perform the FINMA-union sanctions screening. Outbound settlement is unrestricted by Switzerland; there is no surrender tax, no exchange licensing, and no supplementary capital controls. Legal constraints: FINMA Circular 2018/03, the AMLA (Anti-Money-Laundering Act), and the Swiss Code of Obligations require a Swiss-regulated payment institution to perform full CDD, obtain UBO declarations, screen sanctions (SECO, UN, EU, OFAC), retain records for 10 years, and classify business lines according to the AMLA. A foreign EMI can process FX conversion and provide virtual IBANs with underlying Swiss partner banks, but for a CHF 10 million per month meat/seafood settlement flow, the EMI is operationally brittle: it lacks LC capabilities, cannot provide correspondent banking assurances for large seafood trade, and frequently triggers bank-internal enhanced KYC due to impossible verification of onion-transaction counterparties. Therefore, the blueprint must choose a Tier-1 licensed Swiss bank as settlement anchor and use EMIs only for payroll or minor contingent payments.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CHF 5,000,000 aggregate related-party cross-border transaction value in a tax year triggers obligation to produce Local File; additionally group turnover above CHF 80,000,000 calls for a full Master File and Country-by-Country data under Swiss large-business practice (no statutory threshold set in the DBG).

Economic substance scope: Switzerland applies a general economic substance requirement to all taxable entities. An AG must demonstrate premises, qualified staff, genuine board decisions, and operational books and records in the jurisdiction. Tax authorities deny treaty benefits and DTA relief to artificial shell structures under the Swiss General Anti-Avoidance Rules (Art. 210 DBG). There is no formal ESR legislation as in the UAE, but the same economic substance test is enforced through jurisprudence and transfer pricing audits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Fine up to 250% of the federal tax evaded (minimum CHF 10,500 for intentional evasion); cantonal penalties up to 50% of evaded tax; plus late surcharge interest at 4.5% p.a.; criminal imprisonment up to 3 years in aggravated cases.

Director personal liability: Directors and beneficial owners are jointly and severally liable for civil restitution of unpaid tax; the veil is pierced; personal assets can be seized; criminal sentencing includes imprisonment, travel bans, and mutual legal assistance to foreign customs or tax authorities.
Shell Company / Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: Disregard of the legal entity for tax purposes; income reattributed to the ultimate principal; retroactive withholding tax levied at 35%; denial of DTA relief; administrative fine up to 100% of underpaid tax.

Director personal liability: Directors and shareholder principals are personally liable for the tax arrears; their personal mandates are treated as sham; prohibition from acting as director in Switzerland; travel bans issued pending criminal proceedings.
Transfer Pricing Misdeclaration / Non-Disclosure

Statutory sanction: Reassessment with upward profit adjustment to the arm's-length margin; underpayment interest (4.5% p.a.); penalty of 50% of the underpaid tax; customs fines for undervaluation up to 200% of underpaid duties.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign tax returns and TP policies are personally exposed for underpayment if they knew or ought to have known the policy was non-arm's-length; liability extends to personal assets under Swiss debt enforcement; criminal conviction leads to disqualification and removal from board.
AML / Customs / Sanctions Violations

Statutory sanction: Under the Swiss Anti-Money Laundering Act: fine up to CHF 500,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years for directors/employees; customs violations: seizure of goods and fine equal to up to 10 times the value of the goods; freezing of assets under sanctions regulations.

Director personal liability: Directors and beneficial owners are criminally prosecuted individually; travel bans and asset freezing orders are issued; personal wealth is applied to satisfy restitution; management disqualification announced in Swiss official registry.
