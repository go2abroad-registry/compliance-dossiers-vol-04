Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada — Business Registration Online (BRO) and My Business Account portal; Ontario corporate income tax and Harmonized Sales Tax (HST) are administered federally by CRA under Ontario's tax collection agreement, with the Ontario Ministry of Finance responsible for residual provincial tax obligations such as the Ontario Employer Health Tax.

E-government filing portal

A federally or provincially incorporated Ontario business corporation must obtain a CRA Business Number (BN), which is the single statutory tax identifier. A foreign entity carrying on business in Canada must apply for a BN through Business Registration Online or by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number), selecting program accounts as applicable: RC for corporate income tax (T2), RT for payroll source deductions, RM for GST/HST, and RP for non-resident withholding. For a non-resident corporation with a Canadian permanent establishment, registration includes the T2 corporate tax filing and Ontario corporate tax reporting; no separate Ontario tax ID is issued. A foreign corporation must also obtain extra-provincial registration through the Ontario Business Registry, but that registry number is not a tax ID.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation — A federally or provincially incorporated Ontario business corporation must obtain a CRA Business Number (BN), which is the single statutory tax identifier. A foreign entity carrying on business in Canada must apply for a BN through Business Registration Online or by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number), selecting program accounts as applicable: RC for corporate income tax (T2), RT for payroll source deductions, RM for GST/HST, and RP for non-resident withholding. For a non-resident corporation with a Canadian permanent establishment, registration includes the T2 corporate tax filing and Ontario corporate tax reporting; no separate Ontario tax ID is issued. A foreign corporation must also obtain extra-provincial registration through the Ontario Business Registry, but that registry number is not a tax ID.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not operate a US-style check-the-box regime. A federal/provincial business corporation is a per se separate taxable entity, and no pass-through election is available. Relevant statutory elections include functional-currency reporting under s.261 of the Income Tax Act, capital-cost-allowance and depreciation elections, associated-company allocation elections for federal and Ontario corporate tax purposes, GST/HST closely-related-corporation grouping elections under s.156 of the Excise Tax Act, and annual GST/HST filing-period elections. Hybrid mismatch outcomes are not elective; Canada applies mandatory domestic hybrid mismatch and MLI rules.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in gross revenue from taxable supplies made in Canada during the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters, being the small-supplier threshold; persons exceeding this threshold, including non-residents carrying on a Canadian business, must register for GST/HST.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods physically exported from Canada are zero-rated when supplied to an address outside Canada with documentary proof of export within a reasonable period. Services supplied to a non-resident person are generally zero-rated if performed outside Canada and not in respect of Canadian real property or goods. For cross-border money transmission services, transaction fees are financial services under the Excise Tax Act and are constitutionally exempt when supplied in Canada to a Canadian-resident remitter; zero-rating under Schedule VI Part IX is available only where the supplier is a financial institution and the financial service is supplied to a non-resident person in a non-resident capacity and is not connected to a Canadian business, account, or deposit. The remittance agency must classify each fee stream and retain recipient-residency evidence.

  • Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) Export Declaration — Form B13A or Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) confirmation, if applicable
  • Carrier-issued Bill of Lading or Air Waybill for any goods component
  • Signed commercial contract or service agreement identifying the non-resident recipient
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / bank credit advice showing settlement into the corporation's Canadian bank account
  • SWIFT MT100-series transaction records or local cash-pickup clearing settlement confirmation
  • KYC/AML records, destination-country evidence, and FX conversion confirmation supporting the zero-rating claim

No real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or government API pre-approval of invoices is mandated in Canada. CRA requires electronic filing of GST/HST returns through NETFILE or EDI where applicable. E-invoicing is not mandatory, though pan-Canadian PEPPOL standards may be used voluntarily in public-sector transactions. Taxpayers must retain complete audit trails, invoices, contracts, payment rail confirmations, and export substantiation.

CRA may reassess within the normal four-year limitation period, or indefinitely where misrepresentation or fraud is found. Unsupported zero-rating claims are reversed, resulting in additional GST/HST, arrears interest, and penalties up to 50% of the net tax under s.280(1) of the Excise Tax Act for false statements, repeat-offence penalties under s.285, and criminal prosecution under s.327. Input tax credits attributable to exempt financial services are clawed back and require fair allocation among exempt, taxable, and zero-rated activities. For corporate income tax, misstated export revenue or repatriation records may also trigger gross-negligence penalties under s.163.2 of the Income Tax Act.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is generally fully convertible for current-account and capital-account transactions. Canada does not generally impose mandatory foreign-exchange surrender, prior approval for ordinary cross-border remittances, or routine restrictions on repatriation of paid-up capital, dividends, interest, or service fees. Transfers remain subject to lawful-purpose verification, anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing controls under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, sanctions screening under the United Nations Act and related regulations, tax withholding and transfer-pricing rules, corporate authorization, and bank transaction-monitoring requirements. A remittance business must be registered as a money services business with FINTRAC before conducting prescribed services in Canada, maintain a compliance program, verify clients and beneficial owners, retain records, file suspicious transaction reports and applicable large virtual currency or large cash reports, and comply with travel-rule requirements where applicable. Payment service provider registration and operational-risk requirements under the Retail Payment Activities Act may also apply depending on the entity's payment functions, including holding end-user funds or providing payment functions in Canada. Ontario generally does not impose a separate province-wide money-transmission licence equivalent to certain other Canadian provinces, but federal registration, consumer-protection, privacy, employment, tax, and municipal requirements remain applicable. Funds received for consumers should be operationally segregated from corporate funds and safeguarded in accordance with applicable payment, contractual, and prudential requirements.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no general statutory export-proceeds surrender rule, mandatory conversion ratio, or prescribed conversion deadline for ordinary goods or services exports. Export receipts may generally remain in Canadian dollars or foreign-currency accounts and may be converted at the entity's treasury discretion. The company must retain commercially reasonable invoices, contracts, shipping or service evidence, payment records, and reconciliation support; report taxable income and foreign-currency gains or losses under Canadian tax rules; comply with applicable GST/HST and customs reporting; and provide information requested by its bank, FINTRAC, the Canada Revenue Agency, or other competent authority. Any receipt involving sanctioned jurisdictions, prohibited counterparties, unusual third-party settlement, or unexplained source of funds may be delayed, rejected, frozen, or reported.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada. Foreign-exchange administration is not conducted through a general exchange-control authority in Canada. Financial intelligence, AML, sanctions, and money-services-business supervision are principally administered by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), while the Department of Finance Canada administers federal policy and legislative frameworks.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for technology and payment-rail connectivity, but low as a substitute for a Canadian regulated banking relationship. An offshore electronic-money institution may provide collection, conversion, or payout services only if its licence, jurisdiction, permitted activities, correspondent-bank arrangements, and Canadian regulatory perimeter are acceptable. It cannot rely on offshore status to avoid Canadian FINTRAC registration where it conducts money-services-business activities in Canada, and it may trigger Canadian payment-service-provider requirements where it performs regulated retail payment activities in Canada. The entity should use regulated banks or licensed payment providers for client-money safeguarding, maintain documented settlement and reconciliation controls, avoid unlicensed pass-through structures, and obtain legal confirmation on solicitation, agent, outsourcing, consumer disclosure, privacy, sanctions, and data-localization issues. Offshore accounts should not receive consumer remittance funds unless the institution, flow, safeguarding model, and contractual allocation of regulatory responsibility have been approved by counsel and the principal Canadian banking partners.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Route shareholder equity, paid-up capital, and shareholder loans through an account held in the Canadian corporation's legal name at a Canadian regulated bank, using SWIFT MT103 or the bank's ISO 20022 equivalent with complete originator, beneficiary, intermediary, purpose, and invoice or agreement data. Use a clear purpose such as equity subscription, paid-up capital, or shareholder loan; do not describe operating receipts as capital. Provide the bank with board-approved subscription or loan documentation, corporate resolutions, beneficial-owner information, source-of-funds and source-of-wealth evidence, investor identity, foreign-exchange conversion instructions, and expected transaction profile before the wire is sent. Reconcile the incoming amount to the share register, general ledger, loan schedule, and bank statement. For foreign direct investment, assess Investment Canada Act review requirements, sanctions, tax withholding, thin-capitalization and transfer-pricing consequences, and any reporting requirements associated with related-party loans. The bank may hold or reject funds pending enhanced due diligence, especially where the remitter is a non-resident, the payment is routed through multiple intermediaries, or the source jurisdiction is high risk.Use Canadian bank wire transfers through SWIFT MT103 or the applicable ISO 20022 customer-credit-transfer message for approved supplier, agent, correspondent, payroll, refund, and beneficiary settlements. Maintain complete payment data, including payer, beneficiary, ultimate beneficiary where applicable, invoice or remittance reference, purpose, currency, amount, corridor, and sanctions-screening outcome. Commercial letters of credit may be used for genuine goods or service procurement where documentary controls are commercially appropriate; the issuing bank, confirming bank, advising bank, and documents must be independently verified, and the transaction must not disguise consumer remittance settlement or prohibited third-party flows. For local Canadian rails, use Payments Canada-supported EFT, Interac-supported commercial arrangements, or bank-provided real-time and batch settlement products only through an eligible regulated participant. Cash-pickup settlements should be prefunded or collateralized under written agent agreements, subject to per-transaction and daily limits, beneficiary identification, payout confirmation, exception handling, and end-of-day reconciliation. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium for technology and payment-rail connectivity, but low as a substitute for a Canadian regulated banking relationship. An offshore electronic-money institution may provide collection, conversion, or payout services only if its licence, jurisdiction, permitted activities, correspondent-bank arrangements, and Canadian regulatory perimeter are acceptable. It cannot rely on offshore status to avoid Canadian FINTRAC registration where it conducts money-services-business activities in Canada, and it may trigger Canadian payment-service-provider requirements where it performs regulated retail payment activities in Canada. The entity should use regulated banks or licensed payment providers for client-money safeguarding, maintain documented settlement and reconciliation controls, avoid unlicensed pass-through structures, and obtain legal confirmation on solicitation, agent, outsourcing, consumer disclosure, privacy, sanctions, and data-localization issues. Offshore accounts should not receive consumer remittance funds unless the institution, flow, safeguarding model, and contractual allocation of regulatory responsibility have been approved by counsel and the principal Canadian banking partners.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined (15% federal general corporate rate + 11.5% Ontario general corporate rate)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act, imposed on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder. Ontario does not impose a separate dividend withholding tax. Treaty rates commonly reduce the rate to 15%, or to 5% where the beneficial owner is a corporation holding at least 10% voting control (e.g., Canada–US Treaty).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 in total fair market value of non-arm's length transactions with non-resident related parties triggers the mandatory contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation obligation and T106 filing. Multinational enterprises with consolidated group revenue of CAD 750 million or more are additionally subject to Country-by-Country Reporting under the OECD BEPS standard.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not apply the Cayman/Caribbean Economic Substance Regulations regime. However, CRA treats economic substance as critical: a Canadian-incorporated entity or permanent establishment that lacks real business purpose, payroll, physical assets, or decision-making presence in Canada is vulnerable to General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) reassessment, treaty shopping challenges, and transfer pricing reallocation. For money transmission services, holding the relevant FINTRAC money services business registration and maintaining Canadian compliance staff are key substance indicators.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraudulent Return

Statutory sanction: Gross negligence penalty under Income Tax Act s. 163(2) equal to 50% of understated tax; conviction under s. 239(1) carries a fine up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for up to five years. CRA can also impose third-party civil penalties for promoting fraudulent tax arrangements.

Director personal liability: CRA can pierce the corporate veil where directors knowingly authorized fraudulent filings; directors are jointly and severally liable for corporate tax debts in cases of conspiracy or fraudulent conveyance, and may face criminal imprisonment and foreign travel restrictions as a condition of judicial release.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance and Thin Capitalisation

Statutory sanction: CRA may re-assess to an arm's length amount under s. 247(2); a transfer-pricing penalty of 10% of the net adjustment applies where the adjustment exceeds threshold and documentation is inadequate. Late T106 filings attract daily penalties. Interest accrues on all reassessed amounts.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly approve false transfer-pricing documentation can be liable for civil fraud and are jointly and severally liable with the corporation for withheld taxes under s. 227.1; in egregious cases, wilful participation in a sham can expose directors to criminal prosecution and travel bans.
Unlicensed / Unregistered Money Services Business

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, administrative monetary penalties of up to CAD 500,000 can be imposed per violation; Criminal Code money laundering (s. 462.31) is an indictable offense punishable by up to ten years' imprisonment. FINTRAC may suspend or revoke the MSB registration and freeze bank settlements.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and compliance officers who knowingly fail to implement AML/CFT controls may be charged as principals or accomplices; personal fines, seizure of personal assets, prohibition from managing corporations, and cross-border travel bans may be ordered pre-trial or as sentencing conditions.
Shell Company and Beneficial Ownership Concealment

Statutory sanction: GAAR under s. 245 can disallow all tax benefits; CRA and FINTRAC exchange beneficial ownership data under CRS/FATCA; civil penalties for failure to disclose UBO include CAD 500,000 FINTRAC AMPs; a shell structure may be disregarded and all Canadian-source profit reattributed to the Ontario permanent establishment.

Director personal liability: Courts may lift the corporate veil and hold shadow directors personally liable for Canadian tax debts plus restitution; travel bans may be imposed under mutual enforcement agreements, and willful concealment of beneficial ownership triggers criminal fines and imprisonment under the Criminal Code and the Income Tax Act.
Failure to Withhold and Remit Part XIII Tax

Statutory sanction: The payer is liable for the full Part XIII tax plus interest. Penalties under s. 227 of the Income Tax Act can reach 10% of the amount not remitted. CRA can collect the unpaid amount by garnishing the corporation's bank accounts or taking a security interest in assets.

Director personal liability: Section 227.1 makes directors jointly and severally liable for unremitted Part XIII withholding taxes if the corporation fails to pay after CRA has issued a Minister's Certificate. This liability is not dischargeable through corporate bankruptcy; negligent directors can face civil restitution orders, personal asset seizure, and travel bans while leaving Canada is forbidden until tax matters are settled.
