Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Swiss Federal Tax Administration (Eidgenössische Steuerverwaltung ESTV / Administration fédérale des contributions AFC) — official portal estv.admin.ch

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity establishing a Swiss Aktiengesellschaft (AG) must first be entered in the Swiss commercial register; the cantonal tax authority and the Swiss Federal Tax Administration subsequently allocate the company a Swiss UID (Unternehmens-Identifikationsnummer / Identificateur entreprises) and, if applicable, a separate VAT number (MWST/Ust-ID). For corporate income tax the AG registers with the cantonal tax authority of its registered seat. VAT registration is filed electronically with ESTV through the 'eVAT' online portal using the UID. Foreign entities not yet incorporated may obtain a provisional UID and must provide apostilled incorporation documents, a registered office address, and details of the beneficial owner and authorised signatories.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box election exists under Swiss law. A Swiss AG is mandatorily subject to entity-level federal and cantonal income tax. Available statutory elections include participation deduction for dividend and capital gain income, VAT group consolidation under Art. 12 VAT Act, partial realisation of hidden reserves upon relocation or transfer, and the election for privileged status under certain cantonal tax laws (historically for mixed, domiciliary, or holding companies). Hybrid mismatch elections are not available under Swiss domestic law; Switzerland relies on OECD BEPS recommendations and minimum standards.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

8.1%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CHF 100,000 annual worldwide turnover from taxable supplies (excluding exempt and genuine export supplies) per Art. 10 VAT Act; lower thresholds apply for certain domestic supplies from non-residents and for EU-style distance selling situations.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rated export treatment applies to the supply of goods transported by the seller or buyer to a destination outside Switzerland, as evidenced by Swiss customs documentation, and to certain services directly connected to exported goods or rendered abroad. The supplier must produce a valid customs export declaration, transport documents showing departure from Switzerland, and the commercial invoice. For wholesale cement/clinker exports in bulk vessels, zero-rating requires that the physical goods leave the Swiss customs territory and that the counterparty is a taxable person established outside Switzerland.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Swiss electronic export declaration via NCTS/ECOTEC)
  • Bill of Lading (B/L) issued by the ocean carrier
  • Commercial Invoice
  • Packing List
  • Proof of actual departure and third-country arrival (port of destination evidence)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or foreign currency receipt evidence confirming export consideration

Switzerland does not implement real-time fiscal clearance or digital stamping. E-invoicing is not mandatory for domestic B2B transactions, although the Swiss QR-bill standard is required for most open invoices in paper or PDF format. For exports, electronic invoices must contain the required VAT particulars and be retained for at least 10 years. No API-based mandatory clearance system exists.

Swiss customs and tax authorities perform focused audits of export zero-rating claims. If export substantiation is incomplete or false, the supply is reclassified as taxable, VAT is clawed back with statutory interest, and fines may be imposed up to three times the evaded tax for wilful violation. Criminal fraud prosecutions may carry custodial sentences under the Swiss Criminal Code, and the VAT Act prescribes separate monetary penalties for negligent and wilful infringements.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Switzerland maintains full convertibility on both the current account and the capital account. The Swiss franc is freely floating and there are no restrictions on current account payments, trade settlements, dividends, interest, royalties, or repatriation of share capital and liquidation proceeds. No prior exchange control authorization is required for capital inflows or outflows, and profit remittance is subject only to ordinary withholding tax (Verrechnungssteuer) at 35% unless reduced or eliminated under an applicable double tax treaty or the Swiss participation relief rules. Non-resident investors may freely hold and transfer CHF and foreign currency accounts in Switzerland, provided the underlying funds are of documented lawful origin and the transaction complies with AML/KYC requirements.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Switzerland imposes no statutory surrender or mandatory conversion requirements on export receipts. There is no legal timeline requiring the conversion of exported settlement currencies into Swiss francs, and exporters may retain foreign currency balances in Swiss-based accounts or deposit them with banks. SNB monetary policy operates through interest rates and intervention in the foreign exchange market rather than through exchange controls. Consequently, the export channel for Portland clinker and hydraulic cement wholesale transactions can include invoicing and settlement in EUR, USD, CHF, or any mutually acceptable convertible currency, with no forced conversion or compulsory remittance timeline.

Central bank supervision: Swiss National Bank (SNB) acting as the central bank and monetary authority; foreign exchange administration functions are discharged by the SNB together with the State Secretariat for International Finance (SIF) at the Federal Department of Finance.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Foreign fintech electronic money institutions (EMIs) and payment services providers face significant legal constraints in Switzerland. Under the Swiss Financial Institutions Act (FinIA) and Anti-Money Laundering Act (AMLA), any entity carrying out payment transactions or issuing e-money for commercial purposes must hold a FINMA license. A foreign EMI without a Swiss branch license may not provide payment accounts to Swiss-domiciled corporations. The viability of an offshore digital EMI as the primary treasury rail is therefore low. It may be used only as a secondary execution venue for a limited corridor, provided the EMI is authorized in an EEA jurisdiction equivalent to Swiss prudential rules, is registered with FINMA for cross-border activity, and does not become the main repository of corporate funds. Banks in Switzerland are generally reluctant to accept transfers from unlicensed digital EMIs; therefore, counterparty and clearing risk is materially reduced by relying on licensed Swiss banks for all principal liquidity operations.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Capital contributions to the CH_AG must be routed through the Swiss banking system using the beneficiary's IBAN in Switzerland. The transfer should be made by SWIFT MT103 from the shareholder's bank account, with the payment instruction clearly indicating the corporate name of the CH_AG, its IBAN, and the purpose of the payment. The SNB and Swiss payments infrastructure (SIC system) do not prescribe a formal purpose code for capital injections, but banks require the beneficiary to declare the economic purpose on the account credit advice or via an external attested capital contribution form. For share capital subscribed under the articles of association, the bank may require a copy of the notarized capital payment confirmation, particularly when a Capital Escrow Account is used pending registration of the capital increase in the commercial register. For capital above CHF 1 million, the bank will generally request the source-of-funds documentation, beneficial ownership confirmation, and evidence of the corporate resolution authorizing the contribution. Transmissions should avoid third-party intermediary accounts where possible; all inbound transfers must comply with the bank's sanctions screening and SWIFT messaging standards, including the remitter's full name, account number, and address.Outbound trade payments to suppliers of bulk hydraulic cement, clinker, and ocean vessel freight services are settled primarily via SWIFT MT103 wire transfers for immediate value transactions, or through documentary commercial letters of credit where the supplier requires payment security. A commercial LC issued by a Swiss bank can be established in favor of the foreign seller subject to presentation of compliant documents (commercial invoice, bill of lading, certificate of origin, weight certificate, and insurance documents). For intra-European payments, the Swiss SIC and euroSIC systems allow settlement in CHF and EUR respectively, with SEPA-compliant direct transfers for EUR-denominated payments within the EEA. For non-EEA settlement, SWIFT MT103 with dedicated bank-to-bank reimbursement instructions is the standard rail. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal direct tax statutory rate 8.5% on net profit after tax (approx. 7.83% pre-tax); combined federal, cantonal and communal headline rate varies from approx. 11.5% (canton Zug) to 21% (canton Geneva). For a single-tier stock corporation domiciled in the Canton of Zurich, the combined headline rate is approximately 18.0%.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

35% Swiss withholding tax (Verrechnungssteuer) on gross dividend distributions from a Swiss resident AG, including constructive dividends and liquidation proceeds paid to shareholders.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

Tax return must be filed within 6 months after the fiscal year-end (e.g. by 30 June for a 31 December balance sheet date); cantons may prescribe an earlier or later date, commonly between 31 May and 30 September. Upon written request, an extension of 3 months is routinely granted. Interest and procedural sanctions apply from the expiry of the initial deadline.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Mandatory transfer pricing documentation (Local File) is required when the Swiss entity belongs to a multinational group whose consolidated annual turnover exceeds CHF 50 million and the Swiss entity's related-party transactions are economically material; Master File is required simultaneously with Group File for groups exceeding CHF 50 million consolidated turnover. Country-by-Country Report (CbCR) is mandatory when the group's consolidated annual revenue reaches CHF 900 million. Below these thresholds, tax authorities may still demand full documentation upon request if cross-border transactions with related parties exceed one million Swiss francs or where prima facie arm's-length doubt exists.

Economic substance scope: Switzerland does not statutorily apply offshore Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) in the BVI/Cayman style, but it enforces economic substance through three channels: (i) Art. 8 DBG judicial anti-avoidance doctrine (implicit economic substance doctrine), which disregards artificial structures lacking bona fide commercial rationale; (ii) the Swiss tax authority audit practice requiring employees, premises, management decisions, entrepreneurial risk-taking and control functions in Switzerland before treaty benefits or holding privileges are granted; (iii) the arm's-length standard under Art. 58(4) DBG imposing a real functionality and risk analysis for all related-party transactions. Since 1 January 2020, the Swiss Tax Reform and AHV Financing Act (TRAF) abolished last-standing preferential hydrocarbon regimes and introduced the patent box and R&D super-deduction only for entities demonstrating substantive development activity. A cement wholesale distribution company must therefore maintain physical logistics management, trading personnel, pricing decisions, and risk-bearing capacity inside Switzerland; a mere invoice-shell without staff or premises will be recharacterized and denied treaty and domestic tax benefits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Tax Fraud

Statutory sanction: Ordinary tax evasion carries a penalty equal to 25%-50% (minor negligence) or 100%-200% of the evaded federal tax under Art. 175-176 DBG; aggravated tax fraud (Steuerbetrug) under Art. 186 DBG carries imprisonment up to three years and a fine of up to CHF 250,000, in addition to full restitution of principal tax, 4.5% default interest, and possible forfeiture of illegally obtained transport infrastructure benefits. On the cantonal level, parallel penalties of up to 50% of the evaded cantonal tax are added.

Director personal liability: Directors, executive officers and liquidators may be held jointly and severally liable under Art. 754-760 of the Swiss Code of Obligations (OR) to compensate the distressed entity and the State; they face garnishment of private assets, travel bans by insolvency authorities (in cases of bankruptcy), disqualification from board mandates, and imprisonment in the presence of fraudulent bankruptcy or false business certification (Art. 163, 165, 152 Swiss Criminal Code).
Transfer Pricing Mispricing

Statutory sanction: The Swiss tax authority applies a transfer pricing adjustment under Art. 58(4) DBG, re-assessing the Swiss taxable net profit; additionally, the mispriced amount may be recharacterized as a hidden profit distribution subject to 35% Swiss WHT (Art. 20 and 24 Swiss Withholding Tax Law). Administrative fines up to CHF 10,000 for documentation failure apply; in severe and wilful cases, the criminal evasion penalties for tax fraud supplement the civil adjustment.

Director personal liability: Directors approving the non-arm's-length contracts bear civil liability for restitution of the underpaid tax and resulting interest, unless they can prove diligent care; in cases of transnational profit-shifting schemes, directors can be prosecuted for aiding and abetting Swiss tax fraud, triggering 6-month to 3-year custodial sentences under Art. 186 DBG, and the matching OECD/MLI criminal co-operation may enable extradition, cross-border asset freezing, and travel bans.
Shell Company and Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: Swiss anti-avoidance doctrines (Art. 8 DBG) allow the tax agency to set aside the arrangement and tax the profits where economic substance is present. Additionally, the company may lose DTA benefits under the principal purpose test (PPT) of Art. 29(9) OECD Model as implemented in Swiss treaty practice. Retroactive assessments can cover 10 years, and the deemed distributed profits are subject to 35% WHT plus penalties; the commercial register may strike the entity for violation of substantive legal existence (Art. 13 OR) resulting in liquidation and forfeiture of registered activity rights.

Director personal liability: Founding directors and ultimate beneficial owners face personal liability for the entity's debts and taxes due to fraudulent organization under Art. 754 OR; on the criminal front, false commercial registry declarations violate Art. 152 Swiss Criminal Code and art. 232 CC, with imprisonment up to three years. Administrative travel bans and asset blocking can be imposed under international mutual legal assistance procedures, particularly when treaty abuse causes double non-taxation.
AML and Cross-Border Sanctions Breach

Statutory sanction: Violation of the Swiss Anti-Money Laundering Law (AMLA) triggers an administrative fine of up to CHF 500,000 per act under Art. 72 AMLA; knowing participation in or promotion of money laundering under Art. 305bis Swiss Criminal Code carries imprisonment up to three years and a fine; aggravated cases (criminal organization, professional laundering) increase the custodial sentence to five years. FINMA may order immediate asset freezes, bar directors from responsible functions, and impose involuntary liquidation of the commercial vehicle.

Director personal liability: Directors fulfilling compliance officer functions are personally prosecuted for negligent breach of AMLA obligations; they face criminal conviction, restitution orders, confiscation of personal assets and equivalence of travel ban under the International Mutual Assistance in Criminal Matters. In cases of fraudulent money movement tied to the sale of construction materials, the personal liability is not shielded by the corporate veil because the directors knowingly effectuated the payments, and Swiss criminal courts routinely pierce the AG to expose the natural persons behind the transactions.
