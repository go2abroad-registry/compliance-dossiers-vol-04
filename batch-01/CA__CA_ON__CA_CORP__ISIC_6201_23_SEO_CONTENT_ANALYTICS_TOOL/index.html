Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) via Business Registration Online portal (https://www.canada.ca/en/services/taxes/business/business-number.html); Ontario's provincial corporate tax is administered by the CRA under a federal-provincial collection agreement.

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity conducting business in Canada must file Form RC1, 'Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts', online or by mail to the CRA. If the entity is a non-resident without a Canadian establishment, use the non-resident registration process (Form RC276, 'Request for a Business Number and Program Accounts – Non-Resident'), accompanied by the certificate of corporate existence and proof of the entity's registered address. The CRA issues a nine-digit Canadian Business Number (BN), which serves as the company's tax identification number; separate program accounts must be opened for GST/HST, payroll, corporate income tax, and import/export activities as applicable.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a U.S.-style 'check-the-box' election. A corporation incorporated under Canadian law is a resident of Canada and subject to federal Part I tax and Ontario corporate tax on worldwide income, subject to applicable tax treaties. Non-resident corporations may make an election under section 216 of the Income Tax Act to pay tax on rental income from Canadian real property, or under section 219 to establish a Canadian branch with a branch tax allocation. Hybrid mismatch rules under sections 18.2 and 18.21, in force for taxation years commencing on or after July 1, 2022, apply based on the legal characterization of entities under Canadian law; no elective reclassification is available for hybrid entities.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in taxable worldwide revenue from Canadian supplies in any single calendar quarter or in a trailing 12-month period; non-resident digital service providers must register under the simplified non-resident GST/HST regime once their Canadian-source digital service consideration exceeds this threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

An exporter may apply the GST/HST zero rate (0%) to: (i) goods exported from Canada, if the exporter retains documentary evidence that the goods are exported within a prescribed time; (ii) services rendered to a non-resident, provided the service is not performed primarily in Canada for the benefit of a non-resident, and is not related to real property situated in Canada or tangible personal property that remains in Canada; (iii) software, data, and analytics services supplied to non-residents may be zero-rated as intangible personal property or services where the above conditions are satisfied. For digital services supplied by non-resident providers to Canadian consumers, the ordinary place-of-supply rules apply; exported Canadian-origin services may still be zero-rated if properly substantiated.

  • Canadian Customs Export Declaration (below Canada Border Services Agency threshold or a Form B13 for valued exports)
  • Bill of Lading or other verified transport document
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or documentary proof of receipt of consideration from the foreign buyer in the seller's bank account

No real-time electronic invoice clearance or digital stamping requirement is currently imposed at the federal level. GST/HST invoices must contain prescribed information under the Excise Tax Act; electronic invoices are permitted if in a readable format and integrity is maintained. The CRA may require electronic records in audit, but no continuous transaction controls (CTC) or government-specified e-invoicing platform is mandated.

CRA may deny zero-rating and assess unreported GST/HST, interest, and penalties. Knowingly making, or participating in the making of, false statements can trigger a gross negligence penalty of 25% of the amount assessed, and in exceptional cases criminal prosecution under the Excise Tax Act and Criminal Code. If a zero-rating claim is disallowed after refund issuance, the taxpayer must repay the refund and pay the underlying tax, interest accruing from the original receivable date, and penalties of up to CAD 8,000 or double the amount of tax sought to be evaded; voluntary disclosure may mitigate penalties but does not eliminate interest.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible on both current and capital accounts under Canada's IMF Article VIII obligations. Profit remittance is not exchange-controlled; dividends, royalties, interest and management fees may be remitted freely once Canadian withholding tax and tax compliance are satisfied. Practical hurdles are limited to standard banking CDD, sanctions screening, tax withholding under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act, and documentary evidence of the underlying commercial relationship.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement and no mandatory conversion timeline or ratio. Exporters may hold foreign currency proceeds offshore or in Canadian-dollar or foreign-currency accounts; conversion takes place at prevailing market rates, and supporting commercial documents must be preserved for tax and AML source-of-funds verification.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), with FINTRAC as Canada's financial intelligence unit for AML/PFT supervision; Canada has no dedicated Foreign Exchange Administration.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Offshore digital EMIs have Medium-to-Low viability as primary treasury rails. They require FINTRAC registration as foreign money services businesses, a Canadian settlement account, and full CDD before transacting with regulated Canadian banks. Payment facilitators such as Stripe and Paddle have High viability for revenue collection but cannot substitute for a Canadian corporate operating account. Direct payroll and material supplier disbursements should use a tier-1 Canadian banking relationship.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined general corporate income tax rate: 15% federal plus 11.5% Ontario. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally obtain a 12.2% combined rate on up to CAD 500,000 of eligible active business income: 9% federal plus 3.2% Ontario, subject to the federal business limit, associated-corporation allocation, passive-income grind, specified investment-business rules, and other eligibility conditions. A non-resident-controlled corporation is generally not a CCPC.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Canadian Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident, subject to an applicable tax treaty or other statutory exemption. Common treaty outcomes include 5% for qualifying corporate ownership and 15% for other dividend recipients under the Canada-U.S. treaty; exact rates depend on the recipient, ownership percentage, beneficial ownership, limitation-on-benefits provisions, and treaty jurisdiction.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due six months after the end of every taxation year. Any balance is generally due two months after year-end; a qualifying CCPC may receive a three-month balance-due period if the statutory conditions are satisfied. Corporate income tax instalments are due monthly unless a valid quarterly-instalment exception applies.

Deadline: · Frequency:

An Ontario corporation generally files its corporate annual return within six months after the end of its taxation year. A federal corporation generally files its annual return within 60 days after its anniversary date of incorporation, amalgamation, or continuance.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general statutory CAD threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is unnecessary. Under Income Tax Act section 247, contemporaneous documentation must support arm's-length pricing and be provided to CRA within three months of a written request. T106 reporting generally applies when aggregate reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents exceed CAD 1,000,000. Canadian local-file and master-file concepts are not imposed as a universal OECD-style filing requirement, but CRA may require detailed records. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to a Canadian constituent entity of an MNE group with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone general Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to some offshore regimes. Substance remains material through the transfer-pricing rules, the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty beneficial-ownership and purpose tests, foreign-affiliate and FAPI rules, interest-limitation rules, sham-transaction principles, and common-law anti-avoidance doctrines. A Canadian company should maintain real decision-making, personnel or appropriately evidenced outsourced functions, risk control, commercial contracts, IP-development evidence, adequate capitalization, books and records, and bank-account authority proportionate to its MarTech SaaS activities. A conduit or nominal service company may be denied deductions, treaty benefits, or the asserted allocation of profit.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and false tax reporting

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act section 239 may impose a fine generally ranging from CAD 1,000 to CAD 25,000 plus 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, with imprisonment of up to two years for the principal offence; aggravated offences may carry longer imprisonment. Reassessments, arrears interest, gross-negligence penalties, and denial of deductions or credits may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for corporate income tax, but knowingly participating in an offence can create personal criminal exposure. The corporation remains liable for tax, interest, penalties, and restitution. A court may impose probation, restitution, or restrictions; travel restrictions are not an automatic tax penalty and generally require separate judicial authority.
Failure to withhold and remit non-resident tax

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for the unwithheld Part XIII amount, arrears interest, and penalties. Failure-to-deduct or failure-to-remit penalties can reach 10% of the amount not deducted or remitted, and 20% for repeated or grossly negligent failures; failure to file information returns may produce additional penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal liability for source deductions and remittances under the Income Tax Act if the corporation failed to remit and the statutory due-diligence defence is unavailable. Liability can continue after resignation for the statutory limitation period, subject to statutory conditions.
Transfer-pricing misstatement

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess under section 247, deny or adjust deductions, impute income, apply a 10% transfer-pricing penalty to the amount of a qualifying adjustment where the statutory conditions are met, and charge interest. T106 omissions can generate separate penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure for knowingly false records, obstruction, or participation in evasion. Ordinary corporate transfer-pricing reassessments do not by themselves pierce the corporate veil.
Shell company and artificial treaty conduit

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny treaty benefits, apply the principal-purpose or beneficial-ownership analysis where applicable, invoke the general anti-avoidance rule, recharacterize transactions, deny expenses, and assess tax, interest, and penalties. Corporate-law remedies may apply for misleading records or improper conduct.

Director personal liability: Directors can face civil claims for breach of fiduciary duties, statutory offences, and criminal exposure if they knowingly implement fraudulent arrangements. Veil piercing is exceptional and requires more than low substance or limited liability alone.
Unreported foreign affiliates and offshore accounts

Statutory sanction: T1134, T1135, T106, and related penalties may apply, including daily penalties for late or incomplete filings. Income may be reassessed under FAPI rules, foreign tax credits may be denied, and gross-negligence or criminal penalties may apply for deliberate concealment.

Director personal liability: Personal liability may arise for knowing participation in false filings or obstruction. Directors may also face derivative corporate claims and, in serious criminal matters, prosecution and court-ordered restrictions.
GST/HST and indirect-tax non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Assessment of unremitted GST/HST, interest, late-filing penalties, gross-negligence penalties, and denial of input tax credits. Platform and non-resident digital-economy rules may apply depending on customer location, contractual seller, and supply characterization.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unremitted GST/HST under the Excise Tax Act where statutory conditions are met and the due-diligence defence fails.
AML, sanctions, and proceeds-of-crime exposure

Statutory sanction: Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act violations may attract administrative monetary penalties and, for serious offences, substantial fines and imprisonment. Criminal Code offences may include laundering proceeds, fraud, and participation in a criminal organization. Canadian sanctions breaches may attract prosecution and asset-freezing or seizure measures.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorize, direct, or knowingly facilitate prohibited conduct may be prosecuted personally. Asset restraint, forfeiture, restitution, and court-imposed release or travel conditions may arise; a travel ban is not automatic.
Corporate records and beneficial-ownership failure

Statutory sanction: Federal and Ontario corporate statutes may impose offences, fines, compliance orders, dissolution or cancellation risk, and access-to-information or beneficial-ownership consequences. False tax or financial records may trigger additional Income Tax Act or Criminal Code liability.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be personally prosecuted for authorizing false filings or failing to maintain required records. Civil veil piercing remains exceptional but fraud, abuse of corporate personality, and statutory personal-liability provisions can defeat ordinary limited-liability protection.
Improper profit extraction and shareholder benefit

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny deductions, assess shareholder benefits, apply shareholder-loan inclusion rules, impose Part XIII withholding, assess corporate and personal tax, and charge interest and penalties. Improper capital dividends may trigger a penalty tax.

Director personal liability: Recipients may incur personal tax and repayment obligations. Directors may face fiduciary-duty claims, oppression remedies, or personal liability where they authorized unlawful distributions or knowingly participated in misconduct.
