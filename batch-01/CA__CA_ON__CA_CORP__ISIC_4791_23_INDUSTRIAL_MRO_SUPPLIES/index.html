Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) via Business Registration Online (BRO) portal and CRA My Business Account; Ontario-level filings are administered jointly by CRA under the federal-provincial tax collection agreement, with a distinct Ontario identification obligation for extra-provincial registration under the Ontario Corporations Information Act.

E-government filing portal

For a foreign-incorporated corporation carrying on business in Ontario: 1) File extra-provincial registration with Ontario's Companies and Personal Property Security Branch (Form 3) to obtain the Ontario Corporation Number and demonstrate legal presence. 2) Register with CRA through Business Registration Online or Form RC1/RC1A to obtain the 9-digit federal Business Number (BN) and activate the required program accounts: RC account (corporate income tax), RT account (GST/HST), RP account (payroll, if employees), and RM account (import/export, for customs purposes). 3) For non-resident corporations, provide the legal name, articles/constitutive documents, board resolution appointing a Canadian resident representative for service, and a complete Canadian address or address of agent. 4) CRA will assess whether the corporation has 'carrying on business in Canada' nexus; GST/HST registration becomes effective on the date the CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold is exceeded or on the first taxable supply, whichever is earlier. 5) No separate Ontario corporate income tax registration is required because the CRA administers Ontario provincial corporate income tax, but Ontario-specific accounts (e.g., Employer Health Tax) are registered separately through ONT-TAXS only if applicable.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box election exists; every business corporation is a separate taxpayer. A Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) can claim the federal and Ontario small business deduction on active business income up to the CAD 500,000 business limit, subject to grind-down rules. Under the Excise Tax Act, a GST/HST registrant may elect (section 156) to treat certain taxable supplies between closely related corporations as made for nil consideration, and may request an earlier effective GST/HST registration date. Non-resident corporations may elect under Income Tax Act section 216 to file a net-basis return for Canadian rental income, and section 217 elections apply for certain Canadian-source payments. Hybrid mismatch and excessive interest/financing deduction rules (ITA sections 18.4 to 18.7 and the earnings-stripping limit) circumscribe available elective structures; no flow-through pass-through taxation is available to corporations.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies over any trailing four consecutive calendar quarters (small supplier threshold); once exceeded, GST/HST registration is mandatory and the registrant is liable on all taxable supplies made in Canada.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available under sections 22 and 23 of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act for supplies of goods exported directly by the registrant or delivered to a common carrier for shipment outside Canada. MRO industrial supplies sold through an electronic catalogue to non-resident B2B customers qualify as zero-rated if the registrant retains evidence proving export within a reasonable period (commercial goods should normally be exported within 60 days of supply). Services provided to non-residents in respect of the goods (e.g., logistics coordination, catalog licensing) are zero-rated only if the service is delivered to a non-resident and is not used in Canada; mere e-commerce platform access is zero-rated as an exported intangible if the recipient is outside Canada and the use is wholly outside Canada.

  • Customs Export Declaration (B13A electronically filed through GEMS/CAED when goods exceed CAD 2,000)
  • Bill of Lading (ocean, air, or truck waybill evidencing physical movement of goods outside Canada) and commercial invoice/sales contract
  • Inward Remittance / SWIFT wire payment confirmation or equivalent foreign-exchange settlement evidence demonstrating the sale was made to a non-resident and consideration was received in the normal course

No real-time fiscal clearance, API digital stamping, or mandatory B2B e-invoicing clearance is currently imposed by CRA. Electronic invoices and records are accepted if complete, legible, and in English or French; exporters of goods from Canada must file an electronic export declaration through the Canadian Export Reporting System (GEMS/CAED) when the value of goods exceeds CAD 2,000, with the Border Export Declaration (B13A) transmitted to the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA). GST/HST returns are net-filed electronically via GST/HST NETFILE or through third-party software, on a monthly, quarterly, or annual basis depending on revenue level. CRA is developing a pan-Canadian B2B e-invoicing framework to align with future PEPPOL interoperability, but as at the current law no clearance mandate applies.

CRA auditors scrutinize export zero-rating claims by matching the B13A declaration, bill of lading, and payment evidence; if documentation is incomplete or the goods are subsequently re-imported or diverted to the Canadian market, CRA will assess HST at 13% on the supply, disallow related input tax credits, and charge arrears interest from the date of the return. False statements attract the Excise Tax Act penalty equal to the greater of CAD 250 and 25% of the net tax amount, with gross negligence penalties and criminal prosecution for wilful evasion (fines and imprisonment are available). The normal reassessment period is four years from the filing date, and export documentation must be retained for six years; failure to substantiate zero-rating on a retroactive basis exposes the exporter to full HST clawback plus penalties.

Stage 04

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates under a floating exchange rate regime with full convertibility on both current and capital accounts. There are no exchange controls restricting capital inflows or outflows. Profit remittance in the form of dividends, interest, royalties, or management fees is permissible without prior regulatory approval; foreign exchange is freely purchased and sold at market rates. Statutory restrictions are limited to FATF/AML transaction reporting thresholds and, for cross-border payments over CAD 10,000, mandatory FINTRAC reporting. Withholding tax implications under Part XIII of the Canadian Income Tax Act apply to distributions and cross-border payments, but these are tax compliance measures rather than FX controls.

Export proceeds surrender rules

In Canada, there is no statutory obligation to surrender export proceeds, no mandatory conversion into Canadian dollars, and no prescribed timeline for repatriation. Export proceeds may be retained in foreign currency accounts at authorized financial institutions. Canadian businesses must report international electronic funds transfers (EFTs) of CAD 10,000 or more to FINTRAC within prescribed timeframes, and bank wiring instructions must include appropriate SWIFT purpose codes. Proceeds may be converted to CAD at any time at the prevailing interbank spot rate; optional forward hedging contracts are available from Schedule I banks.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); foreign exchange policy coordinated with the Department of Finance Canada and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium feasibility. Offshore digital EMI/neobanks that are not licensed in Canada cannot act as primary settlement institutions for CAD or be recognized as deposit-taking institutions. They may support cross-border payouts through virtual IBANs and SWIFT clearing, but legal capacity is restricted by the Bank Act and the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act. Such entities must register as Money Service Businesses (MSB) with FINTRAC and, if operational in Ontario, comply with the Financial Services Regulatory Authority of Ontario (FSRA) registration. Their inability to accept 'deposits' unless licensed as a Schedule I Canadian bank creates a structural constraint for long-term treasury operations; therefore, they are best used as secondary settlement rails rather than primary banking partners.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

For inbound capital injection into Canada, use SWIFT MT103/202 through the correspondent banking network. The remitting bank must include the Canadian corporate account in SWIFT BIC plus the CA routing code. Compliance purpose codes must reflect 'Foreign Direct Investment – Equity Capital Contra' or, where a shareholder loan is used, 'Intercompany Loan – Subordinate and Unsecured'. Verification at recipient side is performed through the bank's transaction monitoring system and if the aggregate amount qualifies, the bank will file a FINTRAC Large Cash Transaction Report or Cross-Border Currency Report as required. CAD proceeds may be converted outside any central control at the prevailing spot. The recipient bank must receive a board resolution authorizing the capital injection, the executed subscription agreement, and registration of the entity's certificate of incorporation to ensure the capital is accepted into the capital account.Trade settlement for industrial MRO supplies should be executed using the SWIFT MT103 wire transfer for open account payments and supplier settlement; commercial letters of credit (LC) under Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits (UCP 600) settle larger transactions where seller confirmation is required. For B2B e-commerce order disbursement, international card networks (Visa/Mastercard) integrate with the same treasury accounts via acquiring bank settlement under CA-ON operations. Bulk disbursement across multiple suppliers can use SWIFT MT101 through a proprietary multi-currency clearing hub at a Schedule I bank (e.g., RBC Global, TD Treasury Services) or a commercial payment gateway conforming to the Canadian Payments Association model. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Documentation required if total value of transactions with non-residents exceeds CAD 1 million for services or CAD 5 million for other transactions (e.g., goods, intangibles) in a fiscal year. Master File required if total revenue exceeds CAD 50 million and cross-border transactions exceed CAD 5 million.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a specific Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime like the UAE, but applies the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) under Section 245 of the Income Tax Act. Transactions lacking economic substance and solely for tax avoidance may be recharacterized. Additionally, the CRA may apply the 'substance over form' doctrine and transfer pricing rules to ensure arm's length outcomes.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under the Income Tax Act (Section 239): fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded, imprisonment up to 5 years, and mandatory court order to file correct returns. Civil penalties: 50% gross negligence penalty on understated tax.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for corporate tax debts if they fail to withhold and remit source deductions (Section 227.1). For tax evasion, directors can face criminal charges if they knowingly participated. Civil restitution, travel bans, and imprisonment up to 5 years.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA may apply GAAR to deny treaty benefits, reassess income, and impose penalties of 25% of the tax benefit (Section 245). Additionally, transfer pricing penalties up to 10% of the net adjustment if documentation is inadequate.

Director personal liability: Directors may be assessed for penalties if they authorized or acquiesced to the arrangement. Civil liability for unpaid taxes and penalties; potential criminal charges for conspiracy to evade tax.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing

Statutory sanction: CRA may adjust prices and impose penalties under Section 247: 10% of the net transfer pricing adjustment (if adjustment exceeds CAD 5 million or 10% of gross revenue). Interest on reassessed amounts.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for penalties if they signed false or misleading documents. In severe cases, criminal prosecution for tax evasion, leading to fines and imprisonment.
