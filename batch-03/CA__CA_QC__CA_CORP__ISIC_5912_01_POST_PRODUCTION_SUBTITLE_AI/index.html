Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital under the Quebec Business Corporations Act; a corporation may be formed with zero paid-up capital. Shares may be subscribed for nominal consideration (e.g., CAD 1.00 per share) fixed by directors; no escrow or trust account is mandated. Non-cash consideration must be valued per art. 34 QBCA. Non-resident ownership is permitted without restriction.

Funding & unlock path: Fire safety: obtain a fire safety certificate from the competent municipal fire prevention service (e.g., Service de sécurité incendie de Montréal) verifying compliance with the Quebec Fire Safety Code (IFC); maintain portable extinguishers, exit signage, and emergency lighting. Structural: occupancy permit issued under the Quebec Building Code (RBQ) confirming the floors' static load capacity for heavy acoustic isolation and equipment racks; electrical installation certificate issued by a master electrician under the Quebec Electrical Code. Labour: register with the CNESST (Commission des normes, de l'équité, de la santé et de la sécurité du travail) for occupational health and safety, submit a hazard identification and prevention program, and implement a workplace hazardous materials information system (WHMIS) for cleaning agents.

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — federal Income Tax Act and GST/HST; portal: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/services/taxes.html). Quebec: Revenu Québec — QST and provincial corporate tax; portal: Mon dossier d'entreprise (https://www.revenuquebec.ca). Export customs: Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) (https://www.cbsa-asfc.gc.ca).

E-government filing portal

STEP 1: Incorporate the Business Corporation under the Canada Business Corporations Act (federal) or the Quebec Business Corporations Act; for Quebec operations obtain the Quebec Enterprise Number (NEQ) from the Registraire des entreprises du Québec (REQ). STEP 2: Apply to CRA for a 9-digit Business Number (BN) using Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number); non-resident corporations may file RC1 by mail or fax with applicable schedules and must provide foreign registration/TIN evidence plus a Canadian address for service; no minimum capital is required. STEP 3: Register for GST/HST online through CRA My Business Account or with the RC1; registration is mandatory once taxable supplies in Canada exceed CAD 30,000; a foreign entity may voluntarily register to recover input tax credits. STEP 4: Register with Revenu Québec for QST using the prescribed registration form (LM-1-V, Registration Form for the GST/HST and QST) or the online service in Mon dossier d'entreprise, and separately register for Quebec payroll and source deductions if employees are engaged in Quebec. STEP 5: Maintain the federal BN and the Quebec NEQ, appoint a tax services representative in Canada if the corporation is non-resident, and file all returns electronically (NETFILE for GST/HST; Revenu Québec electronic transmission for QST).

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation. The Corporation is a distinct taxpayer from its shareholders (Income Tax Act s.15); a resident corporation (central management and control in Canada) is taxed on worldwide income (ITA s.2(1)); a non-resident corporation is taxed only on Canadian-source business income, is subject to Part XIII withholding on dividends and interest, and may incur a 25% Part XIV branch profits tax (treaty-reduced). No pass-through treatment is available; Quebec imposes a separate entity-level provincial income tax under the Taxation Act (Québec).

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box regime exists in Canada. Elections available include: (i) ITA s.85(1) rollover election on transfer of property to the corporation to defer capital gains; (ii) association-status election under ITA s.256(2.1) for Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) planning; (iii) ITA s.216 net-basis election for non-residents earning Canadian rental or timber royalties (treaty-enabled); (iv) Excise Tax Act s.156 election for supplies between closely-related corporations to neutralize GST/HST; (v) the corresponding QST election for closely-related corporations under the Quebec Sales Tax Act; and (vi) hybrid mismatch rules (ITA ss.18.4-18.6) and the Excessive Interest and Financing Expenses Limitation (EIFEL, ITA ss.18.2-18.3), which are mandatory anti-avoidance provisions accompanied by tax-credit timing elections under ITA s.20(11)-(12). None of these elections converts a Business Corporation into a pass-through entity.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000. Small-supplier threshold for GST/HST (ETA s.148(1)) and QST (QSA s.16), measured over the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters of worldwide taxable supplies; a non-resident supplier of digital services (including AI subtitling and voice-cloning products) must register once the CAD 30,000 threshold is met; voluntary registration is permitted below the threshold to recover input tax credits; separate QST registration is mandatory when a commercial activity is carried on in Quebec.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported qualify for zero-rating under ETA Schedule VI Part V ss.1-6. Services supplied to a non-resident, including AI subtitle transcription and AI voice-cloning studio services, are zero-rated under ETA Schedule VI Part V s.7 (supplies to non-residents) where the service is not performed in respect of Canadian real property or tangible personal property and is not received in Canada by the non-resident; the QST mirror rule applies under the Quebec Sales Tax Regulations Schedule VI Part V s.7. A contract with a Canadian resident studio client does not qualify; the payer's location, place of performance, contract governing law, and digital delivery logs determine eligibility. B2B services received in Canada are subject to 5% GST plus 9.975% QST (14.975% combined).

  • B13A Canada Export Declaration / Customs Coding Form (required only for physical media or goods; service exports are evidenced by a service export worksheet and delivery logs)
  • Duly executed service agreement with the non-resident client (governing law, place of performance, delivery terms, foreign TIN of the client)
  • Commercial invoice referencing the zero-rated export and the ETA Schedule VI citation
  • Bill of lading, airway bill, or electronic export manifest (for goods) plus digital delivery access logs and IP trails (for services)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate, SWIFT MT103, or bank statement evidencing cross-border FX proceeds from the non-resident buyer
  • Non-resident status evidence: foreign tax registration number, certificate of residence, and declaration that the service is not consumed in Canada
  • GST/HST and QST returns with the zero-rated export schedules and the related input tax credit calculation
  • GST189 (General Application for GST/HST Rebate) where a rebate is claimed, plus transfer-pricing documentation for intercompany export transactions

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control, or digital stamping exists in Canada. The CRA operates a voluntary Pan-Canadian e-invoicing pilot using the PEPPOL BIS 3.0 standard; B2G invoices must be submitted through federal supplier portals. Registered businesses must file GST/HST returns electronically via NETFILE and QST returns through Revenu Québec electronic transmission; electronic books and records must be retained in a machine-readable format and produced to CRA or Revenu Québec within prescribed timelines.

CRA and Revenu Québec may reassess within a 4-year limitation period; the period is unlimited in cases of misrepresentation, fraud, or wilful default (ITA s.152(4); QTA s.1016; ETA s.298). False or inflated export zero-rating claims attract gross negligence penalties of 50% of the understated tax (ITA s.163(2); ETA s.285.1) plus specified false-statement penalties; criminal evasion under ETA s.327 or ITA s.239 carries fines up to CAD 250,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years. Reassessed export zero-ratings claw back input tax credits and rebates with compounded daily arrears interest at the CRA prescribed rate (currently approximately 5%-9% per annum). Export refund applications are barred unless filed within the two-year window (ETA s.296(2.1) and QST equivalent). Directors and officers are jointly and severally liable for unremitted GST/HST and QST (ITA s.227.1; ETA s.323; QSA parallel), and fraudulent FX or export documentation triggers parallel AML review and transfer-pricing penalties under ITA s.247.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a fully convertible currency regime for both capital and current account transactions. Residents and non-residents may freely convert CAD to foreign currencies and vice versa without prior regulatory approval. Profit remittances by Canadian corporations are unrestricted provided they comply with corporate tax filing obligations, beneficial ownership disclosure requirements, and anti-money laundering standards.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory requirement exists for mandatory surrender of export proceeds. Receipts may be retained in foreign currency accounts or converted to CAD at the exporter's sole discretion. All transactions remain subject to anti-money laundering and counter-terrorist financing reporting thresholds and must be supported by verifiable trade documentation.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined general corporate income tax rate for Quebec corporations: 15% federal plus 11.5% Quebec. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access a combined small-business rate of approximately 12.2% on eligible active business income within the applicable small-business limits, subject to federal and Quebec rules.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident, subject to treaty reduction, statutory exemptions, and anti-avoidance rules.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due within six months after the end of every taxation year. Any balance is generally due two months after year-end; an eligible Canadian-controlled private corporation may qualify for a three-month balance-due period if the statutory conditions are satisfied.

Deadline: · Frequency:

The CO-17 return is generally due within six months after the end of the corporation's taxation year. Quebec tax balances are generally payable two months after year-end, subject to applicable small-corporation rules and exceptions.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: For Canadian cross-border non-arm's-length transactions, contemporaneous documentation is required under section 247 of the Income Tax Act. Form T106 reporting is generally triggered when aggregate reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions exceed CAD 1 million, subject to exclusions. Canada does not impose a universal OECD-style separate Local File or Master File filing threshold; section 247 documentation must nevertheless support the arm's-length result, and the CRA may require organized transaction-level and group-level information.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no general standalone Economic Substance Regulation equivalent to certain offshore ESR regimes. Substance remains material under section 247 transfer-pricing rules, the general anti-avoidance rule, treaty principal-purpose and limitation provisions where applicable, beneficial-ownership analysis, corporate-residence rules, foreign-affiliate rules, and interest-deductibility rules. The corporation should maintain Canadian personnel, decision-making, functions, assets, risks, contracts, payroll, premises, and operational records consistent with the profits allocated to Quebec and Canada. Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Reporting

Statutory sanction: Under the federal Income Tax Act, conviction for tax evasion may result in a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment of up to five years. Civil reassessment, gross-negligence penalties, interest, and provincial penalties may apply. Fraudulent or forged records may also engage the Criminal Code.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face criminal prosecution where personal participation or authorization is proven. Personal liability is not automatic for ordinary corporate income tax, but directors may be liable for unremitted source deductions, GST/HST, and certain deemed-trust amounts after statutory conditions are satisfied. Imprisonment is possible for conviction; travel restrictions are not automatic but may arise from bail, court orders, or enforcement proceedings.
Failure to File, Remit, or Maintain Records

Statutory sanction: Late-filing penalties, failure-to-file penalties, prescribed interest, director-assessment exposure for source deductions and GST/HST, and penalties for failure to provide information may apply. Repeated non-compliance can lead to enforced collection, garnishment, liens, and prosecution in serious cases.

Director personal liability: Directors may be assessed personally for certain unremitted trust amounts if they failed to exercise the statutory due diligence defence. Corporate debts generally remain corporate debts, but personal guarantees, fraudulent conduct, oppression, and statutory director-liability provisions can create direct exposure.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation and Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: The CRA may adjust taxable income under section 247, impose transfer-pricing penalties generally equal to 10% of the applicable adjustment in qualifying circumstances, deny deductions, apply withholding tax, and charge interest. Reassessments may extend beyond ordinary periods in cases of misrepresentation or neglect.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure for knowingly participating in fraud, false records, or tax evasion. Ordinary transfer-pricing adjustments do not automatically pierce the corporate veil or create personal income-tax liability.
Treaty Abuse and Beneficial-Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for unwithheld Part XIII tax, interest, and penalties. Treaty benefits may be denied under beneficial-ownership principles, anti-conduit principles, the principal-purpose test where applicable, or the general anti-avoidance rule.

Director personal liability: Responsible officers may face civil or criminal exposure for knowing false certifications or deliberate withholding failures. A corporate veil is not ordinarily pierced solely because treaty relief was incorrectly applied.
Shell Company, Sham Arrangement, or Artificial Residence

Statutory sanction: The CRA may deny deductions, credits, treaty benefits, or Canadian residence claims; recharacterize transactions under the general anti-avoidance rule; assess withholding and penalties; and impose interest. Sham transactions and fraudulent documentation may result in criminal prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors and controlling persons may be personally liable for fraudulent conduct, statutory trust amounts, or criminal offences. Imprisonment may apply after conviction; travel bans are not an automatic corporate-tax sanction.
AML, Sanctions, and Payment-Rail Misuse

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties, transaction restrictions, compliance orders, and criminal prosecution may apply. Criminal laundering or proceeds-of-crime offences can result in substantial fines and imprisonment, including imprisonment of up to ten years or more depending on the offence. Sanctions breaches may attract penalties under Canadian sanctions legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and beneficial owners may be prosecuted for knowing participation, authorization, or wilful blindness. Personal asset restraint, forfeiture, prosecution, and court-imposed travel restrictions may occur; none is automatic merely because a corporation received a compliance notice.
AI Intellectual-Property, Privacy, and Data-Governance Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Civil injunctions, damages, accounting of profits, statutory copyright remedies where available, privacy complaints, regulatory orders, contractual claims, and reputational loss may arise. Criminal exposure depends on facts such as fraud, identity offences, unauthorized access, or deliberate misappropriation.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure for intentional torts, knowing authorization, fraud, or statutory offences. Corporate status does not shield a person who personally commits or directs the unlawful act.
