Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Federal: Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — portal: CRA My Business Account at canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/e-services-businesses/business-account.html. Quebec: Revenu Québec — portal: Mon dossier d'entreprise / CléInfo at revenuquebec.ca.

E-government filing portal

A foreign-owned Quebec limited partnership begins by filing its déclaration de société en commandite with the Registraire des entreprises du Québec (REQ) to obtain the Quebec enterprise number (NEQ). The general partner then applies to the Canada Revenue Agency using Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and selected program accounts, principally RT for GST/HST) to obtain the federal Business Number (BN); the partnership itself is the registrant and indirect-tax person. Next, the partnership registers for QST with Revenu Québec using the business registration application (formerly LM-1) and is issued a QST registration number. There is no statutory minimum capital requirement for the limited partnership, and non-resident individuals or entities may own limited-partner interests without any direct registration obligation unless individual Canadian/Quebec tax returns are triggered. All tax accounts must be updated within prescribed statutory time limits and records must be kept in French-acceptable auditable form.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible; for Canadian and Quebec income tax purposes, the limited partnership (société en commandite) is fiscally transparent and its income or loss flows through to the general and limited partners. However, for GST/HST and QST purposes, the limited partnership is treated as a separate registrant and is responsible for collecting, reporting, and remitting indirect tax on taxable supplies made in Quebec.

Available election forms

No check-the-box election is available under Canadian or Quebec domestic tax law; the pass-through classification is automatic for a genuine limited partnership. For U.S. federal tax purposes, the Canadian limited partnership may be classified under U.S. check-the-box rules (Treas. Reg. §301.7701-3), but any such U.S. election does not alter Canadian/Quebec treatment. Canada also applies anti-hybrid mismatch rules under the Income Tax Act and the Quebec Taxation Act that may deny benefits or re-characterise payments where a foreign jurisdiction treats the partnership as opaque; these are statutory constraints, not elective classification mechanisms.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies made in Canada/Quebec during the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters or in any single calendar quarter, excluding certain capital-property sales and with related-person aggregation rules. Once a supplier ceases to be a 'small supplier' and is carrying on business in Canada/Quebec, registration with the CRA for GST and with Revenu Québec for QST is mandatory, generally within 30 days. A non-resident-owned ghost kitchen must monitor platform-sourced gross considerations to stay compliant.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available only for genuine exports: goods exported from Canada in accordance with the Customs Act by the supplier or for the supplier, or services supplied to a non-resident where the service is performed wholly or substantially outside Canada and the Canadian/Quebec consumption benefits do not attach. For an ISIC 5610 ghost-kitchen business, prepared meals delivered to Quebec consumers are taxable supplies and cannot be zero-rated merely because payment is remitted through UberEats or DoorDash. Cross-border kitchen-capacity or intangible services to a non-resident brand may qualify for zero-rating only if all performable services occur outside Canada and supporting records demonstrate the non-resident recipient and lack of Canadian use.

  • CBSA Customs Export Declaration or Canadian Export Reporting System output
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing export from Canada and foreign destination
  • Commercial Invoice and Packing List
  • Proof of export and statutory declaration where the carrier is not a licensed CBSA reporting entity
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or equivalent upstream banking record proving payment from the non-resident customer
  • Contracts, service-delivery logs, kitchen-utilisation records, and revenue-allocation memoranda substantiating the zero-rated portion

Canada and Quebec do not operate a continuous-transaction-control, real-time tax clearance, mandatory e-invoice API, or digital-stamping regime. Electronic invoicing is permitted provided it is legible, accurate, contains prescribed GST/HST and QST information, and is retained for six years. Quebec rules require French-language commercial documents where provided to customers. Payment-rail files from UberEats and DoorDash and platform settlement statements are not substitute fiscal invoices and must be reconciled to issued invoices and general ledger records.

CRA and Revenu Québec apply heightened audit scrutiny to zero-rating claims. If export documentation is missing, contradicted by business reality, or fraudulent, the supplier is reassessed for uncollected GST at 5% and QST at 9.975% on the full consideration, plus retroactive interest. Gross negligence penalties can be 50% of the tax sought to be evaded; criminal tax evasion under the Excise Tax Act carries fines between 50% and 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment. Equivalent Quebec penalties apply under the Tax Administration Act, and input tax credits or input tax refunds are clawed back proportionately to the denied zero-rating.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Fully liberalised. The Canadian dollar is a free-floating, freely convertible currency under IMF Article VIII, with no exchange controls, no surrender requirements, and no prior approval mechanism for current-account or capital-account transactions. Capital proceeds, profit distributions, and trade settlements can be converted and remitted without central-bank authorisation. Practical frictions are limited to: (i) FINTRAC reporting for any cross-border transfer exceeding CAD 10,000; (ii) Part XIII federal and Quebec provincial withholding tax on non-resident limited-partner distributions (25% statutory, treaty-reduced); and (iii) a mandatory Investment Canada Act notification to ISED for the establishment of the new Canadian enterprise, while review thresholds (CAD 1.075 billion for WTO investors) are far above the proposed vehicle size.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No surrender or mandatory conversion regime exists in Canada. Export proceeds and foreign-currency settlement inflows may be held, repatriated, or converted at the treasury's discretion without timeline constraint. For the ghost kitchen, UberEats and DoorDash payouts settle in CAD via domestic direct-deposit rails; foreign-source trade revenue may be retained in USD or any convertible currency, subject only to CRA record-keeping, QST/HST reconciliation, and the fintech platforms' internal payout policies.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), in coordination with the Department of Finance Canada for sanctions and administrative capital controls; FINTRAC (Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada) enforces cross-border EFT monitoring under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA); OSFI (Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions) supervises federally chartered banks.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Low as a primary custody treasury; Medium as a supplementary payout rail. Canada does not issue EMI (Electronic Money Institution) licenses. Cross-border fintechs (e.g., Wise, Payoneer, Airwallex) operate as FINTRAC-registered Money Services Businesses, not as deposit-taking institutions under the Bank Act, and therefore cannot offer CDIC-insured operational deposits. Their UBO and sector-risk models frequently reject ISIC 5610 aggregator settlement profiles. Recommended use: low-value foreign supplier disbursements only, held on a zero-balance pass-through basis.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Not applicable at entity level (Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent). Federal general corporate rate: 15%; Quebec provincial general rate: 11.5%. Combined non-incentivized federal/provincial corporate rate: 26.5%. Active business income of a Canadian-controlled private corporation (not applicable to LP) may qualify for federal small business deduction and Quebec reduced rate, but partnership allocations flow through to partners.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Canada's Part XIII gross withholding tax on dividends paid by a Canadian corporation to a non-resident is 25%. However, an LP is not a corporation; profit distributions to partners are not dividends. When a Canada-resident LP allocates active business income to a non-resident limited partner, the distribution itself is typically a return of capital or profit; no Part XIII withholding applies because the partnership is transparent and the income is taxable to the partner as Canadian-source business income. If the LP pays management fees, interest, royalties, or rental consideration to a non-resident, the payor must withhold 25% Part XIII tax unless reduced by treaty. Quebec also imposes a 15% withholding on distributions to non-residents from a Quebec partnership if the LP has elected to be a 'non-resident-owned investment corporation'? No such election – Quebec has no separate Quebec WHT on non-resident distributions to partners; the federal Part XIII regime applies to certain passive payments. For treaty relief: a non-resident partner can file a Part XIII waiver with CRA by obtaining a treaty-based determination (e.g., Form NR301 for U.S. residents) or by filing a Canadian tax return to claim a refund. The effective treaty rate on Canadian business profits of a non-resident is zero if no permanent establishment (Canada–U.S. Article VII). For dividends sourced from a deemed Canadian subsidiary, treaty reduces the 25% rate to 5% for corporate shareholders holding at least 10% voting stock, or 15% otherwise (Article X of Canada–U.S. Tax Treaty). Since an LP is pass-through, the 'dividend withholding' concept applies only if the LP is a central cash-management vehicle that makes taxable deemed-dividend distributions under subsection 15(1) of the Income Tax Act. Practical guidance: route profit distributions as current-account draws; do not characterize them as dividends or royalties in the partnership agreement.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canadian dollar; under CRA's Transfer Pricing Memorandum (TPM-16), the requirement to prepare contemporaneous documentation (Master File and Local File) is triggered when the aggregate value of cross-border transactions with non-residents exceeds CAD 1 million in a fiscal year for goods/services, CAD 100,000 for patents and intangibles, CAD 100,000 for interest, and CAD 100,000 for management fees (threshold depends on transaction type). For a Quebec LP operating ghost kitchens, if it pays royalties or delivery-platform fees to a non-Canadian affiliate (e.g., a U.S. parent brand licensor) exceeding CAD 1 million, a Country-by-Country Report is required only if the consolidated group revenue exceeds CAD 1 billion for the previous fiscal year. Quebec's provincial Administrative Guideline ARQ.25.2.5 requires a Local File for intra-group transactions if the gross revenue of the Quebec partnership exceeds CAD 100,000 and the partner is non-resident? Use the federal threshold for prudential compliance: prepare a TP questionnaire and benchmarking study if any transaction exceeds CAD 200,000 per category.

Economic substance scope: Canada has not enacted a specific Economic Substance Regulation (ESR) like the UAE or EU ATAD. However, federal general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under Income Tax Act section 245 denies the benefit of a transaction if the primary purpose is avoidance and it is not real actual economic substance. Quebec has a similar GAAR under section 1029.6 of the Taxation Act. The CRA has expressly targeted partnerships used to park intangible assets in low-tax jurisdictions. A Ghost Kitchen LP with no physical location, no employees in Canada, but generating significant revenues through delivery platforms will be subject to the 'permanent establishment' test and the substance-over-form doctrine. Additionally, Quebec imposes an annual $1,000 to $3,000 licence fee for 'restaurant operations' that requires a real location. The LP must demonstrate: (1) actual management operations conducted in Quebec; (2) genuine decision makers with physical offices; (3) tangible kitchen equipment and inventory; (4) valid employment contracts with Canadian staff; and (5) a business plan and board minutes (for corporate partners) reflecting economic activity. The CRA's 'specified non-resident' anti-deferral rules (section 75(2)) can reattribute partnership income to the contributor of property if a person or partnership contributes property to a partnership on condition that income may revert to them – a scenario common in virtual brand licensing.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Reporting

Statutory sanction: Federal: Income Tax Act section 238 – summons default penalties: for each offence, a fine of CAD 1,000 to CAD 25,000 and/or imprisonment up to 12 months. On conviction of evasion (s. 239): a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded or imprisonment not exceeding two years (summary) or up to five years (indictment). Quebec: Taxation Act section 1050 – penalies of 50% of unpaid tax, plus a fine not exceeding CAD 10,000 and imprisonment not exceeding two years for wilful evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors of a corporate partner of the LP are jointly and severally liable for unpaid GST/HST and payroll withholding (source deductions) if they knew, or reasonably should have known, of non-remittance. For tax evasion, criminal liability may attach to persons who 'directly, or through omission or commission, participated in the illegal act' – including officers and directors who authorized the evasion. Civil restitution: directors are liable for the full amount of unpaid taxes, interest, penalties, and may be subject to a court order to restore property to the partnership if illegal distributions are made. Travel bans and asset freezes may be issued under the Income Tax Act s. 226(1) if the taxpayer is attempting to leave Canada without paying assessed taxes. Imprisonment is possible for evasion under the Criminal Code (conspiracy or fraud) up to 10 years.
Transfer Pricing / Non-Arm's-Length Royalty Abuse

Statutory sanction: CRA may re-determine the arm's-length price under s. 247(2), increasing the LP's taxable income by the re-priced royalty, applying 10% interest, and a transfer-pricing penalty up to 10% of the net downward adjustment (s. 247(3)). The penalty applies if the taxpayer failed to make reasonable efforts to determine arm's-length terms. Quebec assesses an additional 10% penalty under the Taxation Act s. 1064.2. Searches and prosecutions may follow if false documents are submitted.

Director personal liability: Directors of the Canadian partner that signed intercompany agreements can be held liable for the TP penalty if they signed a return they knew was incorrect (negligence). The liability is limited to fines of CAD 5,000 to 10,000 for failure to file TP documentation (s. 247(7)). Civil liability: a director who authorizes a royalty payment that drains a partnership may be sued for creditor claims; in bankruptcy, the court may impose a 5-year travel ban for non-cooperation in examinations.
Shell Company / Non-Commercial Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA can disregard the partnership arrangement under section 75(2), attributing income directly to the non-resident, then assessing a 25% Part XIII withholding on distributions or 45% deemed dividend for a 'designated partnership' under s. 16(1.1) of the ITAR. Revenu Québec may impose a fine of CAD 5,000 to CAD 50,000 for operating a restaurant without a valid permit under the Loi sur la protection du consommateur and the Régie des alcools, des courses et des jeux (RACJ) – even if no alcohol, a food services establishment permit is required. Corporate law sanction: the enterprises register may be revoked; the LP loses limited liability protection for partners if no registered head office (Quebec Business Corporations Act, though LP registrations are under the Quebec Civil Code art. 2236).

Director personal liability: If the court pierces the partnership veil (partnership is not a separate legal person), general partners face unlimited liability for the partnership's debts for all approved operations. Directors of a corporate general partner may be liable for the full unpaid taxes under the Quebec Taxation Act s. 23.1 if the corporation is a vehicle of a fraud. A criminal charge under the Criminal Code for fraud (s. 380) carries a maximum imprisonment of 14 years. Travel bans are routinely issued if the director is a foreign national with non-Canadian assets.
Money Laundering / AML (Proceeds of Crime)

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, a food operator is not a reporting entity unless it issues or redeems money orders. Correct trigger: not an AML violation for the kitchen itself, but if the LP systematically handles large amounts of cash (not normal for delivery) without proper books, the CRA can invoke the 'gross negligence' penalty of 50% of concealed income. FINTRAC may be involved if the LP collaborates with a payment service provider to break transactions under CAD 10,000. Criminal sanction for aiding laundering: up to 10 years imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable, but if they personally approve opening multiple corporate bank accounts with the sole purpose of avoiding reporting thresholds, they can be charged with money laundering (Criminal Code s. 462.31). Civil confiscation of the profits from the ghost kitchen operation is possible. A travel ban can be imposed by the court under mutual legal assistance requests if the director attempts to flee Quebec to a non-extradition country.
