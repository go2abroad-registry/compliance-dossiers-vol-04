In Quebec, a company must maintain a registered office (siège social) in Quebec at a physically accessible address. The address must appear in a public registry and be a place where documents may be served. A virtual office may be permissible if a physical mailbox/service address in Quebec is provided by a licensed service provider and the provider accepts service of process and statutory notices; however, for a retail food-service business (specialty tea salon) the municipal food establishment licence, Québec Restauration sector, and municipal zoning permits entail physical commercial premises and on-site kitchen/service area. Thus, a purely virtual office for the registered office is typically acceptable, but a physical operation location is required for business licensing.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods are zero-rated under GST/HST (s. 123 and Schedule VI, Part V) when the supplier ships the goods directly outside Canada and maintains evidence of export within the required time; services supplied to non-residents (except certain services performed in Canada, services to a non-resident who is registered, or services relating to real property in Canada) may be zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V and s. 7; Quebec QST zero-rating mirrors the GST/HST for exports under QST Act (s. 185 and Schedule VI); a specialty tea salon exporting packaged loose-leaf tea, tea ceremony equipment, or ceramic wares to non-resident customers must show that the goods are delivered outside Canada by common carrier, courier, or mail, or that the purchaser is a non-resident and the goods are exported within a reasonable time. For B2B zero-rated services, the supplier must maintain the recipient's non-resident status evidence and the service must be performed wholly outside Canada or be an excluded service under Schedule VI. Refund mechanism: net tax calculation includes input tax credits (ITCs) or ISCs (Quebec input tax refunds) for embedded GST/QST, and zero-rated exporters can claim full input tax credits; the foreign supplier may apply for a rebate under s. 252 of the Excise Tax Act for tax paid on imported goods/services used in commercial activities; the rebate application is Form GST189.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a fully convertible currency under a free-floating exchange rate regime. There are no exchange controls or restrictions on current account transactions, capital account movements, or profit remittances under federal or Quebec law. Dividends, interest, royalties, and management fees may be remitted to non-resident shareholders or affiliates without prior central bank approval, provided that applicable Part XIII non-resident withholding taxes under the Income Tax Act (Canada) are satisfied and the remitting corporation is duly compliant with Quebec Revenue Agency (Revenu Québec) requirements. No material convertibility hurdles exist; however, the source-of-funds and beneficial-ownership verification requirements imposed by domestic banks and FINTRAC constitute practical gateways for cross-border remittances.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada does not impose statutory export proceeds surrender rules or mandatory conversion ratios. Exporters are free to retain foreign currency in foreign or domestic bank accounts. Nonetheless, exporters must (i) report goods and services trade data under the Customs Act and the Canadian International Merchandise Trade statistics program administered by Statistics Canada, (ii) maintain records sufficient to reconcile export proceeds for tax purposes, and (iii) comply with FINTRAC reporting thresholds for large cross-border currency movements and electronic funds transfers. No CAD conversion mandate applies to export receipts of the specialty tea salon lounge.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (central monetary authority); Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) (prudential regulation of federally-chartered banks); Autorité des marchés financiers (AMF) (Quebec financial sector regulation); Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) (AML/CFT supervision of reporting entities)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium feasibility with material legal constraints. Non-bank offshore financial technology entities and electronic money institutions (EMIs) are not considered 'banks' under the Bank Act (Canada) and cannot accept deposit liabilities in the ordinary course of a retail banking business. Offshore EMIs may operate lawfully in Canada only as Money Services Businesses (MSBs) or Foreign Money Services Businesses (FMSBs) registered with FINTRAC, and in Quebec may require a licence under the Act respecting money-services businesses administered by the AMF. Such entities can provide payment facilitation, foreign exchange conversion, and cross-border disbursement rails, but they are not eligible to hold corporate escrow balances or provide government-guaranteed CDIC deposit protection. Accordingly, reliance on EMIs is acceptable as a secondary payments rail; the primary treasury relationship must rest with a Schedule I domestic bank or a Quebec-licensed financial institution.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

All inbound equity subscriptions, shareholder loan advances, and intra-group funding must be routed through a Canadian Schedule I bank or a Quebec-licensed caisse using SWIFT MT103 wire instructions. The remitter must reference the correct Canadian cross-border payment purpose code, typically 'Direct Investment — Equity' or 'Direct Investment — Intercompany Loan' as defined under Statistics Canada's Classification of Outward and Inward Direct Investment. For Quebec corporate filings, the wire narrative must include the REQ enterprise number and the business corporation 'legal name' exactly as registered. The receiving bank will verify the source of funds, UBO structure, and authorization matrix before crediting the capital escrow or operating account. Payments exceeding CAD 10,000 in a single transaction are reportable to FINTRAC where cross-border electronic funds transfer thresholds are met. For tea salon operations, inbound injections are normally routed as CAD-denominated wires to avoid FX slippage, with FX conversion performed at the receiving bank at the prevailing wholesale rate.Outbound settlement for imported loose-leaf tea, ceramic teaware, and cultural inventory is executed via (i) SWIFT MT103 for direct supplier wire payments, (ii) commercial letters of credit (sight or usance) for bulk procurement where the supplier requires documentary protection, and (iii) Payments Canada Lynx same-day large-value settlement for domestic water transfers in CAD. For sub-CAD-clearing-value payments, the Real-Time Rail (RTR) and Interac e-Transfer rails are permissible for local vendors, utilities, and Quebec service providers. Outbound USD or foreign-currency disbursements are coordinated through the bank's FX desk under an executed master FX agreement. Non-bank offshore financial technology entities and electronic money institutions (EMIs) are not considered 'banks' under the Bank Act (Canada) and cannot accept deposit liabilities in the ordinary course of a retail banking business. Offshore EMIs may operate lawfully in Canada only as Money Services Businesses (MSBs) or Foreign Money Services Businesses (FMSBs) registered with FINTRAC, and in Quebec may require a licence under the Act respecting money-services businesses administered by the AMF. Such entities can provide payment facilitation, foreign exchange conversion, and cross-border disbursement rails, but they are not eligible to hold corporate escrow balances or provide government-guaranteed CDIC deposit protection. Accordingly, reliance on EMIs is acceptable as a secondary payments rail; the primary treasury relationship must rest with a Schedule I domestic bank or a Quebec-licensed financial institution.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined general (Federal 15% + Quebec 11.5%); 12.2% combined for a Canadian-controlled private corporation eligible for the federal small business deduction (9%) and the Quebec SMB reduced rate (3.2%) on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% on gross taxable dividends paid or deemed paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident of Canada, applied under Part XIII of the federal Income Tax Act

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CRA requires contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation for cross-border transactions with non-arm's length parties where transaction values exceed CAD 5,000,000 in the fiscal period for goods, services, interest and intangible rights; or CAD 7,500,000 for capital transactions. No mandatory Local File/Master File regime applies to small corporations, but OECD-aligned financial records are prudent for groups with global consolidated revenue in excess of CAD 750,000,000.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no separate ESR (Economic Substance Regulations) file; the business purpose doctrine and the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) under ITA s.245 apply to disregard transactions or arrangements lacking a genuine commercial objective. A specialty tea salon lounge will satisfy substantive requirements through a physical service location, full-time personnel, tea inventories, customer contracts, utilities, and a valid business licence. Structuring the corporation solely as a treaty-shopping vehicle or maintaining offshore accounts without operational backing will expose it to GAAR adjustments, interest, and penalties.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False or Misleading Statements

Statutory sanction: Gross negligence penalty under ITA s.163(2) of 50% of the tax understated; late-filing penalties of 5% plus 1% per month up to 12 months; criminal prosecution under Criminal Code s.380(1)(a) for fraud over CAD 5,000, which is an indictable offence in Canada, also covered by Quebec's Tax Administration Act anti-evasion measures; maximum prison sentence up to 14 years for criminal fraud; fines without limitation; additional civil penalties at both federal and provincial levels

Director personal liability: Where the corporation is insolvent or fails to pay source deductions (payroll, GST/HST), directors may be jointly and severally liable under ITA s.227.1 and Quebec corresponding provisions if due diligence defence fails. Criminal conviction can result not only in monetary fines and imprisonment but also travel restrictions during court proceedings and professional disqualification. Courts may order restitution and issue departure bans pending trial.
Shell Company / Artificial Transfer Pricing

Statutory sanction: GAAR recharacterization: CRA may disallow the benefit and reassess tax as though the arm's-length result had occurred; transfer-pricing penalties under ITA s.247(3) for failing to use arm's length terms: penalty equal to 10% of the net adjustment (reduced to 5% if reasonable efforts demonstrated); interest and compounding on reassessed tax; denial of loss carryovers and retroactive reassessment

Director personal liability: Directors knowingly approving a sham structure may be jointly liable with the corporation under s.160 for unpaid taxes resulting from the adjusted income; in criminal fraud scenarios, personal assets can be reached through civil actions, and restrictions on leaving Canada may be sought to secure attendance. Directorial good faith is not a defence if the arrangement is fraudulent.
AML / Record-Keeping and Source Deduction Failures

Statutory sanction: Under ITA s.230(7), failure to keep books and records: CAD 1,000 to CAD 8,000 fine and possible imprisonment up to 6 months (for each offense); under the Excise Tax Act, failure to remit GST/HST: penalty equal to 6% of the unremitted amount plus daily interest at prescribed rates; Quebec may impose additional fines for co-extensive failures; tax withholding omissions trigger director liability for unremitted amounts with recovery directly from corporate assets, and standard bank/lockbox seizure powers

Director personal liability: Directors or officers who fail to deduct, withhold, remit, or pay GST/HST or source deductions are jointly and severally liable for the required amounts; if the corporation is wound up without settlement, the directors may be personally assessed without transient immunities; imprisonment may be ordered for chronic non-remittance under the Excise Tax Act for willful evasion; civil recovery may include seizure of personal assets and restrictions on travel or bank accounts in support of tax collection.
