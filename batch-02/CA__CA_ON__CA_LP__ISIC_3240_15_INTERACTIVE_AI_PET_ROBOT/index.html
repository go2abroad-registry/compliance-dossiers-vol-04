Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — An Ontario Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent under ITA s. 96; the LP is not an income tax taxpayer, and taxable income and losses are allocated directly to partners. Territorial_Scope applies to non-resident partners: they are subject to Canadian income tax only on Canadian-source amounts allocated from the LP (business income carried on in Canada under ITA s. 2(3), plus Part XIV branch tax on Canadian business income where the partner is a foreign corporation). For GST/HST purposes, however, the LP is a separate person under ETA s. 123(1) and must register, file and remit HST in its own right.

Available election forms

Canada has no domestic check-the-box election for a domestic partnership; pass-through treatment is automatic. Available elections for this Ontario LP include: (1) ETA s. 156 election to treat supplies between the LP and a closely related registered corporation or partnership as made for nil consideration, provided the 95% related-party ownership test is met; (2) ETA s. 227 Quick Method election for simplified ITC accounting, available to specified registrants with worldwide taxable supplies not exceeding CAD 400,000 per fiscal year (excluding financial services and zero-rated supplies); (3) ETA s. 239(1) election for annual GST/HST return filing by small suppliers; (4) ITA s. 216 election for non-resident partners to be taxed on net Canadian-source rental income at Part I rates instead of Part XIII gross-basis withholding, which may apply if robotic units are leased rather than sold; (5) ITA s. 249.1(7) fiscal-period election for a partnership to adopt or retain a non-calendar tax year with the unanimous concurrence of members and compliance with the designated-member rules. For foreign hybrid members (e.g., a US LLC partner), CRA classification follows Income Tax Folio S4-F18-C1 and not the US check-the-box election; Canada's hybrid mismatch rules (ITA ss. 18.2-18.4) apply mandatorily with no domestic opt-out election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 small supplier threshold (ETA s. 148(1)). The LP must register for GST/HST once its worldwide taxable supplies, including taxable supplies of associated persons, exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over the four preceding consecutive calendar quarters. Since the LP is registered in Ontario and its taxing member carries on the partnership business at the Ontario registered office, the threshold applies on a global basis; voluntary registration below the threshold is permitted under ETA s. 240(2).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

The supply of bionic companion robotic animals (tangible personal property) by the Ontario LP is zero-rated at 0% GST/HST under ETA Schedule VI, Part V, s. 1 when: (a) the recipient is a non-resident who is not registered for GST/HST and has no permanent establishment in Canada; (b) the goods are exported from Canada 'as soon as is reasonable' after the supply, either by the LP, by a common carrier engaged by the LP, or through the LP's agent; (c) possession of the goods passes to the non-resident recipient outside Canada; and (d) the LP obtains and retains documentary proof of export in accordance with GST/HST Memorandum 7-1 and CRA Policy Statement P-240. Offshore services ancillary to the AI pet robot (e.g., firmware updates and companion-app connectivity services performed entirely outside Canada for non-residents) qualify for zero-rating under ETA Schedule VI, Part V, s. 7. Input tax credits for robotic components, AI chips, speech-synthesizer modules, assembly costs and export freight remain fully recoverable despite the 0% output rate, subject to the substantiation requirements of ETA ss. 169 and 199.

  • Customs Export Declaration — CBSA Form B13A or the electronic Canadian International Trade Single Window equivalent; required where the export value exceeds CAD 2,000 or the goods are controlled, prohibited or regulated.
  • Bill of Lading (ocean) / Air Waybill (air) / CMR transport document (land) — the transport contract evidencing that the goods left Canada and were delivered to the non-resident buyer.
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / SWIFT MT103 payment evidence — showing receipt of export proceeds from the non-resident buyer into the LP's Canadian settlement account.
  • Commercial Invoice — quoting the LP's GST/HST registration number, the 0% rate, the Schedule VI Part V s. 1 reference, and the non-resident buyer's legal identity.
  • Packing List / Container Manifest — reconciling shipment contents, weights and consignee details.
  • Foreign Customs Entry or destination-country import declaration — obtained from the buyer where available to corroborate physical export.
  • Complete retention file per GST/HST Memorandum 7-1 — all export evidence must be preserved for at least six years under ETA s. 289(1).

Canada does not impose a mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, continuous control, or digital stamping regime for GST/HST invoices; the CRA does not require API-based pre-clearance or fiscal codes. Invoices must nonetheless satisfy ETA s. 223(1), including the supplier's name, the GST/HST registration number, the date of supply, a description of the goods, and the rate applied (including a 0% indicator for exports). Canada is voluntarily adopting Pan-Canadian e-invoicing based on the PEPPOL/BIS 3.0 standard under the 'e-invoice Canada' initiative; participation is optional and is not a precondition for export zero-rating. For online B2C sales using Stripe or credit card rails, the LP remains the supplier and must issue compliant invoices unless the marketplace-facilitator deemed-supplier rules (ETA s. 136.1 et seq.) transfer supplier status to a platform.

CRA audit programs target proof-of-export files and ITC substantiation for zero-rated exporters, examining transportation routes, consignee identity, and payment flows; artificial value chains or reinvoicing arrangements are presumptively abusive under CRA policy. If zero-rating is denied, the CRA retroactively claws back the exemption by reassessing the full 13% HST on export proceeds under ETA s. 296, together with: (i) repayment of all ITCs claimed on inputs embedded in the exported goods; (ii) interest at prescribed rates from the original due date; (iii) a false-statement penalty under ETA s. 280(1) equal to the greater of CAD 250 or 50% of the net tax understated; (iv) a gross-negligence penalty under ETA s. 285.1 equal to 50% of the tax sought to be evaded; and (v) criminal liability for willful evasion under ETA s. 327, with conviction fines ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment of up to five years on indictment. Where export receivables remain outstanding beyond 180 days without adequate security, ITA s. 17(1) deems interest income at prescribed rates on the foreign receivable, and transfer-pricing penalties under ITA s. 163(3) may apply for non-arm's-length balances.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada imposes no exchange controls, no multiple-currency practices, no capital-approval regime, and no restrictions on profit repatriation for non-resident limited partners of an Ontario Limited Partnership. Current and capital account transactions are fully convertible into CAD, USD, EUR and CNH at freely negotiated market rates. The material friction is fiscal rather than monetary: Canadian-source income allocated to non-resident limited partners is subject to Part XIII non-resident withholding tax under the Income Tax Act (Canada) at the statutory 25% rate, reducible under an applicable tax treaty (for example, the Canada-US Convention may lower the rate to 0%–15% for treaty-eligible partners). The Ontario Limited Partnerships Act (R.S.O. 1990, c. L.8) permits non-resident limited partners and accepts foreign-currency capital contributions without statutory restriction, provided the limited partner does not take part in control of the business.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no repatriation timeline; exporters of robotic/AI pet toy hardware may retain and hold export receipts in any currency indefinitely. The applicable compliance obligations are: (i) filing a B13A Export Declaration with the CBSA for every shipment exceeding CAD 2,000 in value; (ii) obtaining an Export Permit from Global Affairs Canada if any hardware or subcomponent falls under Canada's Export Control List (Groups 1–3 dual-use items), with particular scrutiny for bionic components, speech synthesizers, sensors and AI-capable integrated circuits destined to China, Russia, Belarus or other controlled destinations; (iii) reporting movement of currency or monetary instruments equal to CAD 10,000 or more to the CBSA; and (iv) triggering FINTRAC reporting by the partnership's bank for international electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more. No FX surrender or forced conversion is imposed at any point.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) in coordination with the Department of Finance Canada under the Currency Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. C-52) and the Bretton Woods and Related Agreements Act. Canada's IMF Article VIII obligations are observed, and FINTRAC (Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada) administers AML/ATF reporting under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, while the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) administers cross-border currency declarations. Payments Canada operates the national clearing infrastructure: Lynx for high-value real-time gross settlement and the Real-Time Rail for instant payments.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium as an ancillary payout rail; Low as a primary treasury or operating-account substitute. Legal constraints: (a) offshore digital EMIs are not Canadian chartered banks under the Banking Act (S.C. 1991, c. 46) and cannot provide Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC) deposit coverage; (b) an EMI providing payment or payout services to Canadian residents in CAD, or holding funds on behalf of Canadians, may trigger a FINTRAC Foreign Money Services Business (FMSB) registration obligation under the PCMLTFA, including a full AML/ATF compliance program, a designated compliance officer, and Chinese-wall record-keeping duties, despite lacking any Canadian bank license; (c) offshore EMIs are not direct participants in Payments Canada's Lynx or Real-Time Rail, so all CAD settlement is executed through a Canadian correspondent bank, adding latency, pricing spreads, and freeze risk during compliance reviews; (d) PIPEDA data-residency restrictions apply when Canadian personal information is processed offshore; and (e) e-commerce settlements from Stripe and credit-card acquirers are best received directly into a Schedule I bank account rather than through a non-bank intermediary payer. Recommended architecture: a Schedule I bank as the sole operating, capital-escrow, and trade-settlement hub; a regulated EMI (for example, a UK-FCA-authorised or US-MSB-registered provider) may be used only for low-value niche vendor payouts under a written treasury policy with contractual audit rights over the EMI's own KYC files.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium as an ancillary payout rail; Low as a primary treasury or operating-account substitute. Legal constraints: (a) offshore digital EMIs are not Canadian chartered banks under the Banking Act (S.C. 1991, c. 46) and cannot provide Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC) deposit coverage; (b) an EMI providing payment or payout services to Canadian residents in CAD, or holding funds on behalf of Canadians, may trigger a FINTRAC Foreign Money Services Business (FMSB) registration obligation under the PCMLTFA, including a full AML/ATF compliance program, a designated compliance officer, and Chinese-wall record-keeping duties, despite lacking any Canadian bank license; (c) offshore EMIs are not direct participants in Payments Canada's Lynx or Real-Time Rail, so all CAD settlement is executed through a Canadian correspondent bank, adding latency, pricing spreads, and freeze risk during compliance reviews; (d) PIPEDA data-residency restrictions apply when Canadian personal information is processed offshore; and (e) e-commerce settlements from Stripe and credit-card acquirers are best received directly into a Schedule I bank account rather than through a non-bank intermediary payer. Recommended architecture: a Schedule I bank as the sole operating, capital-escrow, and trade-settlement hub; a regulated EMI (for example, a UK-FCA-authorised or US-MSB-registered provider) may be used only for low-value niche vendor payouts under a written treasury policy with contractual audit rights over the EMI's own KYC files.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada's transfer pricing documentation threshold is CAD 1 million in gross revenue for the taxation year, exclusive of non-resident transactions; compliance with the Canada Revenue Agency's TP documentation requirements (Local File and Master File) is mandatory for transactions subject to the Income Tax Act section 247.

Economic substance scope: The Income Tax Act's general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under section 245 applies to transactions lacking economic substance, including artificial allocations of profit to low-tax jurisdictions. Ontario does not currently maintain a standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime; however, the federal CRA and provincial tax authorities may challenge incentive structures lacking commercial substance. Moreover, if the limited partnership is resident in Canada, Canadian tax applies on worldwide income and the OECD BEPS principles may be relevant.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Criminal offence under section 239 of the Income Tax Act: fine of no less than 50% and no more than 200% of the tax evaded, plus imprisonment of up to 5 years. Civil penalties may also be imposed under the Income Tax Act (e.g., 5% plus 1% per month for late filing), and civil assessments can include interest and gross negligence penalties of up to 50% of the understated tax.

Director personal liability: Director and officer liability under section 227.1 of the Income Tax Act for failure to remit source deductions; for a limited partnership, general partners can be held personally liable for partnership tax debts if they have management authority and are grossly negligent. Criminal tax evasion may lead to imprisonment. Moreover, CRA may issue a compliance order, freeze assets, and apply for a directive that civil fraud remedies and director restitution be paid from partnership assets or personal estates.
Shell Company / Economic Substance Red Flag

Statutory sanction: The CRA can re-assess the partnership's tax filings under GAAR (section 245), disallow the transfer pricing benefit, impose a 10% penalty on the excessive portion of the adjustment, and add gross negligence penalties. In egregious cases, criminal charges may be pursued for misrepresentation. Provincially, Ontario may exercise its regulatory authority to revoke corporate/partnership registrations if the entity fails to file annual returns or is found to be misrepresenting its place of business.

Director personal liability: General partners (and possibly limited partners exercising control) may have their limited liability shield pierced if the tribunal finds the partnership was used as a sham; the CRA may assert civil liability against directors/general partners for unpaid taxes, penalties, and interest, and may seek a personal bankruptcy order or a travel ban against individuals who attempt to leave Canada with unpaid assessments.
AML / Proceeds of Crime

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties (AMPs) can be up to CAD 1,000,000 for a corporation or partnership in respect of certain PCMLTFA violations; criminal sanctions for laundering the proceeds of crime can result in imprisonment up to 10 years under the Criminal Code. FINTRAC enforcement can also issue suspension of licenses, compliance orders, or begin criminal prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and general partners may be personally liable based on a failure to implement and enforce a compliant AML program. Courts may impose restitution orders, director disqualification, travel bans, and in serious cases, criminal imprisonment for individuals directly participating in laundering operations.
Failure to Withhold or Remit Part XIII Tax

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act, the payer is liable for the full amount of withholding tax that should have been remitted, plus interest and penalties (typically 5% of the amount, 1% per month, and 10% on gross negligence). CRA may assess an additional penalty under subsection 227(10) for repeated failures.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be jointly and severally liable under subsection 227.1 for the unpaid tax and penalties to the extent the failure happened while they were directors. In cases of willful avoidance, criminal charges could be pursued, subject to fine and imprisonment.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Section 247 of the Income Tax Act authorizes the CRA to adjust the amount to an arm's length price and impose a 10% transfer pricing penalty (plus arrears interest). The penalty cannot be waived by a competent authority. Sustained non-compliance may lead to criminal tax evasion if concealment is proved.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable under the umbrella of section 227.1 for unpaid withholding surcharges; in egregious cases, a court may pierce the corporate veil if the transfer-pricing scheme was a sham, leading to personal liability for the company’s tax debt.
Environmental and Product Safety Non-Compliance (CSA / FCC / UKCA)

Statutory sanction: The Canada Consumer Product Safety Act allows fines of up to CAD 100,000 for a first offence and up to CAD 250,000 for any subsequent offence, plus possible imprisonment for up to 2 years for knowingly contravening an order. The Competition Bureau may also pursue misleading advertising remedies. If the product causes injury, civil product liability claims may be significant.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be charged under the Act and are subject to criminal/regulatory penalties if they knowingly or recklessly participate in the distribution of unsafe devices, leading to financial restitution, product recall costs, and potential imprisonment.
False Representation / Import and Export Controls

Statutory sanction: Under the Customs Act, customs penalties may be up to 50% of the value of the goods; the Competition Bureau may levy up to CAD 10 million in fines for misleading advertising. Export violations under the Export and Import Permits Act are criminal offences and may result in substantial fines, imprisonment, and loss of export privileges.

Director personal liability: Officers and directors can face criminal corporate liability, personal monetary fines, and imprisonment if they authorize or participate in false import declarations; a travel ban may be imposed pending investigation, and they may be disqualified as directors in Ontario.
