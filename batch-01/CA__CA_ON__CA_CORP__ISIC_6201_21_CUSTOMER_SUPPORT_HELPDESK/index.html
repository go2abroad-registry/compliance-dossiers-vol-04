Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – My Business Account portal and Non-Resident GST/HST Registration portal (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services.html)

E-government filing portal

A non-resident corporation providing digital services to Canadian recipients must obtain a Business Number (BN) and a GST/HST account by filing Form RC1 or using the CRA's online non-resident registration portal. The applicant must provide certified foreign articles of incorporation, proof of foreign tax residence, director identification, and the foreign tax identification number. For a Canadian-incorporated Business Corporation, incorporation is completed under the Canada Business Corporations Act or Ontario Business Corporations Act, and the CRA automatically issues the BN upon incorporation; registration for GST/HST can be added separately. No minimum capital is required, and a Canadian registered office is necessary for Ontario corporations.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not have a check-the-box regime comparable to the United States. Available elections include: the closely related corporations election under section 150 of the Excise Tax Act (to eliminate GST/HST on certain intercorporate supplies); the joint venture election under section 273 of the Excise Tax Act (permitting election into a GST/HST joint venture for qualifying activities); and the associated corporations election under the Income Tax Act (for allocation of the small business deduction). Hybrid mismatch rules follow the OECD recommendations and are contained in the Income Tax Act; no elective hybrid mismatch neutralization is available.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies (for non-residents, taxable supplies made in Canada over any trailing 12-month period; the small supplier threshold is CAD 30,000)

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rated export treatment is available under sections 21 to 29 of the Excise Tax Act. Goods exported from Canada are zero-rated, and services (including helpdesk and cloud SaaS) supplied to a non-resident recipient are zero-rated if the recipient is not resident in Canada at the time the service is performed and the service is not in connection with real property or tangible personal property situated in Canada. The supplier must retain full documentation showing the supply was made to a non-resident and that payment was received in the ordinary course of trade, including contracts, invoices, and evidence of outbound service delivery.

Canada does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or government pre-clearance of invoices. A GST/HST registrant must issue an electronic or paper invoice containing prescribed information (vendor and recipient identification, description, tax amount, and GST/HST rate). Mandatory electronic filing through the CRA's GST/HST NETFILE or the GST/HST E-Filing service applies to registrants whose annual taxable supplies exceed CAD 10 million; all other registrants may file electronically or on paper. No API-based fiscal device connectivity is required.

The CRA may reassess a GST/HST return within four years (or indefinitely where a taxpayer has made a misrepresentation that is attributable to neglect, carelessness, or wilful default or has committed fraud). If export zero-rating is disallowed, the exporter is liable for uncollected GST/HST, plus compound interest calculated daily at the prescribed rate. Penalties under the Excise Tax Act include a 20% penalty on any net tax evaded, and gross negligence penalties of up to 50% of the additional tax payable. Criminal prosecution is possible for wilful evasion, with fines up to CAD 200,000 or imprisonment of up to two years, or both.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible under the Currency Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. C-52). Canada operates an open capital account with no exchange-control legislation, no prior-approval regime, and no formal distinction between current-account and capital-account transactions. Profit remittance is unrestricted at the FX level; the only fiscal impediment is Part XIII non-resident withholding tax of 25% on dividends paid to non-residents, treaty-reduced (e.g., 5% for a US corporate shareholder holding at least 10% of voting shares under the Canada-US Tax Convention). A Helpdesk SaaS corporation may invoice and hold USD/EUR, convert currencies at its own treasury discretion, and repatriate funds without central-bank approval. Compliance duties are transactional only: FINTRAC electronic funds transfer reporting for remittances of CAD 10,000 or more, and CBSA physical currency declaration for cash or bearer instruments crossing the border.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canadian federal and Ontario law impose no statutory surrender timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no repatriation deadline for export or SaaS service receipts. Revenue generated under ISIC 6201 / NAICS 511210 may be retained indefinitely in foreign currency. The only applicable duty is the PCMLTFA cross-border reporting regime: physical import or export of currency or monetary instruments of CAD 10,000 or more must be declared to CBSA on Form E677, and non-physical wire receipts are automatically reported to FINTRAC by the receiving financial institution without any separate corporate filing.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as monetary authority and, since the Retail Payment Activities Act regime, as the regulator for registered payment service providers; Department of Finance Canada retains exchange-rate and financial-sector policy authority; Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) supervises Schedule I banks; Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) enforces the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA); Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) administers cross-border currency declaration forms.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Form T106 is mandatory when non-arm's-length cross-border transactions exceed CAD 1,000,000 in aggregate in the taxation year; country-by-country reporting is required for multinational groups with consolidated group revenue of at least CAD 750,000,000; contemporaneous OECD-style Local File / Master File documentation is expected for any significant related-party transaction to protect against transfer-pricing penalties.

Economic substance scope: Ontario and Canada do not apply offshore Economic Substance Regulations. The CRA instead enforces legal substance through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule, the doctrine of sham or colourable transactions, corporate-residence rules based on central management and control, thin-capitalisation restrictions, and section 247 transfer-pricing recharacterisation.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Statements

Statutory sanction: Criminal liability under Income Tax Act section 239: fine from 50% to 200% of the evaded tax, a maximum CAD 50,000 fine on summary conviction, and imprisonment up to 5 years on indictment; civil gross-negligence penalty under section 163(2) of 50% of the understated tax plus daily arrears interest.

Director personal liability: Directors who signed or directed the false filing are treated as parties to the offence; they may face imprisonment, extradition under bilateral criminal law, Interpol alerts, and civil restitution for any personal benefit received from the concealment.
Shell Company / Transfer Pricing Abuse

Statutory sanction: CRA may recharacterise the arrangement under section 247(2), disallow deductions, and impose a 10% transfer-pricing penalty where the adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 and 10% of gross revenue; GAAR can eliminate the entire tax benefit from the round-trip structure.

Director personal liability: Where the structure is a sham or fraudulent conveyance, courts may pierce the corporate veil; directors may be personally assessed under section 160 for assets transferred out of the corporation, and CRA can freeze or seize personal bank accounts and property.
Unremitted Payroll Withholdings / GST-HST

Statutory sanction: The corporation is liable for unremitted amounts, statutory penalties, and compounded daily interest; under Income Tax Act section 227.1 and Excise Tax Act section 323, directors are jointly and severally liable for the full unremitted amounts without any requirement of fraudulent intent.

Director personal liability: CRA may assess directors personally, file liens against personal residences, garnish salaries, and oppose discharge in bankruptcy if the failure resulted from the director's own negligence, reckless conduct, or knowing participation.
AML / Sanctions / Payment-Rail Compliance

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, failure to meet AML obligations can trigger administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 2,000,000 and imprisonment for officers in aggravated cases; breaches of Canadian sanctions under SEMA or SIMSA may attract corporate fines up to CAD 25,000,000.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly arrange, direct, or benefit from evasive payment schemes may be charged for money laundering or sanctions evasion, subjected to asset forfeiture, barred from corporate directorships, and restrained from international travel pending criminal proceedings.
