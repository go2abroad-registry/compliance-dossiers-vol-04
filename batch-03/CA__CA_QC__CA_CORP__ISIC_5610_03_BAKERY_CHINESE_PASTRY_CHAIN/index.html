Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation. The Business Corporation is a distinct taxpayer under Part I of the Income Tax Act and the Quebec Taxation Act; it has no pass-through eligibility, and shareholders are taxed only on dividends or capital gains. Territorial_Scope: a Canadian-resident corporation is taxable on worldwide income; a non-resident corporation is taxable on Canadian-source employment income, business income carried on in Canada, and taxable Canadian property gains. Tax_Exempt_Zone: none; no Canadian province offers an income tax exemption zone, although customs duty relief is available through bonded warehouses and customs-controlled areas. The corporation is eligible for the Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) small business deduction only if owned by Canadian residents, with the federal 9% and Quebec 3.2% reduced rates on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income, subject to associated-corporation and taxable-capital limits; where foreign ownership applies, the general combined rate of 26.5% (federal 15% plus Quebec 11.5%) applies.

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box election exists in Canada; an incorporated entity cannot elect partnership or flow-through treatment. Material elections and statutory mechanisms include: (1) allocation agreement among associated corporations under subsection 125(6) of the Income Tax Act to divide the CAD 500,000 small business limit; (2) the refundable dividend tax on hand election under subsection 129(7) for dividend refund planning; (3) the section 216 election for a non-resident owning Canadian rental real estate to be taxed on net rental income rather than Part XIII gross withholding; (4) voluntary GST/HST and QST registration under section 240 of the Excise Tax Act even before the small-supplier threshold is crossed, allowing input tax credit recovery; and (5) OECD hybrid mismatch measures, which are mandatory anti-avoidance rules in the Income Tax Act (effective primarily for payments made after June 30, 2023, and mirrored under the Quebec Taxation Act) that deny deductions or require inclusion where hybrid instruments or entities produce deduction/non-inclusion outcomes or duplicate deductions. No election can oust these hybrid-mismatch provisions.

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies in the preceding four consecutive calendar quarters (the small-supplier threshold under section 148 of the Excise Tax Act and the equivalent QST provision; same CAD 30,000 threshold applies to QST). A person who ceases to be a small supplier at any time must register within 30 days, and, once registered, must collect tax on all subsequent taxable supplies. Non-resident suppliers of specified digital services or goods delivered through a distribution platform/fulfillment warehouse in Canada have mandated registration independent of the CAD 30,000 threshold, effective as of July 1, 2021, for GST/HST and QST.

Canada has no mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or certification-of-invoice regime. CRA and Revenu Quebec accept paper, EDI, PDF, and structured XML invoices; the Pan-Canadian Peppol interoperability framework is voluntary in business-to-government and business-to-business procurement, not a tax clearance requirement. A compliant tax invoice must contain the supplier's BN (and QST registration number), the purchaser's name, the date, a description of the supply, the GST/HST and QST amounts or rates, and the total consideration; these data elements are the gateway to the purchaser's input tax credit claim under subsection 169(4) of the Excise Tax Act and section 25 of the Quebec Sales Tax Act. No API-based tax authority clearance or digital fiscal seal is required before a sale.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is freely convertible for ordinary commercial, current-account, and capital-account transactions. Canada generally has no approval requirement, exchange-control quota, mandatory conversion of foreign currency, or restriction on repatriation of paid-in capital, dividends, royalties, management fees, loan principal, or interest. Remittances remain subject to corporate-law authorization, solvency requirements, beneficial-ownership and source-of-funds checks, sanctions screening, transfer-pricing rules, thin-capitalization rules, withholding tax, treaty eligibility, and supporting commercial documentation. A Canadian corporation may maintain foreign-currency accounts and retain export receipts offshore or domestically, subject to bank policies, tax reporting, accounting, and anti-money-laundering controls. Payments to non-residents may generally require Canadian withholding tax, commonly at the 25% statutory rate unless reduced or eliminated under an applicable tax treaty and properly documented.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no mandatory surrender of export proceeds, no statutory conversion ratio, and no general deadline requiring exporters to convert foreign-currency receipts into Canadian dollars. Exporters must comply with customs, sanctions, tax, accounting, and contractual reporting obligations. Goods exports generally require appropriate Canada Border Services Agency reporting, while services, franchise fees, royalties, and other intangible-related receipts must be supported by contracts, invoices, and tax records. Foreign-currency receipts should be reconciled to invoices and bank statements, and foreign-exchange gains or losses should be recorded under applicable Canadian accounting and tax rules. The corporation should separately assess GST/HST treatment, non-resident withholding, transfer pricing, and foreign-asset reporting where relevant.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada. Canada has no general foreign-exchange administration imposing capital-account controls. Financial institutions, money services businesses, and payment service providers may also be subject to the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, FINTRAC requirements, sanctions legislation, and applicable Bank of Canada payment-system and retail-payment-supervision rules.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for supplementary payment functionality and Low as a replacement for a primary Canadian corporate bank. An offshore electronic-money institution may be usable for limited collections, card acquiring, foreign-currency conversion, or vendor payments only if its licensing, territorial permissions, correspondent-bank access, sanctions controls, and Canadian regulatory status are acceptable. The provider must not conduct regulated payment, money-services, remittance, or deposit-taking activity in Canada without the required registration, authorization, or exemption. Relevant regimes may include FINTRAC registration and compliance obligations for money-services activity and the Retail Payment Activities Act for covered payment-service activities. Offshore EMI balances may not provide Canadian deposit-insurance protection and may be restricted by Canadian banks, payment networks, franchise counterparties, or tax auditors. Maintain a Canadian operating account as the primary treasury and settlement account.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Combined general corporate rate: 26.5% (federal 15% net of provincial abatement + Quebec 11.5%). Active business income of a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) up to CAD 500,000: combined 12.2% (federal 9% + Quebec 3.2%), subject to phase-out based on taxable capital and passive income.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the federal Income Tax Act, applicable to dividends paid or credited by a Canadian-resident corporation to a non-resident shareholder; a corresponding Quebec tax is levied on the same base.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

T2 and CO-17 must be filed by the last day of the 6th month following the fiscal year-end (e.g., June 30 for a December 31 year-end). The balance of corporate income tax is due by the last day of the 2nd month after fiscal year-end, or the 3rd month for an eligible CCPC whose taxable income is at or below the small business limit. Monthly instalments are due throughout the year.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File / contemporaneous TP documentation is required where the total annual gross revenue from transactions with a single non-arm's length non-resident reaches or exceeds CAD 1,000,000. Master File and Country-by-Country Report are required for multinational enterprise groups with consolidated revenue of CAD 1,100,000,000 or more, consistent with CRA's OECD-aligned filing requirements.

Economic substance scope: Canada and Quebec do not enact a separate Economic Substance Regulations statute of the kind found in zero-corporate-tax jurisdictions. However, the federal General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s. 245 ITA), the Quebec GAAR equivalent, the common law sham doctrine, and the central management and control test for corporate residence jointly require demonstrable economic substance. A Chinese pastry bakery chain that centralizes trademark ownership, intercompany royalty streams, and a central kitchen in Quebec must show physical premises, local employees, active board decision-making in Canada, and real operational risk. A structure that is a bare conduit or letterbox company will be re-characterized, the royalty deduction denied, and the 50% GAAR penalty imposed in addition to reassessed tax.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion – Suppressed Cash Sales

Statutory sanction: Federal: Income Tax Act s. 239(1) – fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for up to 5 years; gross negligence penalty of 50% of the understated tax under s. 163(2). Quebec: Tax Administration Act – penalty of 50% of the evaded tax and imprisonment for up to 5 years. The CRA Voluntary Disclosures Program will deny relief where the non-disclosure was wilful.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who directed or participated in the evasion are jointly and severally liable for the corporate tax, fines, and restitution; they face personal criminal prosecution, imprisonment, CRA jeopardy orders freezing assets, and court-ordered travel bans preventing departure from Canada while the assessment is in dispute.
Shell Company / Sham Conduit

Statutory sanction: The General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s. 245 ITA) empowers CRA to deny the tax benefit and re-characterize the whole arrangement; a 50% penalty applies to the tax benefit obtained. Revenu Québec applies a parallel provincial GAAR. At common law the arrangement may be void as a sham, and CRA may disregard the corporate entity and assess the foreign principal directly on Canadian-source income.

Director personal liability: Piercing the corporate veil exposes directors to personal liability for corporate debts, unremitted source deductions, and GST/QST. Directors who knowingly sign false or misleading returns face criminal fines and imprisonment under s. 239; the courts may also impose personal restitution equal to the tax avoided.
Transfer Pricing Non-Documentation / Excess Royalty

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess the royalty and markup to an arm's length amount under s. 247(2); a 10% penalty under s. 247(3) applies to the net transfer pricing adjustment where the adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5,000,000 or 10% of the taxpayer's gross revenue. Compound daily interest accrues from the date the return was due.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly approve inflated intercompany royalties or who sign declarations containing false TP information may be assessed for gross negligence; the corporate penalty attaches first, but directors may be pursued where the overpayment is combined with unpaid source deductions or GST/QST, and criminal prosecution is available in cases of deliberate falsification.
AML / FINTRAC Reporting Failure

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, FINTRAC may impose an administrative monetary penalty of up to CAD 500,000 per violation on a corporation; criminal conviction is punishable by a fine of up to CAD 1,000,000 and/or imprisonment for up to 5 years. Revenu Québec may additionally assess penalties for undeclared cash income and unreported GST/QST.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who directed or authorized the concealment may be prosecuted individually, with personal fines and imprisonment upon conviction. They are also personally liable for the corporation's unremitted GST/QST and payroll source deductions where the cash scheme is part of a deliberate plan to suppress taxable sales.
