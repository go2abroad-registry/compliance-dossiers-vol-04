Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC), federal administration of corporate income tax, GST/HST, payroll and Part XIII non-resident withholding; portal: Business Registration Online (BRO) and My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html). Ontario corporate income tax is collected by the CRA under the Ontario-CRA tax collection agreement; there is no separate Ontario tax registration for income tax.

E-government filing portal

Corporate existence arises on filing Articles of Incorporation under the Canada Business Corporations Act (CBCA) or Ontario Business Corporations Act (OBCA). The CRA thereafter issues the statutory nine-digit Business Number (BN) with program accounts: RT0001 (GST/HST), RC0001 (Corporation Income Tax), RP0001 (Payroll) and RN0001 (Non-Resident Withholding). Foreign incorporators may register remotely through BRO; a Canadian registered office and one or more resident or permissible non-resident directors are statutory requirements. GST/HST registration becomes mandatory within 29 days of the day the small-supplier threshold of C$30,000 is exceeded. Where the fund supplies only exempt financial services domestically, voluntary registration is recommended to zero-rate exported financial services and to claim input tax credits on financing, legal, due diligence and administrative inputs attributable to those exports.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation — a CBCA/OBCA business corporation is a separate legal taxpayer resident in Canada by virtue of incorporation under subsection 250(4) of the Income Tax Act (ITA); taxed on worldwide income with no flow-through attributes. General combined federal/Ontario corporate rate is 25% (federal 15% plus Ontario 10%); the small-business deduction rate of 12.2% is unavailable where the corporation is controlled by non-residents. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — a CBCA/OBCA business corporation is a separate legal taxpayer resident in Canada by virtue of incorporation under subsection 250(4) of the Income Tax Act (ITA); taxed on worldwide income with no flow-through attributes. General combined federal/Ontario corporate rate is 25% (federal 15% plus Ontario 10%); the small-business deduction rate of 12.2% is unavailable where the corporation is controlled by non-residents. As a standalone Canadian corporation, no branch-tax or treaty-branch attribution applies.

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box entity classification election exists; Canadian corporate status is statutory. Material elections available include: (i) subsection 85(1) ITA rollover election on qualifying asset transfers to corporations; (ii) subsection 83(2) election designating a Capital Dividend Account distribution; (iii) section 156 joint election by corporate partners to harmonize taxation years; (iv) subsection 256(9) associated-corporation designation; and (v) subsection 216(4) deemed-disposition election for rental real property income. Hybrid mismatch and EIFEL rules (ITA sections 18.21–18.26 and section 18.2 respectively, as renumbered by the June 2024 EIFEL enactment) operate by mandatory ordering rules with no elective relief.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

C$30,000 — small-supplier threshold under subsection 240(1) and section 148 of the ETA: a person carrying on commercial activity in Canada must register within 29 days after the total consideration for taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies) in a single calendar quarter or over the immediately preceding four consecutive calendar quarters exceeds C$30,000. Exempt financial supplies (e.g., lending to Canadian residents) do not count toward the threshold; voluntary registration is available and prudent for a royalty-financing fund to zero-rate financial services supplied to non-resident borrowers and to recover input tax credits.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exported goods are zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part V, ETA. Supplies of financial services to non-resident persons — including revenue-based financing and royalty-stream funding arrangements — are zero-rated under Schedule VI, Part VII, section 7 ETA, provided: (i) the recipient is a non-resident; (ii) the financial service is not in respect of a Canadian financial instrument, Canadian deposit, or asset situate in Canada; (iii) the borrower does not on-lend or deploy funds in a Canadian business or against Canadian collateral; and (iv) the supplier retains evidence satisfactory to the CRA of the non-resident status of the counterparty and the offshore use or consumption of the service. Financial services supplied to Canadian resident borrowers remain exempt supplies without input tax credit entitlement; legal fees, credit underwriting, treasury operations and administration costs allocated to exported zero-rated services qualify for full input tax credits.

  • B13A Export Declaration filed through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS) for any tangible goods or collateral crossing the border; for pure financial services, a complete contract-and-settlement file identifying the non-resident borrower and the offshore destination of the advanced funds
  • Bill of Lading / Airway Bill covering physical assets, or the executed facility agreement and transfer instructions evidencing disbursement of funds and the foreign situs of the funded royalty pool
  • Non-resident status evidence (foreign incorporation certificate, tax residence certificate, or W-8BEN-E-style certification) together with SWIFT MT103 / bank wire records substantiating the incoming flow of funds, serving in lieu of an inward remittance certificate

No real-time fiscal clearance, mandatory digital stamping, or API invoice-authorization regime exists in Canada. Federal e-invoicing is voluntary under the Pan-Canadian E-Invoicing / Peppol interoperability framework (Pan-Canadian Trust Framework launched by the CRA in 2023). Registrants with annual taxable supplies of C$1.5 million or more must file GST/HST returns electronically using GST/HST NETFILE; prescribed invoice content under the Input Tax Credit Information Regulations (supplier name, BN, HST rate, amount, recipient name) remains mandatory for input tax credit substantiation. Electronic records may be maintained if legible and accessible; CRA-approved EDI or API integration for bulk filing is permitted but not compulsory.

The CRA may reassess incorrect zero-rating within four years (unlimited where misrepresentation or wilful default exists), converting exported financial services to taxable or exempt status and retrospectively clawing back input tax credits with full accrued interest. Gross negligence penalty under subsection 285.1(1) ETA equals 25% of net tax understated; wilful evasion attracts criminal fines up to C$100,000 and imprisonment for up to two years; failure-to-file penalties range from 1% to 8% of unreported net tax per month, capped at 12 months. Cross-border financing mismatches attract transfer pricing adjustments under subsection 247(2) ITA on receivables outstanding beyond the 180-day arm's-length benchmark, plus a 10% penalty under subsection 247(3) ITA for severe understatements.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is generally freely convertible for current-account and capital-account transactions. A Canadian corporation may generally receive foreign investment, maintain foreign-currency accounts, pay foreign suppliers, remit dividends, interest, royalties, principal, and service fees, and repatriate capital without a general Bank of Canada approval. Practical hurdles arise from bank source-of-funds and beneficial-ownership verification, sanctions screening, anti-money-laundering controls, transfer-pricing and withholding-tax rules, thin-capitalization and interest-limitation rules, non-resident reporting, corporate-authority evidence, and documentation supporting the commercial purpose of royalty-financing transactions. Dividends are generally paid from legally available corporate funds and must be supported by proper corporate authorization. Canadian withholding tax may apply to payments to non-residents, subject to treaty relief and prescribed documentation. Transactions involving designated persons, sanctioned jurisdictions, restricted goods, or prohibited financing activities may be blocked or require authorization.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no general statutory deadline requiring exporters to convert or repatriate foreign-currency receipts. Export proceeds may ordinarily be retained in foreign-currency accounts or converted at the corporation's discretion. The corporation must nevertheless comply with applicable customs and export-reporting requirements, sanctions and trade-control laws, income-tax and transfer-pricing rules, GST/HST rules where relevant, books-and-records obligations, and bank transaction-monitoring requirements. Large international electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more, including linked transfers meeting the reporting threshold, may trigger reporting by the financial institution to FINTRAC. Foreign assets may create additional tax-information reporting, including Form T1135 where the statutory conditions and thresholds are met.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada. Canada has no general foreign-exchange administration imposing routine capital controls, mandatory conversion of foreign currency, or general approval requirements for inbound or outbound corporate remittances. FINTRAC, the Canada Revenue Agency, the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions, the Department of Finance Canada, the Canada Border Services Agency, and applicable sanctions authorities impose complementary compliance obligations.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for payment functionality and Low as a substitute for a Canadian operating bank. Canada does not operate a general European-style electronic-money institution licensing regime. A foreign EMI may provide services only within the scope of its home-country authorization and applicable Canadian law, and it cannot automatically accept deposits, provide Canadian payment accounts, or conduct regulated payment activities in Canada. A payment service provider carrying on a retail payment activity in Canada may be subject to registration and operational requirements under the Retail Payment Activities Act administered by the Bank of Canada, subject to statutory implementation and scope. Money-transfer and foreign-exchange services may also constitute money services business activities requiring FINTRAC registration and compliance controls. Foreign fintech access remains subject to sanctions, AML, tax, data, consumer-protection, safeguarding, and bank-de-risking requirements. The corporation should retain a Canadian deposit-taking bank as the primary treasury and settlement institution.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Route shareholder equity, preferred equity, or shareholder-loan proceeds from an account held in the documented name of the investor or lender to the Canadian corporation's bank account. Use the corporation's legal name, account number, transit and institution identifiers, SWIFT/BIC where applicable, and a consistent payment reference. Canada does not prescribe a universal foreign-exchange purpose-code system for ordinary corporate capital injections; the bank may request an internal purpose classification and supporting documents. State the purpose accurately as equity subscription, paid-in capital, or shareholder loan, and do not describe debt as equity or operating revenue. Pre-clear the transaction with the bank, provide the executed funding instrument, board or shareholder approvals, capitalization table, source-of-funds evidence, beneficial-owner information, and expected use of proceeds. Reconcile the incoming wire to the ledger and preserve the bank confirmation, foreign-exchange ticket, loan or equity agreement, and accounting entry. Consider thin-capitalization, interest-deductibility, transfer-pricing, withholding-tax, corporate-law, and non-resident reporting consequences before funding.Use the Canadian operating account or dedicated foreign-currency account for supplier, lender, royalty, and service payments. SWIFT MT103 or the bank's ISO 20022 successor may be used for single-customer credit transfers, subject to the sending and receiving banks' formats and migration requirements. Include complete originator, beneficiary, intermediary-bank, invoice, contract, and payment-purpose information. For documentary trade, use a commercial letter of credit issued or confirmed by an acceptable bank, with compliant presentation of the invoice, transport document, insurance document, certificate of origin, inspection certificate, and other stipulated documents. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: No statutory de minimis threshold in CAD; all controlled transactions require contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation. CRA risk assessment and audit selection typically focus on controlled transactions with aggregate fair market value exceeding C$1,000,000.

Economic substance scope: Canada and Ontario do not impose a formal Economic Substance Regulation, but CRA applies the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR), transfer pricing rules, anti-deferral rules (foreign accrual property income rules), and common law sham/economic substance doctrines. Actual business operations, decision-making, functions, assets, and risks must be substantiated in Canada; a royalty-financing conduit lacking substantive staff, records, or purpose can be recharacterized.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Willful Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s.239(1): fine up to 200% of evaded tax plus imprisonment up to five years; civil reassessment plus 50% gross-negligence penalty under s.163(2); CRA may also impose third-party civil penalties under s.163.2.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for unremitted source deductions and withholding taxes under s.227.1; in fraud cases the corporate veil is pierced, exposing directors to civil restitution, asset freezing, travel bans, and imprisonment.
Transfer Pricing Documentation and T106 Failures

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s.247(3): 10% penalty on the net transfer-pricing adjustment; T106 late-filing penalties of C$500 per month for each corporation plus mandatory information return penalties; CRA may reallocate revenue, deny deductions, and reassess withholding taxes.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly direct false transfer pricing positions or sign false T106 returns may be assessed as co-planners; personal civil restitution for taxes and penalties, travel restrictions, and criminal exposure for false declarations under s.239.
Abusive Treaty Shopping / Shell Corporation / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: GAAR under s.245 permits denial of the tax benefit and reassessment at the full 25% Part XIII withholding rate; treaty limitation-of-benefits or main-purpose provisions may also deny relief; CRA may impose interest, civil penalties, and seize records by warrant.

Director personal liability: Directors of a shell entity can be held personally accountable for diverted tax recoveries under s.160(2); a cross-border sham scheme may trigger criminal prosecution, travel bans, and personal restitution orders.
AML / KYC Deficiencies in Cross-Border Financing Flows

Statutory sanction: Penalties under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act: administrative monetary penalties up to C$250,000 per violation for record-Keeping/reporting failures; Criminal Code money-laundering offence (s.462.31) carries up to ten years imprisonment and forfeiture of funds.

Director personal liability: Senior officers and directors face personal criminal liability for knowingly facilitating money laundering; FINTRAC may issue penalties against the entity and individuals; civil restitution, travel bans, and imprisonment apply under the Criminal Code and applicable extradition treaties.
