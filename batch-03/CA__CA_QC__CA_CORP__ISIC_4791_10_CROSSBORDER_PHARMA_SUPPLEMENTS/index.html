Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), including the CRA Business Registration Online service and My Business Account portal; for Quebec provincial tax administration, Revenu Québec, including the clicSÉQUR – Entreprises portal.

E-government filing portal

A corporation incorporated in Canada obtains a nine-digit Business Number (BN) from the CRA through Business Registration Online or applicable incorporation and registration processes. It then opens the required CRA program accounts, including the GST/HST account (RT), payroll account (RP), import-export account (RM), and corporation income tax account (RC), as applicable. A non-resident corporation must generally apply to the CRA for a BN and the relevant GST/HST, corporation income tax, and import-export accounts using the prescribed registration process and must provide legal name, jurisdiction of incorporation, registered address, business activities, directors, authorized representative, and Canadian business details. A corporation carrying on business in Quebec or making taxable supplies subject to Quebec sales tax must separately register with Revenu Québec for QST and obtain the applicable Quebec identification number through clicSÉQUR – Entreprises or prescribed forms. Importers must obtain an RM import-export account before commercial importation. Registration for GST/HST and QST depends on taxable supplies, small-supplier status, non-resident e-commerce rules, and the place-of-supply rules.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no general United States-style check-the-box election for classifying corporations, partnerships, or disregarded entities. A Canadian corporation is generally a separate taxpayer subject to Canadian corporate income tax. Available statutory elections and regimes include subsection 85(1) and 85(2) rollover elections for eligible transfers of property to a corporation, section 86 capital reorganizations, amalgamation and winding-up rules, partnership and joint-venture elections where applicable, and elections under the foreign affiliate regime. Canadian hybrid mismatch rules can deny deductions, exempt amounts, or foreign tax credits in specified cross-border mismatch situations; they do not provide a general elective entity-classification mechanism. Treaty residence and fiscally transparent entity treatment are determined under Canadian law, the applicable tax treaty, and the relevant factual and legal classification.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, calculated under the applicable GST/HST small-supplier rules, generally measured over the relevant consecutive-quarter and preceding-four-quarter periods. Quebec generally uses a CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold for QST purposes, subject to special rules. The threshold does not necessarily protect non-resident suppliers of qualifying digital products, services, or qualifying goods supplied under special registration regimes. The applicable federal rate in Quebec is 5% GST; the general Quebec QST rate is 9.975%, producing a combined general rate of 14.975% on supplies subject to both taxes.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Canada are generally zero-rated for GST/HST when supplied for export and the supplier obtains and retains evidence that the goods were exported in accordance with the applicable Excise Tax Act rules. Goods delivered in Canada to a non-resident are not automatically zero-rated; the statutory export conditions and documentary evidence must be satisfied. Services supplied to a non-resident may be zero-rated where the place-of-supply and recipient-status requirements are met, subject to exclusions for services connected with real property, tangible personal property, litigation, events, and other specified Canadian activities. Dietary supplements and nutraceuticals must also be correctly characterized under GST/HST and QST rules; zero-rating of an export does not remove product, labeling, health-product, customs, or licensing obligations. Exporters may recover eligible GST/HST and QST through input tax credits, input tax refunds, or applicable rebate mechanisms, provided tax invoices, accounting records, export evidence, and commercial-purpose documentation are retained.

  • Canada Border Services Agency export declaration or other prescribed export reporting record, where an export declaration is required under Canadian customs rules
  • Commercial invoice identifying the exporter, purchaser or consignee, goods, quantities, values, currency, terms of sale, and destination
  • Packing list and transport documentation, such as bill of lading, airway bill, courier record, or carrier manifest
  • Proof of delivery or proof of receipt outside Canada, where necessary to substantiate export and zero-rating
  • Import or customs entry documentation in the destination jurisdiction, where available
  • GST/HST and QST sales invoice or accounting record supporting the zero-rated treatment
  • Bank settlement or inward remittance evidence where relevant to the commercial transaction; Canada generally does not make an inward remittance certificate a universal statutory condition for export zero-rating

Canada and Quebec do not generally impose a universal real-time electronic invoicing clearance or government pre-approval system for ordinary domestic B2B and B2C transactions. Registered suppliers must issue invoices or receipts containing the information required for GST/HST and, where applicable, QST documentation, retain electronic books and records in an accessible and reliable form, and support input tax credit and input tax refund claims. Electronic filing is required or strongly prescribed for many registrants and corporation income tax filers through CRA and Revenu Québec portals. CRA and Revenu Québec may require electronic data, records, or audit access. Canada does not generally require digital fiscal stamping of each commercial invoice. Customs declarations and commercial invoices must be transmitted through applicable Canada Border Services Agency systems, including the CARM framework for commercial importation.

Canada has no general foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for export proceeds and no general requirement for an inward remittance certificate as a condition of GST/HST or QST zero-rating. CRA or Revenu Québec may reassess tax where export conditions, recipient status, place-of-supply rules, or documentary support are not established. The supplier may become liable for uncollected GST/HST or QST, interest, denial of input tax credits or refunds, and applicable gross-negligence or other statutory penalties. False statements, deliberate tax avoidance, obstruction, or fraudulent refund claims may result in civil penalties and criminal prosecution, including fines and imprisonment under applicable federal or Quebec law. Refunds or input tax credits obtained on the basis of false or inadequate export evidence may be clawed back on reassessment, with interest and penalties. Customs violations may separately trigger seizure, administrative monetary penalties, assessment of duties and taxes, or prosecution under the Customs Act and related legislation.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada is an IMF Article VIII jurisdiction with full current- and capital-account convertibility; the CAD is a freely floating G10 currency and there are no exchange controls, no central-bank approval for inward equity injections, and no profit-remittance licensing. Cross-border currency conversion is unrestricted for nutraceutical e-commerce receipts. The only compliance overlay is regulatory reporting: FINTRAC cash transaction reports (CTRs), electronic funds transfer reports (EFTRs) for transfers ≥ CAD 10,000, and suspicious transaction reports (STRs). Outbound dividends/interest are subject to Part XIII withholding tax (generally 25%, treaty-reduced), which is a tax compliance event, not a convertibility restriction.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no holding-period obligation exists for export proceeds under Canadian law. Exporters may retain 100% of foreign-currency receipts in domestic or offshore accounts and may settle in USD, EUR, or CAD at their own election. For this high-risk special-licence merchant profile, the acquiring bank will nevertheless require a treasury confirmation for each cumulative FX conversion to support FATCA/CRS and Part XIII documentation, with USD merchant settlement typically swept T+1 on the bank's standard merchant tariff.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) is the monetary authority and custodian of the Exchange Fund Account under the Currency Act; there is no dedicated foreign-exchange control administration. Prudential regulation of banks is delegated to the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); AML/CTF supervision is executed by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC); in Quebec, financial-sector conduct and distribution practices are additionally supervised by the Autorité des marchés financiers (AMF).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 aggregate value of reportable non-arm's length cross-border transactions in the taxation year – triggers mandatory T106 Information Return filing and contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation; CRA may request documentation below this threshold if transactions exist.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no separate ESR regime; however, CRA/Revenu Québec apply substantive principles: General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s.245 ITA), business purpose and sham transaction doctrines, beneficial ownership analysis, and the Multilateral Instrument Principal Purpose Test for DTA relief. Cross-border e-commerce must maintain real employees, inventory, logistics, and decision-making in Quebec; otherwise DTA benefits and SBD may be denied.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Federal: criminal fine equal to 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, or imprisonment up to 5 years (s.239 ITA); civil gross-negligence penalty of 50% of the understatement (s.163(2)); Quebec: additional strict fine under the Tax Administration Act (RLRQ c. L-4.1).

Director personal liability: Crown may collect from directors personally under s.227.1 ITA for unremitted source deductions and GST/QST; criminal conviction can trigger arrest, travel restrictions, asset forfeiture and disqualification from managing corporations.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation and Thin Capitalization

Statutory sanction: Section 247(3): 10% transfer-pricing penalty on the net adjustment; reassessments with interest at prescribed rate; disallowance of interest under thin-capitalization rules; T106 late-filing penalty up to CAD 8,000.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in fictitious intercompany transactions can be jointly and severally liable for resulting tax debts if funds were available for remittance; aggravated criminal charges for false invoices can extend to individuals.
Treaty Abuse / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: GAAR denial of treaty benefits, reassessment without audit limitation for tax year; Part XIII tax at 25% on entire outflow; MLI Principal Purpose Test may deny all treaty relief on the transaction; penalty of 50% for misrepresentations.

Director personal liability: Civil liability extends to directors where earmarked funds were not remitted due to deliberate planning; prosecution for conspiracy to evade tax under criminal code carries penal terms and travel bans.
AML/High-Risk Payment Processing and Unlicensed Money Services

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act: administrative monetary penalty up to CAD 1,000,000 per corporate violation; criminal penalties of up to 5 years imprisonment for money-laundering offences; Quebec-specific orders to cease business.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers and beneficial owners can be charged personally if they authorized or failed to prevent non-compliance; travel escorts and asset seizure under Criminal Code forfeiture provisions may be imposed.
