Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), including the Business Registration Online (BRO) portal for obtaining a Business Number (BN) and program accounts. Revenu Quebec administers Quebec corporate income tax and, generally, Quebec sales tax (QST), through its online services and clicSÉQUR platform. A Quebec corporation also generally requires registration with the Registraire des entreprises du Québec and a Quebec Enterprise Number (NEQ), which is a corporate registry identifier rather than a tax identification number.

E-government filing portal

A foreign corporation conducting business in Canada should obtain a CRA Business Number through Business Registration Online or the applicable non-resident registration process, provide legal formation documents, registered address, directors, ownership information, business activities, authorized representative details, and the effective date of Canadian activities, and open the required program accounts, including GST/HST, payroll, import-export, and corporation income tax accounts as applicable. A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Quebec should separately assess Quebec corporate income tax, QST, and Quebec employer registration obligations. GST/HST registration is generally required when taxable supplies exceed the CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold, subject to special rules for non-resident digital-economy suppliers and voluntary registration. QST registration may be required under Quebec's specified-system rules, including separate rules for non-resident suppliers and specified digital economy supplies. Registration applications may require a Canadian representative, security, supporting documents, and proof of commercial activity.

Registration applications may require a Canadian representative, security, supporting documents, and proof of commercial activity.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no general United States-style check-the-box election for corporations. A corporation incorporated in Canada is generally a separate taxpayer subject to federal corporate income tax and, where applicable, Quebec corporate income tax. Relevant statutory elections concern transactions and tax attributes rather than elective entity classification, including section 85 rollovers for eligible property transfers, section 86 reorganizations, section 97(2) partnership transfers, amalgamations, wind-ups, and designated surplus or foreign-affiliate elections. Canadian hybrid mismatch rules may deny deductions or impose income inclusions for specified deduction or inclusion mismatches; they do not provide a general elective pass-through classification. A foreign entity's Canadian classification is determined under Canadian law based on its legal characteristics and applicable treaty principles.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

5%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, measured under the GST/HST small-supplier rules, generally triggers mandatory GST/HST registration. Quebec's QST has separate registration rules and thresholds, including special rules for non-resident suppliers of specified digital economy supplies; the applicable threshold depends on the supplier's residence, registration status, customer type, and supply classification. A supplier may register voluntarily before exceeding the threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of qualifying goods may be zero-rated for GST/HST when the goods are exported from Canada under the statutory export conditions and the supplier retains evidence of export. Certain supplies of services to non-residents may be zero-rated where the recipient is a non-resident and the statutory conditions are satisfied. For B2B SaaS, the result depends on the legal characterization of the supply, customer status and location, place-of-supply rules, and exclusions. A service is not automatically zero-rated merely because the customer pays from outside Canada. The supplier should verify that the customer is a non-resident, obtain satisfactory non-residency evidence, document contractual service delivery and user locations, and confirm that no exclusion applies, including services in relation to Canadian real property, tangible personal property, or services performed in Canada where the relevant zero-rating provision does not apply. Input tax credits or rebates generally require valid supporting documentation and commercial-use evidence.

  • Canada Border Services Agency export declaration or applicable export reporting record for goods where export reporting is required
  • Commercial invoice, purchase order, customer contract, and packing list identifying the goods, supplier, purchaser, destination, value, and terms of sale
  • Bill of lading, air waybill, courier record, or other carrier evidence showing export from Canada
  • Proof of delivery or foreign importation, where available
  • For zero-rated services, executed customer agreement, customer non-residency evidence, billing records, service-delivery and user-location records, and evidence supporting the applicable GST/HST zero-rating provision
  • Foreign-exchange and payment records, including bank statements or payment processor records, where relevant to commercial substantiation; Canada does not generally require an inward remittance certificate for GST/HST zero-rating

Canada and Quebec do not generally require real-time government clearance, continuous transaction controls, or mandatory electronic fiscal stamping for ordinary B2B SaaS invoices. Electronic invoices are generally acceptable if they preserve invoice integrity, accessibility, and required tax information. GST/HST invoices must contain prescribed information, including supplier identity and GST/HST registration number, date, recipient information where applicable, description, consideration, tax rate, and tax amount. QST invoices must satisfy Quebec documentation requirements where QST is charged. CRA and Revenu Quebec may require electronic filing of returns and records through their portals, and electronic records must be retained in an auditable form, generally for at least six years after the end of the relevant period unless a different statutory period applies.

Canada has no general statutory requirement to repatriate export proceeds within 180 days and no general foreign-exchange surrender rule. CRA or Revenu Quebec may deny zero-rating, input tax credits, rebates, or refunds where export, non-residency, place-of-supply, or documentary requirements are not proven, and reassess tax retroactively with applicable interest. GST/HST penalties may apply for false statements, omissions, late filing, failure to remit, or unsupported input tax credits; gross-negligence penalties can generally equal 50% of the understated tax or improperly claimed amount. Quebec may impose corresponding QST assessments, interest, and penalties. Failure to maintain records or produce supporting evidence may result in adverse assessments. Improper customs declarations may trigger Canada Border Services Agency Administrative Monetary Penalty System sanctions, seizure, duties and taxes, reassessment, and, in serious cases, prosecution. Intentional tax evasion, fraud, or false documentation may result in criminal fines and imprisonment.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada is an IMF Article VIII member and maintains full current-account and capital-account convertibility. The Canadian dollar floats freely and there is no central-bank permit required for inbound equity, outbound dividends, or profit repatriation. The only practical remittance hurdle is tax compliance: Canadian-resident payers must withhold Part XIII non-resident tax on dividends, interest, and royalties, generally 25% statutory but often reduced under tax treaties to 5% to 15%, with reporting through CRA Form NR4 and the Quebec revenue system where applicable.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no export-proceeds surrender rules, no statutory conversion ratio, and no mandatory receipt-holding timeline. Exporters may retain foreign-currency proceeds in Canadian commercial accounts or convert to Canadian dollars at market rates. Reporting is AML-driven: financial institutions must report incoming and outgoing electronic transfers of C$10,000 or more to FINTRAC, and physical cross-border movements of currency of C$10,000 or more must be declared to the Canada Border Services Agency.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) exercises monetary authority; Canada does not operate a single centralized foreign exchange control administration. The prudential regulator for federally chartered banks is the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), and the financial-intelligence unit responsible for AML/CFT supervision is the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Feasibility rating is medium for payment facilitation but low as a sole treasury bank. Offshore digital EMIs cannot directly connect to Payments Canada Lynx clearing or operate as the primary CAD settlement bank unless they are authorized or registered as a money services business under PCMLTFA and, where operating in Quebec, comply with Quebec money-services regulation under the Autorité des marchés financiers. Wise Business/B2B is operationally viable for supplier payouts, payroll in foreign currencies, and small disbursements, but it should be used only as a non-bank payout rail; primary liquidity, FX, and commercial banking must remain with a Canadian Schedule I bank.
