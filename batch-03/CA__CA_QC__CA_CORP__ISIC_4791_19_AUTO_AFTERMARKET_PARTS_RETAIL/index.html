Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation. A Business Corporation is a separate taxpayer subject to federal corporate income tax under Part I of the Income Tax Act and Quebec corporate income tax under the Taxation Act; no flow-through treatment applies. In Quebec, the corporation is also liable for the QST as a dealer if taxable supplies exceed the registration threshold.

Available election forms

Canada has no US-style 'check-the-box' classification election; corporations are taxed at the entity level. Available statutory elections affecting indirect tax status include: the section 273 ETA election allowing closely related corporations to be treated as a single GST/HST registrant; the section 156 election under the Tax Administration Act for QST consolidated filing; and, for non-resident corporations, the section 216(1) ITA election to compute Canadian tax on certain rental or timber income. Hybrid mismatch elections may be available under Part XII.6 of the ITA for foreign-affiliate surplus accounts, but are not relevant to this retail e-commerce structure.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD $30,000 in taxable supplies made by the supplier in Canada (Quebec for QST) over the current and preceding four calendar quarters, known as the small-supplier threshold; for non-resident digital or remote sellers, the same $30,000 threshold applies on Canadian sales.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating (0% GST/HST/QST) applies to supplies of tangible personal property (auto parts and accessories) and to services made by a registrant where the property is exported by the supplier or delivered to a common carrier, forwarding agent, or an outbound mail service for direct delivery to a destination outside Canada, and the supplier obtains and retains evidence that the property was exported. Services are zero-rated if supplied to a non-resident person not necessarily based on the Canadian market, provided the service does not directly relate to Canadian real or corporeal moveable property. For Quebec QST, provincial zero-rating parallels the GST/HST export rules for actual shipments out of Quebec.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Canada Border Services Agency) or Canada Customs Invoice / commercial invoice showing HS classification and export departure point
  • Bill of Lading / Waybill / Air Waybill issued by a common carrier evidencing shipment and foreign destination
  • Inward Remittance Certificate from a Canadian financial institution or foreign bank transfer advice proving foreign currency settlement and repatriation of export proceeds

No federal or Quebec real-time fiscal clearance, e-invoicing clearance, API telemetry, or digital stamping mandate currently applies to automotive parts e-commerce. Electronic invoices are accepted if they contain all required GST/HST/QST disclosures and are accessible for CRA or Revenu Québec audit. Quebec permits the use of authorized electronic invoicing solutions, but no pre-clearance or government approval of each invoice is required.

CRA and Revenu Québec may reassess zero-rated supplies retroactively within the normal period (four years for federal, three years for Quebec corporations, extended for wilful misrepresentation). Claiming zero-rating without proper export evidence attracts penalty under section 280.01 of the Excise Tax Act of 50% of the refund or credit claimed, plus unpaid GST/HST interest and arrears interest under section 280. The penalty for making a false statement or omission is 15% of the tax amount deemed collectible under subsection 281.1(1), and criminal charges under subsection 327(1) may be pursued for fraudulent evasion, subject to fines up to CAD $20,000 and imprisonment of up to five years. Revenu Québec imposes equivalent penalties under the Tax Administration Act, and both authorities may claw back input tax credits and demand immediate repayment of zero-rated export refunds.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar enjoys full de jure and de facto convertibility for both current and capital account transactions under IMF Article VIII. There are no exchange controls restricting the inflow or outflow of funds, and no prior government approval is required for profit remittance, dividend distributions, capital subscriptions, or trade settlements. Companies may convert CAD freely at commercial banks; however, cross-border transfers exceeding CAD 10,000 are reportable to FINTRAC, and remittances of earnings can be subject to Part XIII withholding tax under the Income Tax Act unless reduced by treaty.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory export proceeds surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no prescribed timeline for repatriating export receipts into CAD. Exporters may hold foreign-currency accounts, retain balances in USD or other denominations, and convert at flexible commercial rates. For statistical and customs purposes, export declarations (B13A / ACI) must be filed to the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA), but these are not currency or treasury controls.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (central bank); Department of Finance Canada and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) administer currency and transaction reporting obligations. Canada maintains no dedicated foreign-exchange administration; the federal Exchange Control Act was repealed and currency controls are prohibited under the Currency Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

High feasibility for ancillary transaction clearing, but low suitability as primary treasury destination for a Quebec-registered corporation. Non-resident fintech EMIs such as Wise, Payoneer, and Stripe Treasury are not chartered banks and cannot take deposits in Canada under the Bank Act. They must operate through registered Canadian Money Service Businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC, and only through contractual payment distribution channels. Such rails may be used legally for inbound customer settlement (e.g., Stripe/PayPal payouts) or for low-value remittances, but should never hold material capital or regulatory escrow balances. For a robust cross-border treasury, a tier-1 Canadian bank account must be the primary financial institution.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Federal general corporate income tax rate: 15%. Quebec general corporate income tax rate: 11.5%. The combined general rate is 26.5%, before provincial allocation adjustments and applicable credits. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) may receive the federal small business rate of 9% on eligible active business income within the federal business limit; Quebec's small-business deduction can reduce the Quebec rate to 3.2% where its eligibility conditions are met. Retail corporations must assess Quebec's prescribed-hours and other eligibility tests; non-Canadian-controlled corporations generally cannot claim the CCPC federal small business deduction.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The Canadian domestic Part XIII withholding rate on dividends paid or credited to a non-resident is generally 25% of the gross amount. An applicable tax treaty may reduce the rate, commonly to 15% or 5% for qualifying corporate shareholders meeting ownership conditions; the precise rate depends on the treaty, beneficial ownership, and any limitation provisions.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general CAD threshold that exempts cross-border non-arm's-length transactions from the arm's-length rule or contemporaneous-documentation requirement. Under Income Tax Act section 247, contemporaneous documentation is generally required for relevant transactions with non-residents. The specific transfer-pricing penalty threshold includes an adjustment exceeding the lesser of 10% of gross revenue and CAD 5 million, subject to statutory conditions. Canada does not impose a general OECD-style Master File and Local File submission regime; specified large multinational groups may have country-by-country reporting obligations, generally where consolidated group revenue meets the CAD 1 billion threshold.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no general standalone Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to certain offshore jurisdictions. Canadian tax results remain subject to transfer-pricing rules, treaty anti-abuse provisions, the general anti-avoidance rule, beneficial-ownership principles, sham doctrine, and applicable specific anti-avoidance rules. A corporation must maintain genuine commercial records and support its functions, assets, risks, and intercompany charges.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion, false statements, or wilful failure to comply

Statutory sanction: Civil tax reassessment, interest, and penalties may apply. Criminal prosecution under the Income Tax Act can result in fines of 50% to 200% of tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years; indictable offences may carry imprisonment of up to 14 years, depending on the offence and circumstances.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for corporate income tax solely by reason of office. Personal criminal liability may arise for directors who participate in or authorize an offence. Director-liability provisions may apply to specified source deductions and other statutory remittances. Civil or criminal consequences depend on the evidence and statutory basis; travel restrictions are not an automatic tax penalty.
Transfer-pricing non-compliance

Statutory sanction: The CRA may adjust income and impose a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment where statutory thresholds and conditions are met, in addition to tax and interest. Separate information-return penalties may apply.

Director personal liability: Corporate reassessments and transfer-pricing penalties are generally imposed on the corporation. Directors may face personal exposure for participation in tax offences or under a specific statutory liability provision; veil piercing is not automatic.
Failure to withhold or remit non-resident tax

Statutory sanction: The payer may be liable for unremitted Part XIII tax, interest, and penalties, including penalties for failure to deduct or remit and failure to file required information returns. Treaty relief is not available solely on the basis of a recipient's unsupported assertion.

Director personal liability: Director liability depends on the applicable statutory provision and the director's due-diligence defence. Personal liability is not automatic; criminal liability requires the relevant offence and proof.
Corporate tax-return or information-return default

Statutory sanction: Late-filing penalties, information-return penalties, reassessment, and interest may apply. Persistent non-compliance can result in collection action and prosecution where statutory elements are met.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal consequences only under applicable statutory provisions or for their own participation in an offence. Corporate tax debts do not generally become directors' personal debts merely because they are directors.
Money laundering, proceeds of crime, or sanctions evasion

Statutory sanction: The Criminal Code and applicable sanctions legislation may impose asset forfeiture, substantial fines, and imprisonment; specific penalties depend on the offence and conduct. Reporting entities may also face regulatory penalties for breaches of applicable AML obligations.

Director personal liability: Individuals who authorize, direct, or participate in an offence may face personal prosecution, fines, imprisonment, and asset forfeiture. Travel restrictions may result from criminal process or sanctions measures but are not an automatic corporate tax consequence.
