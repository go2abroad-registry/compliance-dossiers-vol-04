Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC) — federal GST/HST and income tax administration via the My Business Account portal (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services.html); Ontario HST is administered by the CRA under the Comprehensive Integrated Tax Coordination Agreement (CITCA) with the Ontario Ministry of Finance.

E-government filing portal

A non-resident entity must obtain a 9-digit Business Number (BN) by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) or by online registration through CRA's Business Registration Online (BRO) portal. For GST/HST purposes, the foreign entity applies for a separate GST/HST account under the same BN. Required documentation for non-residents: certificate of existence/registration from home jurisdiction, partnership agreement or extract, articles/declaration of the general partner, and a Canadian representative/agent contact. The BN is issued within 5-10 business days; a GST/HST account number (BN-RT suffix) is issued concurrently if the $30,000 small-supplier threshold is exceeded or voluntary registration is requested. Ontario-specific registration is not separate; the BN integrates Ontario HST and payroll (if employees are in Ontario, payroll account with suffix RP) but the LP itself is not registrable in Ontario corporate registry for tax; however, the extra-provincial LP must file a Limited Partnership Declaration in Ontario if carrying on business in Ontario.

Tax ID type & application path: Pass_Through_Eligible — a Canadian limited partnership is statutorily fiscally transparent under subsection 96(1) of the Income Tax Act (Canada): no entity-level federal income tax is imposed on the LP; each partner's share of income/loss is computed as if the partnership were a separate person and allocated to partners annually. For GST/HST, the LP is generally treated as a separate person (ETA s. 272.1) and must register in its own BN when making taxable supplies; however, a 'partnership' for GST/HST purposes without a separate legal personality may use one partner's registration if members elect jointly (ETA ss. 272.1 and 242). The LP precedent is not itself a 'territorial scope' carve-out; income taxation follows the residence of each partner, and a non-resident partner carrying on business in Canada through the LP is subject to Canadian Part I tax on its share of Canadian-source business income (subject to treaty relief under the Canada-US Treaty, Article IV(6) fiscally transparent entity rule). Ontario: no separate Ontario corporate tax applies to the LP; non-resident partners may be subject to Ontario non-resident income tax under the Ontario Income Tax Act if allocated Ontario-source income. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible — a Canadian limited partnership is statutorily fiscally transparent under subsection 96(1) of the Income Tax Act (Canada): no entity-level federal income tax is imposed on the LP; each partner's share of income/loss is computed as if the partnership were a separate person and allocated to partners annually. For GST/HST, the LP is generally treated as a separate person (ETA s. 272.1) and must register in its own BN when making taxable supplies; however, a 'partnership' for GST/HST purposes without a separate legal personality may use one partner's registration if members elect jointly (ETA ss. 272.1 and 242). The LP precedent is not itself a 'territorial scope' carve-out; income taxation follows the residence of each partner, and a non-resident partner carrying on business in Canada through the LP is subject to Canadian Part I tax on its share of Canadian-source business income (subject to treaty relief under the Canada-US Treaty, Article IV(6) fiscally transparent entity rule). Ontario: no separate Ontario corporate tax applies to the LP; non-resident partners may be subject to Ontario non-resident income tax under the Ontario Income Tax Act if allocated Ontario-source income. Tax-exempt zone: none; ghost kitchen premises in Ontario are fully taxable (no freeport/FTZ income tax holiday).

Available election forms

Canada has no check-the-box regime. Available elections: (1) GST/HST partnership election under ETA s. 242(1) to treat the partnership as a registrant rather than each member; (2) ETA s. 156 election for closely related corporations (not applicable where partners are natural persons); (3) Income Tax Act s. 216 election for non-residents investing in rental real property to claim net-basis taxation (not relevant to ghost kitchen operations unless structure includes Canadian real estate held separately); (4) s. 93.1(1)/foreign affiliate elections for upstream loans if a foreign corporate partner is used; (5) for US partners, file US Form 8832 to classify the CA_LP as a partnership for US federal tax purposes absent a per-se entity election; CRA will respect the Canadian transparent treatment regardless of foreign check-the-box elections; (6) treaty tie-breaker and permanent establishment planning under Canada-US Treaty (with PE risk triggered by delivery-only kitchen fixed place of business and dependent agents).

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — the small-supplier threshold under ETA s. 148(1): a non-resident person carrying on business in Canada becomes a mandatory GST/HST registrant once worldwide taxable supplies (excluding zero-rated exports of goods and financial services, but including zero-rated services for this threshold) exceed CAD 30,000 in any single calendar quarter or over the trailing four consecutive calendar quarters. A ghost kitchen LP with no Canadian presence but selling digital/virtual brand licences into Canada may still be required to register if threshold met; a non-resident supplying solely zero-rated services may voluntarily register for rebates. Once registered, the LP must charge 13% HST on all taxable Canadian supplies and may claim input tax credits (ITCs) for Canadian GST/HST paid on expenses.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies under ETA s. 23 for supplies of goods exported from Canada and under ETA s. 7 or ss. 9-12 for services and intangible personal property (IP) supplied to non-residents, provided the non-resident is not registered under the ETA and the supply is not made 'in the course of' a Canadian business or for consumption in Canada. For the ghost kitchen scope: (a) physical export of meal-kit goods (non-basic grocery prepared items) physically shipped from Ontario to a US customer is zero-rated if the supplier retains proof of export; (b) virtual brand IP licence royalties paid by a non-resident licensee are zero-rated under ETA s. 10 (intangible personal property supplied to a non-resident); (c) offshore franchise consulting, menu-engineering, and virtual-brand operations support services performed for US/offshore delivery-network principals qualify as zero-rated 'services performed for a non-resident' under ETA s. 7 if not supplied to a person in Canada and not in respect of Canadian real property; (d) food prepared in the Ontario kitchen and sold through UberEats/DoorDash to Canadian consumers is standard-rated 13% HST — domestic delivery does not qualify for zero-rating; (e) a US-based customer ordering meal kits for cross-border shipment may be zero-rated only if the LP (not the platform) acts as principal exporter and title transfers export-side. Required preconditions: supplier must retain documentary evidence of export, supply to non-resident, and absence of Canadian use; CRA policy P-051/P-169 permits zero-rating only when the invoice is made to the non-resident and payment is received from abroad.

  • Canada Border Services Agency Form B13A (Export Declaration) filed via CADEX/Electronic Commerce Client Registration System for goods worth > CAD 2,000 (or consolidated monthly B13A for exporters under a CBSA export permit); for low-value meal-kit shipments via courier, the commercial invoice with Shipment Reference / Airway Bill is sufficient
  • Bill of Lading or Airway Bill naming the CA_LP as exporter and the foreign customer as consignee; carrier or freight forwarder certificate of export listing airport/seaport/land border crossing date
  • Commercial invoice and underlying purchase order evidencing sale to non-resident
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / SWIFT MT103 / bank advice evidencing receipt of foreign currency proceeds within the prescribed period
  • Cargo Control Document or CBSA exit confirmation for truck/courier export of prepared food
  • CRA Form GST66 (Application for Rebate of Federal Part of HST) where applicable for foreign registrants claiming rebates on Ontario HST paid

Canada has no real-time fiscal clearance, central tax authority API clearance, or digital stamping mandate. CRA requires invoices to contain the BN, date, vendor/customer identification, and tax amount (ETA s. 169 and s. 223); invoices for taxable supplies over CAD 30 must comply. The CRA promotes voluntary Peppol-based e-invoicing (Pan-Canadian E-invoicing Initiative) but it is not mandatory. Ghost kitchens using UberEats/DoorDash payouts must ensure platform-issued tax summaries and receipts are retained; the platform may itself be the supplier of delivery service and charge HST, while the LP charges HST on the meal sale. No API-based fiscal clearance applies in Ontario as of the 2025 compliance year; a voluntary Business-to-Government e-invoicing capability is being piloted, and CRA audits rely on EDI/PDF/paper records rather than live clearance.

CRA and CBSA conduct joint targeted audits of export zero-rating claims. The burden is on the taxpayer to prove export with contemporaneous documentation (CRA policy: evidence must be retained for 6 years; CRA will disallow zero-rating if the exporter cannot establish physical removal from Canada or that the foreign recipient received the supply; a common clawback scenario arises where a ghost kitchen invoices a US intermediary but the meal or IP service is ultimately consumed in Canada, or where payment routing through a foreign entity is a sham). Penalties: (1) ETA s. 285(1) gross negligence penalty of 50% of the net tax misstated where false statements or omissions are made knowingly or under circumstances amounting to gross negligence; (2) ETA s. 280 penalty of CAD 250 plus CAD 25/day up to CAD 2,500 for failure to file or provide information; (3) ETA s. 279(2) penalty equal to the amount of any false ITC claimed; (4) Income Tax Act s. 163(2) 50% civil penalty for false tax returns on the income side; (5) criminal tax evasion under the Income Tax Act s. 239 and Criminal Code s. 380 punishable by up to 5 years' imprisonment and fines up to 200% of the tax evaded (ETA s. 327 also carries imprisonment up to 5 years and a fine of up to CAD 100,000 plus the evaded amount); (6) interest under ETA s. 280.1 accrues daily on unreported amounts, and the normal four-year limitation period for reassessment is extended to seven years for misrepresentation attributable to neglect or carelessness and indefinitely where fraud or wilful default is established; CRA may also revoke the BN or impose an immediate requirement to post security under ETA s. 296 when there is a history of failing to remit HST; in an export delivery context, the CRA may additionally require the exporter to obtain a Certificate of Origin or NAFTA/CUSMA origin evidence where food imports to the US are at stake, and the US FDA/Canada Border Services Agency cooperation can trigger a CBP penalty if valuation or origin declarations are false.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Under IMF Article VIII obligations Canada maintains free convertibility on both current and capital-account transactions. There are no general exchange controls, no restrictions on inward/outward fund transfers, and no prior approval for profit remittance. Diverting dividends, interest, royalties, or management-fee payments must satisfy transfer-pricing rules and may attract Part XIII withholding tax where applicable to Canadian payors; an Ontario LP passed-through profit allocation to non-resident partners is instead assessed under the applicable tax treaty and Canadian Part I/Part XII.6 rules.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender, conversion, or holding-period requirements on export receipts. A ghost-kitchen business may retain foreign-currency proceeds in any Canadian or foreign bank account and deploy them freely. However FINTRAC and Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLFTA) require reporting of all international electronic funds transfers over CAD 10,000, and CRA requires full disclosure of worldwide income on Canadian filings.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BoC) is the monetary and bank-lending authority; Department of Finance Canada and FINTRAC administer cross-border fund monitoring and AML/CFT reporting. Canada does not maintain a separate exchange-control administration.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility: Medium-to-Low for principal treasury operations. Canada has no licensed 'electronic money institution' charter; non-bank payment service providers must register with FINTRAC as Money Services Businesses (MSBs). Digital EMIs such as Wise, Stripe, Payoneer, and Brex can process marketplace payouts and low-value bills, but they do not hold deposit-account licences and lack direct membership in Payments Canada, so they cannot safely custody LP capital, escrow funds, or serve as the sole clearing bank. A Tier-1 Canadian bank hub must remain the primary liquidity infrastructure and offshore EMIs may be used only as supporting F-X settlement or virtual-brand marketplace receipt channels.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Use SWIFT MT103 or ISO 20022 pacs.008 customer credit transfer from the investor's global bank to the Tier-1 Canadian bank, quoting recipient bank BIC, Ontario LP account/transit number, and a wire instruction narrative e.g. 'LP capital contribution for Ontario cloud-kitchen operations under Limited Partnerships Act'. The transaction must be sent with purpose code CAPT or INVE under the ISO 20022 classification; the Canadian receiving bank will complete FINTRAC EMT/PART reporting for transfers exceeding CAD 10,000. For USD, settle via CHIPS or Fedwire; for CAD, settle through the Bank of Canada / Payments Canada ACL. Physical currency in excess of CAD 10,000 crossing a border must be reported to CBSA, but is not a viable funding route.Operational payments to overseas ingredient and equipment suppliers should run as SWIFT MT103 / ISO 20022 pacs.008 via the Tier-1 Canadian bank with full invoice narrative and an underlying commercial contract. For large or repeat supply contracts, use irrevocable letters of credit issued under UCP600 against presentation of compliant shipping documents; CAD domestic payments can clear through Payments Canada ACSS or the Real-Time Rail, whereas USD foreign payments clear over CHIPS/Fedwire. Marketplace payouts and merchant disbursements from DoorDash/UberEats should be routed into the operating account as pre-allocated settlement streams and then reconciled invoice-by-invoice.Outright deliverable FX forwards (CAD/USD and CAD/EUR) for genuine commercial procurement or platform-payout exposure, evidenced by underlying ghost-kitchen revenue forecastsFX swaps combining spot and forward legs to bridge timing gaps between inbound capital injections and outbound ingredient costsVanilla currency options (premium and physical delivery regimes) to hedge procurement-side foreign-currency exposures without creating speculative leverageNDFs are legally permissible in Canada only for non-deliverable cross-currency pairs where the CAD leg cannot be physically delivered; CAD-based NDFs are uncommon and may trigger OTC derivatives margin rules under CSA/OSFI, therefore internal policy should exclude them unless a clear offshore counterparty constraint existsCross-currency interest rate swaps (CCIRS) for financing structures tied to the Ontario LP debt or intercompany loans, not for speculative trading, and approval by a licensed Canadian derivatives dealer is required under securities registration rules

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility: Medium-to-Low for principal treasury operations. Canada has no licensed 'electronic money institution' charter; non-bank payment service providers must register with FINTRAC as Money Services Businesses (MSBs). Digital EMIs such as Wise, Stripe, Payoneer, and Brex can process marketplace payouts and low-value bills, but they do not hold deposit-account licences and lack direct membership in Payments Canada, so they cannot safely custody LP capital, escrow funds, or serve as the sole clearing bank. A Tier-1 Canadian bank hub must remain the primary liquidity infrastructure and offshore EMIs may be used only as supporting F-X settlement or virtual-brand marketplace receipt channels.

Stage 06

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Ontario general corporate income tax combines the federal 15% and Ontario provincial 10% rates. However, a Limited Partnership is fiscally transparent for Canadian income tax purposes; income, deductions, and credits flow through to partners. The partnership itself does not pay income tax; each partner is taxable on its allocated share. For an active business carried on in Canada by a Canadian-resident partner, the combined general rate is approximately 26.5% (federal 15% plus Ontario 10.5% net of the Ontario small business deduction where applicable). Non-resident corporate partners may be subject to Part XIV tax under the Income Tax Act, plus Ontario tax under the Corporations Tax Act.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

As a pass-through entity, the limited partnership does not pay dividends; distributions to partners are generally non-taxable returns of capital or withdrawals. Where a corporate partner (including a non-resident corporate partner) receives a distribution that is sourced from Canadian active business income, no Canadian withholding tax applies if the non-resident partner does not have a permanent establishment in Canada and the income is not derived from Canadian real property. For direct corporate dividend distributions from a Canadian corporation (if the LP holds shares), the statutory Part XIII rate is 25% for non-resident corporate shareholders; treaty-reduced rates under the Canada-US DTA (subject to limitation on benefits) are typically 5% for a 10%+ corporate shareholder and 15% otherwise, and under many other Canadian DTAs 15% or 5%.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: There is no explicit monetary threshold for preparing transfer pricing documentation; however, CRA requires contemporaneous documentation where transactions with non-arm's length non-residents exceed $1 million CAD for the year, assessed under section 247 of the Income Tax Act and CRA Information Circular IC-87-2R. For an Ontario limited partnership operating a ghost kitchen, relevant controlled transactions include management fees, franchise/brand royalties, cloud kitchen rent paid to a related party, procurement of food and packaging from related suppliers, and interest on shareholder loans. Master File is required where the MNE group meets the CBCR filing threshold: consolidated revenue of at least $750 million CAD for CBCR (CRA Form RC4640) and the OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines apply. A Local File must be prepared and filed with the partnership's Canada return upon CRA request; CRA does not require a separate local file filing but requires contemporaneous documentation.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not maintain jurisdiction-specific Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) akin to UAE/Ireland; however, the economic substance doctrine is embedded in Canadian tax law through GAAR (sections 245, 246.1 of the Income Tax Act) and in the 'carrying on business in Canada' threshold for non-residents. The CRA will disregard transactions lacking substantial economic purpose, re-characterize partnerships lacking real commercial substance (e.g., a limited partnership that is merely a shell without actual delivery kitchen operations and employees), and apply the anti-avoidance rules to deny treaty benefits. For an Ontario LP, the limited partnership must have real presence in Ontario: actual kitchen lease, operational staff, delivery contracts, capital contributions, and board/partner management, or the CRA may deem the arrangement a sham and reassess partners personally, including imposing penalties and gross negligence.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Reporting

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act section 238: summary conviction fine of 50% to 200% of tax evaded (minimum fine of $10,000 for failure to file); sections 239 and 327.1: tax evasion criminal penalty up to $25,000 fine and imprisonment up to two years for summary conviction, up to $100,000 and five years for indictable; CRA civil penalties of 50% of gross-negligence penalty, plus interest and gross-up. Also Part 1.1 penalties under section 162 for late filing.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners are jointly and severally liable under section 160 and section 227.1 of the Income Tax Act for unremitted withheld GST/HST and payroll taxes, and under common law for conspiracy; CRA may assess directors personally for any tax liabilities of the partnership where the partnership fails to remit, and may impose travel bans under section 226 of the Income Tax Act for serious evasion. Imprisonment up to five years applicable to officers and directors who knowingly participated in evasion.
Failure to Withhold and Remit Part XIII Tax

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act section 227(8) and 229: the partnership is liable for 10% of the amount required to be withheld if no withholding, plus 10% per month default penalty and interest; section 237.1 failure to remit penalties; CRA can assess the non-resident payee personally for unpaid tax, and directors' liability arises under section 227.1 for the full amount of unremitted withholding tax plus interest and penalties.

Director personal liability: General partners, and any directors of a corporate general partner, may be personally liable under section 227.1 for the withholding amount, interest, and penalties. Travel bans and asset freezes are possible where CRA issues a section 226 (certificate of tax debt) against the partnership and directors; civil restitution includes personal judgments for the full unremitted amount.
Transfer Pricing / Economic Substance Abuse

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act section 247: CRA may reassess the partnership to the arm's length price, deny expense deductions, impose withholding tax on the deemed payments, and apply penalties under section 247(3)(b) equal to 10% of the gross understated tax where documentation is inadequate (minimum $10,000, no maximum). GAAR can unwind transactions deemed abusive; international tax evasion penalties up to 200% of tax avoided; interest compounded daily.

Director personal liability: Directors of corporate partners are liable for corporate penalties under section 247 and may be assessed personally for provable claims if the CRA pierces the corporate veil based on fraud, misrepresentation, or absence of an independent corporate form; travel bans may be imposed under section 226 for failure to pay; criminal prosecution under section 239 is possible where deliberate false documentation is used.
Shell Company / Sham Arrangement

Statutory sanction: Under common law and statutory GAAR, the CRA can disregard the partnership existence, treat the foreign principal as carrying on business in Canada and deem a permanent establishment, assess the foreign entity on Canadian-source income with penalties and interest, impose Part XIII withholding on deemed dividends or royalties, and impose monetary penalties under the provincial Business Corporations Act or Limited Partnerships Act, Ontario (failure to file annual returns). CRA may issue a formal demand and collect from the general partner personally; gross negligence penalties of 50%.

Director personal liability: Courts will pierce the corporate veil where the limited partnership is used for fraud or sham, rendering the foreign parent and its officers jointly liable for all Canadian tax liabilities; the Ontario court can issue a travel ban for debtors and criminal proceedings for tax evasion; directors of the general partner may face imprisonment up to five years and personal judgments for restitution.
GST/HST Registration and Remittance Failure

Statutory sanction: Excise Tax Act sections 240, 280: mandatory registration; section 280(1) failure to register penalty equal to the amount of tax not collected plus 50% of the tax; section 280(1.1) late filing penalty of $250 per month; section 280(2) may be assessed up to 5% of net tax for failure to file; CRA garnishments, liens, and director liability under ETA section 323 for unremitted GST/HST.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners are personally liable under ETA section 323 for the full amount of unremitted GST/HST collected, plus penalties and interest, without proof of knowledge; CRA may impose a travel ban under section 226 where the debt is certified; civil restitution and bankruptcy restrictions can follow.
