Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) – GST/HST Registry and Business Number (BN) portal | https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html

E-government filing portal

A foreign or domestic entity obtains its statutory TIN in the form of a CRA Business Number (BN) and, where applicable, a separate GST/HST registration number. Registration may be completed through CRA Business Registration Online (BRO), by mail, or by fax. Non-resident applicants use Form RC160 (Application to Register a Non-Resident) and must provide evidence of legal existence, foreign tax identification, and a Canadian representative or address where required. Upon approval, the CRA issues a 9-digit BN followed by program-specific account numbers (e.g., RT for GST/HST).

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a check-the-box election analogous to the US flow-through regime. A Business Corporation is automatically a separate taxpayer. Elections relevant for indirect tax include: (i) the Quick Method Election under the Excise Tax Act (ETA), (ii) the annual GST/HST filing election, (iii) the related-entity election under Section 156 ETA, and (iv) the nil consideration inter-corporate election. For income tax hybrid mismatch rules (ITA sections 18.75–18.8), compliance is mandatory and no elective opt-out is available.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies measured over any four consecutive calendar quarters (exclusive of certain zero-rated supplies); non-resident digital suppliers without physical presence in Canada may be obliged to register under the simplified non-resident GST/HST regime if annual Canadian-source digital turnover exceeds CAD 30,000.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A zero-rating claim may be made for supplies of digital magazine subscriptions and sponsored media placement where the recipient is a non-resident of Canada and not acquiring the supply for use in Canada, or where the supply is delivered via telecommunication to an address or IP location outside Canada. Tangible goods exported directly outside Canada are zero-rated under ETA Schedule VI Part V. Services and digital products provided to non-residents under qualifying export contracts generally qualify at 0% GST/HST; in all cases the supplier must maintain documentary evidence showing the foreign status of the recipient and the export nature of the transaction.

  • Customs Export Declaration (for tangible media shipments; not applicable to pure digital downloads)
  • Bill of Lading or freight waybill (for physical export evidence; substitute with digital delivery logs for online magazine portals)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / financial institution proof of foreign-source payment
  • Digital jurisdictional evidence: IP geo-location, subscriber country code, customer contract with non-resident terms, and internal zero-rating certification

No real-time fiscal clearance, mandatory digital stamping, or government API clearance is currently required for GST/HST. Electronic invoicing is fully permitted and, for commercial VAT/GST audit purposes, e-invoices must be retained in machine-readable and human-readable formats. CRA offers API-based filing and registration services for approved software vendors and tax preparers. Records supporting zero-rated export supplies and input tax credit claims must be preserved for six years.

CRA may deny zero-rating, reverse input tax credits, and reassess any claim lacking substantive commercial export evidence. Penalties include: (i) 50% gross-negligence penalty under ETA section 285.1; (ii) demand for interest and arrears on recovered GST/HST; (iii) failure-to-register penalties up to 15% of uncollected tax; and (iv) criminal sanctions for fraudulent export schemes, including fines and imprisonment. Retroactive clawback periods extend up to four years, or indefinitely where misrepresentation or fraud is established.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible in both current-account transactions (trade in goods, services, royalties, digital subscription fees, advertising revenue) and capital-account transactions (shareholder equity injection, intercompany loans, dividend repatriation, and liquidation proceeds). Canada maintains no general exchange-control restrictions or queuing mechanisms. Profit remittances are unrestricted, subject only to applicable Canadian withholding taxes on certain cross-border payments (e.g., Part XIII tax on dividends, interest, rents, and royalties) and to reporting thresholds under the Income Tax Act and the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA). Inbound investment may be subject to review under the Investment Canada Act for control transactions or for certain sensitive media-sector activities; a digital-magazine and sponsored-media platform is generally treated as low-sensitivity, but a non-resident ownership structure should document Canadian residence and commercial substance to avoid treaty-benefit denial and remittance-file red flags.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender requirement and no mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts. Export earners may maintain foreign-currency bank accounts denominated in USD, EUR, or other freely accepted currencies. However, all cross-border electronic transfers of CAD 10,000 or more must be reported by Canadian banks to FINTRAC, and financial institutions apply the FINTRAC Large Cash Transaction Report and International Electronic Funds Transfer Report regimes. Export receipts should be documented with commercial invoices, shipping/digital-delivery records, payment gateway settlement statements, and foreign-currency conversion invoices to satisfy tax audit and banking-AML due-diligence expectations. For balance-of-payments statistics, banks may capture the purpose of payment codes for reporting to Statistics Canada, but there is no exchange-control surrender duty.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada, in coordination with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for AML and monitoring obligations; Canada does not maintain a dedicated foreign-exchange control authority, and the Minister of Finance retains residual review authority under the Currency Act and the Investment Canada Act for broader macroeconomic and national-security concerns

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium with legal limitations. Foreign digital EMI licensees (e.g., UK/FCA-authorized EMI or EU EMI) may provide payment-processing and settlement convenience for a Canadian corporation, but they are not authorized to accept Canadian demand deposits in a banking-law sense. A foreign EMI acting in Canada may be conducting money-services business (MSB) activity under the PCMLTFA, requiring FINTRAC registration and Canadian MSB licensing, unless strictly serving as a payment facilitator on a non-deposit basis. For treasury-grade liquidity, a Canadian entity should maintain a primary operating account at a Schedule I bank and use EMI/payment-facilitator rails (Payoneer, Wise Business, Stripe, Airwallex) only for collection-side settlement or for small vendor disbursements where local clearing is unavailable. Major banks may decline sweeppayments from unregistered foreign EMI pools; therefore, full onboarding with wire-transfer instruction documentation and source-of-funds evidence is recommended before relying on EMI settlement.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity injections from foreign shareholders should arrive via SWIFT MT103 to the corporation's major operating account at a Canadian Schedule I bank, using a written payment instruction that identifies the Canadian entity, the shareholder/remitter, and a purpose narrative of 'Shareholder Capital Contribution' or 'Investment for General Corporate Purposes'. The corresponding bank transaction code/commercial code should align with the IMF Balance-of-Payments category 'Direct investment – equity capital' where codes are available; if the receiving bank uses a proprietary purpose-code list, the remitter should select 'Capital contribution / intercompany investment' and include the Canadian provincial business number. Verification should include a board resolution approving the share issuance or shareholder loan, a subscription agreement, an updated Ontario ISC record showing the new investor, and bank-side confirmation of the MT103 SWIFT reference number. For amounts over CAD 10,000, the receiving bank will complete a FINTRAC International Electronic Funds Transfer Report. Inbound loans, if used instead of equity, require a written cross-border loan agreement with arm's-length interest, a Canadian cross-border loan/withholding-tax analysis, and either thin-capitalization compliance or a treaty capital-monitoring note.For vendor, advertising, host, content-licensing, and software-supplier settlements, the primary clearing rail is SWIFT MT103 for international wires in the currency of the invoice (USD, EUR, CAD). Canadian-dollar domestic vendor payments may use the Lynx high-value clearing system operated by Payments Canada; lower-value payments can be sent through Automated Funds Transfer (AFT) or the Real-Time Rail once commercially available. For high-value procurement requiring documentary security, the corporation may issue a Commercial Letter of Credit (L/C) via the issuing Canadian bank and have the beneficiary present complying documents through their correspondent bank; for digital-magazine operations this is rare, but it may apply to outsourced print-distribution partners. All outbound payments over CAD 10,000 require standard FINTRAC reporting, and the paying bank must verify the recipient and document the commercial or service basis. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium with legal limitations. Foreign digital EMI licensees (e.g., UK/FCA-authorized EMI or EU EMI) may provide payment-processing and settlement convenience for a Canadian corporation, but they are not authorized to accept Canadian demand deposits in a banking-law sense. A foreign EMI acting in Canada may be conducting money-services business (MSB) activity under the PCMLTFA, requiring FINTRAC registration and Canadian MSB licensing, unless strictly serving as a payment facilitator on a non-deposit basis. For treasury-grade liquidity, a Canadian entity should maintain a primary operating account at a Schedule I bank and use EMI/payment-facilitator rails (Payoneer, Wise Business, Stripe, Airwallex) only for collection-side settlement or for small vendor disbursements where local clearing is unavailable. Major banks may decline sweeppayments from unregistered foreign EMI pools; therefore, full onboarding with wire-transfer instruction documentation and source-of-funds evidence is recommended before relying on EMI settlement.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: No statutory transaction-level minimum threshold; however, contemporaneous documentation is essential to avoid penalties when the adjustment exceeds the lesser of 10% of the taxpayer's gross revenue or CAD 5,000,000.

Economic substance scope: Canada applies substance-over-form through General Anti-Avoidance Rules (GAAR, s.245) and judicial doctrines of fiscal nullity. The CRA may disregard an intermediate non-resident shell with no real employees, office or decision-making functions. For treaty benefits, beneficial ownership and bona fide substance are positively required; Ontario does not impose a separate Economic Substance Regulations filing, but the OECD Pillar Two and anti-hybrid rules require that digital publishing entities retain genuine operational substance.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Statement in T2

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s.239(1) – fine up to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to 2 years on summary conviction or 5 years on indictment; interest and civil penalties apply regardless of criminal outcome.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorized, participated in or acquiesced to the evasion may be personally assessed for unremitted withholding taxes and are subject to directors' liability proceedings, travel bans, asset freezes, and imprisonment upon conviction.
Transfer-Pricing Mispricing / Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s.247 reassessment of taxable income upward, disallowance of expense deductions, and a penalty of 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment where the penalty threshold (CAD 5,000,000 or 10% of gross revenue, whichever is less) is exceeded.

Director personal liability: Directors may be held liable for the corporate penalty to the extent they caused the false documentation to be prepared; in winding-up or insolvency, directorship liability can be asserted for tax debt from intercompany mispricing where the transaction was a fraudulent preference or diversion.
Failure to Withhold Part XIII Tax on Dividends/Interest/Royalties

Statutory sanction: The entire withholdable amount becomes a debt to the Crown; administrative penalties include 10% of the unremitted amount, daily compounding interest, and a 5%–10% late-filing penalty on top of the underlying tax.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable under s.227.1 of the Income Tax Act for unremitted withholding taxes; civil suits may result in personal career assessments, travel bans, and directors' disqualification under corporate insolvency statutes.
Shell Company / Absence of Economic Substance for Treaty Benefits

Statutory sanction: GAAR reassessment nullifying the tax benefit, recomputation of withholding tax at the full 25% statutory rate, interest, and consequential penalties up to 10%; the CRA can disregard the conduit company and attribute income to the Canadian payor.

Director personal liability: Courts may pierce the corporate veil if the intermediary is found to be a sham or an agent of the Canadian entity; personal liability follows for participating directors, including restitution orders, contempt travel bans, and, in egregious cases, criminal sanctions for conspiracy to evade tax.
