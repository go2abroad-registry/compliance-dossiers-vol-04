Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) - Business Registration Online (BRO) portal; canada.ca/en/revenue-agency. Ontario HST and Ontario provincial corporate income tax are administered centrally through the CRA under federal-provincial tax coordination.

E-government filing portal

A non-resident Canadian limited partnership applies for a 9-digit Business Number (BN) from the CRA by filing Form RC1 or using Business Registration Online. The partnership must submit formation documents, the limited partnership agreement, identification/beneficial-owner details of all partners, and, where applicable, a Canadian representative or receipt agent authorization. The CRA issues a program account for GST/HST (RT suffix) and may issue related accounts for payroll or other statutory obligations. Ontario provincial tax positions are integrated through the same CRA accounts.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada has no check-the-box election. A Canadian limited partnership is statutorily fiscally transparent; partners are taxed directly on partnership income. The partnership may elect under Income Tax Act s.249.1(4) to adopt a non-calendar fiscal period. Partners may jointly elect under s.96(3) to determine the adjusted cost base of a partnership interest. Hybrid mismatch outcomes are limited by the general anti-avoidance rule (s.245) and the foreign accrual property income (FAPI) rules; no broad OECD hybrid mismatch election is available.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter (GST/HST small-supplier threshold under s.148 of the Excise Tax Act); mandatory registration is triggered once taxable supplies in Canada exceed that threshold during the relevant period; no separate Ontario provincial threshold applies.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating on export is available under section 23 and Part V of Schedule VI of the Excise Tax Act. The registered supplier must establish that the pre-packaged spicy tallow hotpot soup base bricks are physically exported from Canada, whether delivered to a common carrier for shipment abroad, mailed, shipped to a non-resident non-registrant, or exported by the recipient under qualifying conditions. Offshore services are generally zero-rated under Schedule VI s.9 if performed for a non-resident and not related to Canadian real property or goods in Canada. Documentary proof of export must be retained.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA B13A / CAED electronic record)
  • Bill of Lading, air waybill, or equivalent transport document
  • Commercial invoice and packing list
  • Proof of export destination (e.g., foreign customs entry, dock receipt, or forwarder's certificate of export)
  • Bank remittance confirmation / SWIFT MT103 or letter of credit documentation
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or equivalent bank-issued remittance confirmation (where obtained)

Canada does not operate a national real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or government pre-clearance API mandate for GST/HST invoices. Electronic invoices are legally valid. GST/HST registrants must file returns electronically through CRA My Business Account or GST/HST NETFILE in specified circumstances. Import and export declaration data must be transmitted through CBSA eManifest and the CARM (CBSA Assessment and Revenue Management) system.

The CRA may demand export substantiation at any time during the assessment period. If the supporting documents are not produced to the CRA's satisfaction within the time demanded (typically 30 days), zero-rating will be denied, HST will be clawed back on the export sale, and interest plus penalties will be assessed. Civil penalties may be imposed for misrepresentation, gross negligence, or false statements under the Excise Tax Act; intentional evasion or fraud may lead to criminal prosecution and expanded reassessment periods of up to ten years.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada offers full capital-account and current-account convertibility for the Canadian dollar under the Currency Act and the Bank of Canada Act. There are no general exchange controls, no prior approval for outward remittance, and no restriction on profit repatriation by a non-resident limited partner. The only practical frictions are: (a) enhanced KYC/AML clearance for wire transfers originating from or destined to jurisdictions listed as high-risk, (b) sanctions screening under the Special Economic Measures Act and the United Nations Act, (c) withholding-tax compliance on partnership income allocated to non-residents under Part XIII of the Canadian Income Tax Act, and (d) FINTRAC reporting obligations for international electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory timeline for repatriation of export proceeds, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no requirement to surrender foreign currency earned from exports. Exporters may maintain foreign-currency-denominated operating or settlement accounts at a Schedule I Canadian bank. For tax compliance, the Limited Partnership must compute income and translate foreign currency amounts into CAD in accordance with sections 96 and 261 of the Canadian Income Tax Act, unless a functional-currency election is available and made. All cross-border funds movements are subject to FINTRAC electronic funds transfer reporting and record-keeping obligations.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); foreign-exchange administration is coordinated with the Department of Finance Canada, the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC). Canada maintains no dedicated foreign-exchange-control agency or exchange-control approval body.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Medium-High as a payments overlay; Low as a substitute for regulated trade finance. Canada does not issue electronic-money-institution licences. A non-bank payment provider must be registered as a Money Services Business (MSB) with FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and, if serving end-users in Canada, must comply with the Retail Payment Activities Act and Bank of Canada retail-payments oversight. Offshore digital EMIs such as Airwallex, Wise, or Currencycloud may operate in Canada only through a FINTRAC-registered Canadian entity or an agent arrangement with a Canadian Schedule I bank. They may be viable for high-frequency, small-value supplier settlements and FX conversion, but they cannot issue documentary letters of credit, provide banker's acceptances, or offer ISDA-governed hedging, all of which are required for this cross-border food-distribution mandate.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined federal (15%) and Ontario provincial (11.5%) general corporate income tax rate for 2024, subject to allocation rules

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

For a Canadian resident corporation paying dividends to a non-resident corporate partner, Part XIII withholding tax is 25% on the gross amount of dividends, unless reduced by treaty. For a non-resident partner receiving partnership income allocation (not dividend), the allocation is taxed as business income under Part I on a net basis if the LP has a permanent establishment in Canada; otherwise, no Canadian tax on allocation. For a Canadian corporation exiting via dividend distribution, the statutory rate is 25%.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada requires a contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation (Local File and Master File) if the aggregate amount of transactions with non-resident non-arm's length parties exceeds CAD 1 million per year. A Master File is required for entities with foreign revenue of CAD 10 million or more and group transactions over a threshold; the Local File must be prepared within 6 months of fiscal year-end. Keep documentation for 6 years from filing deadline.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a codified Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) similar to the UAE/BVI, but the common law economic substance doctrine applies via general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under s.245 of the Income Tax Act and the statutory rules for limited partnerships under the Ontario Limited Partnerships Act. A Canadian LP must have real substance in Ontario: business purpose, premises, records, employees, and decision-making; otherwise CRA may re-characterize transactions, deny treaty benefits, or apply GAAR to eliminate tax benefits. For partnerships with non-resident partners, Canada's treaty abuse rules (under MLI and domestic substance arguments) require that the partnership and each partner have a substantive business presence. A shell LP with no Canadian office or activity will be disregarded, and the non-resident partner will be treated as carrying on business directly in Canada.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion - Failure to File

Statutory sanction: Criminal tax evasion (s.239 Income Tax Act): fine up to 50% to 200% of tax evaded, imprisonment up to 2 years. Civil penalties: late filing or failure to file penalty of 5% of unpaid tax plus 1% per month (max 12 months). Gross negligence penalty under s.163(2): 50% of understated tax.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners of a limited partnership are jointly and severally liable for unpaid taxes under s.160 and s.227.1 if the entity fails to deduct, withhold, or remit. Directors can be subject to personal assessment for tax debts, interest, and penalties. Travel bans (via CRA's certification under s.226.4) for individuals owing cumulative taxes over CAD 5,000, and criminal prosecution for directors who knowingly participate in evasion.
Shell Company / Artificial Transaction (GAAR)

Statutory sanction: GAAR (s.245) may re-characterize the transaction, deny the tax benefit, and issue reassessment plus interest and penalties. Penalty of 5% of the denied tax benefit may apply under s.245(2.1). CRA may also strike the limited partnership veil under the Ontario Limited Partnerships Act if it is merely an agent of a foreign principal.

Director personal liability: General partners and directors who sign tax returns for the LP can be held jointly liable for GAAR reassessments and penalties if they authorized the transaction. Judicial risk of 'piercing the corporate veil' exposes director's personal assets for restitution where the LP was a facade. CRA may impose a travel ban on the director or general partner for unpaid outstanding taxes.
AML / Anti-Money Laundering (Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada - FINTRAC)

Statutory sanction: Administrative monetary penalties for failure to comply with the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA): up to CAD 500,000 for corporations and CAD 250,000 for individuals per violation. Criminal fines up to CAD 2 million and imprisonment up to 5 years for deliberate violations.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly fail to implement AML compliance program can be personally fined up to CAD 250,000 and imprisoned. Directors can be subject to civil liability for assisting money laundering if they facilitate undocumented wire transfers. FINTRAC can publicly name non-compliant businesses and directors.
Transfer Pricing Penalties

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing transfer adjustment (reassess income) plus a penalty of 10% of the net transaction adjustment (s.247(3)) if the adjustment exceeds CAD 100,000, unless the taxpayer made reasonable efforts to determine arm's length price. Interest applies retroactively; no statute of limitations for reassessment beyond 3 years if any misrepresentation is proven.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign the T2 return and knowingly allow transfer pricing misstatements can be assessed under s.160(1.1) as joint and several obligors for penalties. Criminal liability can follow if deliberate avoidance of tax is proven. CRA may issue a requisition for full personal bank records and property liens.
