Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); portal: My Business Account and Business Registration Online (BRO) at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html; customs and AEO/PIP administration by the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA). Ontario corporate income tax is administered federally by the CRA under the 2009 Ontario-CRA tax administration agreement; Ontario HST is collected jointly with the federal GST.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Incorporate federally under the Canada Business Corporations Act or provincially under the Ontario Business Corporations Act; foreign non-resident corporations performing AEO supply-chain audit services in Ontario require an extra-provincial licence but may alternatively register directly with CRA if a permanent establishment (PE) exists. Step 2: Apply for a 9-digit Business Number (BN) using CRA BRO (online), Form RC1, or phone registration; program accounts suffixes: RC (Part I corporate income tax), RT (GST/HST), RP (payroll), RN (non-resident withholding). Step 3: Register for GST/HST once worldwide taxable supplies exceed CAD 30,000 (small-supplier threshold); for non-residents carrying on business in Canada the measurement is worldwide and includes supplies made outside Canada. Step 4: Open a payroll account if Canadian employees are engaged and a non-resident tax account for Part XIII withholding on dividends, interest, and royalties paid to foreign affiliates. Step 5: File T2 corporate income tax return within six months of fiscal year-end; foreign-controlled corporations must file Form T1134 (information return for non-resident reporting), Form T106 (transfer-pricing disclosure), and FAR/Part I Canada schedule; Ontario tax schedules are integrated into the T2. Step 6: Register for Ontario HST through the same CRA RT account; no separate Ontario corporate tax registration is required.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — a Business Corporation (Inc./Corp.) is a separate taxpayer under the Income Tax Act, R.S.C. 1985, c.1 (5th Supp.), s.2; it is not a pass-through entity and no check-the-box regime exists. Resident corporations pay federal Part I tax at a general rate of 15% (not eligible for the 9% small-business deduction if controlled by non-residents) plus Ontario corporate income tax at the general rate of 11.5%, for a combined rounded headline rate of 26.5%. Non-resident corporations with a PE in Canada are taxable on Canadian-source business income at the same rates and are subject to Part XIII withholding on outbound payments. Territorial scope: residents are taxed on worldwide income; non-residents are taxed on Canadian-source business income, Canadian-source capital gains, and certain passive income. No tax-exempt zone applies to ISIC 7020 AEO consulting services.

Available election forms

No U.S.-style check-the-box election exists in Canada. Available statutory elections relevant to a foreign-owned Ontario consulting corporation include: (1) section 156 Excise Tax Act election to treat taxable supplies between closely related registrants as nil-consideration supplies, simplifying input tax credit recovery; (2) section 261 Income Tax Act functional-currency election for Canadian-dollar reporting in U.S. dollars; (3) section 256(3) ITA loss-consolidation election for affiliated Canadian corporations; (4) section 216 ITA election for non-residents to compute Canadian rental income on a net basis; (5) treaty-based relief filing via Form NR301 for non-residents claiming reduced Part XIII withholding under an applicable tax treaty; (6) hybrid-mismatch rules under sections 18.2/18.21 ITA apply automatically to deny otherwise-deductible payments on hybrid instruments or entities where a deduction/no-inclusion outcome arises — no elective opt-out is permitted for structured arrangements.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — a person, including a non-resident carrying on business in Canada, becomes a mandatory registrant when total worldwide taxable supplies (excluding exempt financial services) in the current calendar quarter and the four preceding consecutive calendar quarters exceed CAD 30,000. Registration is effective immediately upon breach of the threshold. Non-residents with a PE in Canada, non-residents supplying specified digital services to Canadians, and non-residents making taxable supplies through a Canadian agent may be required to register even if below the threshold. Zero-rated export supplies count toward the threshold but are attributed at their actual value.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Two statutory pathways: (A) Export of goods — section 1 of Part V, Schedule VI, ETA zero-rates tangible personal property supplied by sale when the registrant proves export by delivering the goods to a destination outside Canada, exporting them as soon as reasonably possible (normally within 60 days), and retaining transportation documents and customs declarations. (B) Services supplied to non-residents — section 7 of Part V, Schedule VI, ETA zero-rates a supply of a service made to a non-resident person, including advisory, consulting, professional, and security audit services, unless: (i) the service is rendered to a non-resident individual present in Canada at the time of performance; (ii) the service is in respect of real property situated in Canada; (iii) the service is in respect of tangible personal property situated in Canada at the time of performance; or (iv) the service is a personal service to an individual. ISIC 7020 AEO supply-chain security audit services performed from Ontario for a foreign principal, where the audit assesses foreign facilities, international trade lanes, or non-resident counterparties and the benefit is realized outside Canada, ordinarily qualify for 0% HST/GST. The supplier must be a GST/HST registrant and must obtain contemporaneous evidence of the recipient's non-resident status (foreign certificate of incorporation, board resolution, foreign address, or signed declaration).

Canada imposes no mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or API-based invoice clearance regime as of 2025. The voluntary 'Canada E-Invoicing' service is built on the Peppol PINT interoperability model and is recommended for government procurement (B2G), but is not legally mandated for commercial B2B invoices. Mandatory electronic filing applies: (1) GST/HST returns must be filed electronically (GST NETFILE/XML/TEI) by registrants whose annual taxable supplies exceed CAD 1.5 million; (2) T2 corporate income tax returns with gross revenue exceeding CAD 1 million must be filed electronically; (3) electronic refunds are mandatory for all taxes remitted via CRA when the amount exceeds CAD 25,000 (GST/HST) and CAT? For GST/HST, EFT remittance is mandatory for net tax owing over CAD 10,000; for T2 instalments and balances over CAD 10,000, electronic payment is required.

CRA may reassess a zero-rated supply within the normal reassessment period (three years for corporations that are not large corporations; four years for large corporations), and without time limit where the registrant has made a misrepresentation or committed fraud. Consequences of disallowed zero-rating: (1) reassessment of 13% HST/GST on the gross value of the supply, plus statutory interest at the CRA prescribed rate (compounded daily); (2) clawback of input tax credits previously claimed and denial of refunds; (3) gross-negligence penalty under subsection 285(1) ETA equal to 25% of the net tax amount where false statements are made knowingly or under circumstances amounting to gross negligence; (4) for income tax, the gross-negligence penalty under subsection 163(2) ITA is 50% of the understated tax; (5) transfer-pricing penalties under subsection 247(3) ITA of 10% of the net adjustment (25% for non-arm's length adjustments) where documentation requirements are not met; (6) criminal prosecution under section 327 ETA and section 239 ITA — fines up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment up to five years on indictment, or summary conviction; (7) CRA may require security under subsection 307(3) ETA before continuing a registration; (8) CBSA may suspend or revoke PIP/AEO status, resulting in customs clearance delays and loss of mutual recognition privileges. The CRA and CBSA conduct joint verification audits of export documentation, including CEECD (Customs Export Enforcement and Compliance Division) reviews and ICP (Internal Control Program) testing.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible for ordinary current-account and capital-account transactions. Canada does not generally restrict the purchase, sale, remittance, repatriation, or conversion of foreign currency. A Canadian corporation may receive foreign investment, shareholder loans, service receipts, dividends, interest, royalties, and sale proceeds without a general exchange-control approval. Practical hurdles are bank KYC, beneficial-ownership verification, source-of-funds and source-of-wealth evidence, sanctions screening, tax documentation, transfer-pricing support, withholding-tax compliance, and financial-institution reporting. Dividends and other distributions require corporate-law solvency and authorization compliance. Payments to non-residents may require Canadian withholding tax, treaty analysis, information reporting, and arm's-length documentation. Related-party cross-border financing may require transfer-pricing support and thin-capitalization analysis.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no general deadline requiring export receipts to be converted into Canadian dollars or repatriated through a Canadian bank. Exporters may retain foreign-currency proceeds offshore, subject to contractual, tax, accounting, sanctions, anti-money-laundering, customs, and financial-reporting obligations. The target entity primarily supplies professional compliance and audit services rather than goods; service-export receipts should be supported by contracts, invoices, engagement evidence, and records establishing the place and nature of supply. International electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more, or multiple transfers meeting applicable aggregation rules, may trigger reporting by the reporting financial institution to FINTRAC. Foreign assets and accounts may create CRA reporting obligations, including Form T1135 where the statutory thresholds and conditions are met.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada. Canada has no separate foreign exchange administration imposing general exchange controls. Relevant supplementary authorities include the Department of Finance Canada, the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC), the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI), the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), and the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA), where applicable.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Medium to High for payment functionality, but not equivalent to a Canadian deposit-taking bank account. Canadian non-bank payment service providers may be subject to the Retail Payment Activities Act framework and related Bank of Canada supervision, while money-services businesses may be subject to FINTRAC registration and AML obligations. A foreign electronic-money institution or payment platform, including a provider such as Wise where available, may support inbound and outbound transfers subject to eligibility, corridor, currency, transaction-size, and compliance restrictions. It generally should not be used as the sole treasury bank for capital contributions, payroll, tax payments, client-funds segregation, or material operating liquidity without confirming Canadian licensing, safeguarding, settlement, insolvency, and account-closure protections. Offshore EMI use does not eliminate Canadian corporate tax, transfer-pricing, sanctions, AML, beneficial-ownership, or foreign-asset reporting obligations.

Core KYC Rejection Triggers & Defense Dossier

Root cause: Non-resident ownership or directors with no established Canadian operating footprint

Defense evidence: Certificate and articles of incorporation, Ontario corporate profile report or equivalent status document, registered-office evidence, lease or service-office agreement where genuine, detailed Canadian business plan, customer and supplier contracts, director and officer registers, certified identity documents, residential-address evidence, and a written explanation of the Canadian commercial rationale.
Unclear beneficial ownership, nominee arrangements, layered holding companies, or inability to identify individuals exercising control

Root cause: Unclear beneficial ownership, nominee arrangements, layered holding companies, or inability to identify individuals exercising control

Defense evidence: Complete ownership chart to the ultimate natural persons, registers of shareholders and directors, shareholder agreements, trust declarations where relevant, certificates of incumbency or equivalent corporate records, beneficial-owner identification, control-person declarations, and board resolutions confirming authorized signatories.
Large or unexplained initial capital injection, shareholder loan, or transfer from an unrelated offshore account

Root cause: Large or unexplained initial capital injection, shareholder loan, or transfer from an unrelated offshore account

Defense evidence: Executed subscription or loan agreement, board and shareholder approvals, bank statements, audited or management financial statements of the funding entity, tax returns or other lawful wealth evidence, source-of-funds narrative, remittance instructions, ownership linkage, and confirmation that the sending account is held by the investor or documented lender.
High-volume international receipts inconsistent with a professional supply-chain audit and compliance business

Root cause: High-volume international receipts inconsistent with a professional supply-chain audit and compliance business

Defense evidence: Service descriptions, engagement letters, invoices, statements of work, deliverables, client onboarding files, expected-volume schedule, country and counterparty matrix, pricing methodology, proof of completed work, contracts with overseas clients, and an explanation distinguishing client revenue from pass-through amounts.
