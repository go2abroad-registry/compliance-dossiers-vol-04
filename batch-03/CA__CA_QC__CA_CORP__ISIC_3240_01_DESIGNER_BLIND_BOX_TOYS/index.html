Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — My Business Account portal (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html); Quebec sales tax (QST) administered by Revenu Québec (https://www.revenuquebec.ca); corporate income tax administered federally by CRA and, for attribution purposes, by Revenu Québec under provincial tax collection agreements.

E-government filing portal

A foreign corporation must first obtain a federal Business Number (BN) by filing Form RC1 or RC1A (Non-Resident) with CRA, either through CRA Business Registration Online or by paper application. If the entity is engaged in a commercial activity in Quebec, supplies taxable property or services in Quebec, or has a permanent establishment in Quebec, it must also register for the Quebec Sales Tax (QST) with Revenu Québec using the Registraire des entreprises du Québec (REQ) or Revenu Québec's Business Registration service. The registration application requires legal entity identification, registered office address, directors' details, proof of foreign incorporation or registration, and, if applicable, a Quebec representative for service of process. A single BN may incorporate GST/HST, payroll, import/export, and provincial QST accounts, but QST registration is separately administered by Revenu Québec and requires completion of applicable forms (e.g., REQ business number registration).

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation (Canadian corporations are separate taxable entities; no pass-through regime; resident corporations are taxed on worldwide income, while non-resident corporations are taxed only on Canadian-source income under Part I and passive income under Part XIII; foreign entity classification follows Canadian domestic law and not a US-style check-the-box election).

Available election forms

No general check-the-box election is available under Canadian federal or Quebec provincial law.Certain statutory elections exist under the Income Tax Act, including: (i) Section 216 election for non-residents to file an adjusted return for Canadian rental income; (ii) Section 217 election for non-residents to file a return with respect to pension, RRSP, and similar income; and (iii) home-country/foreign-affiliate elections under sections 113 and 95 for purposes of foreign affiliate surplus characterization. For GST/HST purposes, a closely related corporation election (Excise Tax Act s. 156) permits supplies between specified related registrants to be treated as made for nil consideration. Quebec mirrors GST/HST registration and QST election concepts under the Tax Administration Act. Hybrid mismatch rules follow OECD BEPS Action 2, but no elective classification of hybrid entities is provided; entities are characterized by domestic law.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold for GST/HST and QST registration, measured over any four consecutive calendar quarters (rolling period). A non-resident supplier carrying on business in Canada must register once taxable supplies in Canada exceed this threshold. For specified digital services and e-commerce supplies, a similar CAD 30,000 threshold applies, zero-rating is not allowed if the supply is considered domestic, and mandatory registration may arise earlier where inventory or a distribution channel exists in Canada.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply of tangible personal property (designer blind box toys) is zero-rated for GST/HST and QST when the goods are exported from Canada as part of the sale and the supplier maintains documentary proof of export. The property must be shipped by the supplier or delivered to a common carrier for shipment outside Canada, or delivered outside Canada to a non-resident purchaser, and exportation must occur as soon as reasonably practicable. For services, zero-rating applies only where the services are performed in Canada for a non-resident and are not related to Canadian real estate or Canadian tangible personal property; for goods, physical export and contemporaneous documentation are mandatory. The supplier must maintain evidence of exportation within a reasonable time; absence of documentation nullifies the zero-rated status and exposes the supply to the full GST/HST and QST rates.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA Export Declaration, Quebec export declaration where applicable, or approved CAERS electronic report)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill showing consignee address outside Canada and proof of physical exportation
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or equivalent banking/foreign exchange record evidencing receipt of foreign currency payment within the required period
  • Commercial Invoice matching the shipment value, product description, HS/NAICS classification codes, and consignee
  • Booking confirmation, port/airport terminal receipts, customs stamp, or freight forwarder certificate of export

Canada does not currently mandate national real-time electronic invoicing, fiscal clearance, or digital stamping. Registered suppliers must issue invoices containing prescribed particulars under Excise Tax Act s. 221(1) and retain them electronically where customary. Electronic filing of GST/HST returns is mandatory for most registrants through CRA's My Business Account; Revenu Québec requires electronic filing and remittance of QST for reporting periods exceeding thresholds. For imported/exported goods, customs declarations must be submitted electronically through the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) using the Canadian Export Reporting System (CAERS) or approved eManifest/ACI protocols. No pre-clearance by tax authority is required before issuing an invoice, but all invoices must be kept in a readable format for audit.

CRA and Revenu Québec may reassess the supply to disallow zero-rating if export documents are incomplete, inconsistent, or fraudulent, resulting in collection of unremitted GST/HST and QST plus arrears interest. Gross negligence penalties under Excise Tax Act s. 285 and Quebec Tax Administration Act provisions apply, and a person who knowingly makes false statements may be liable for fines up to 125% of the tax avoided and imprisonment for wilful evasion. For fraudulent refund claims, the tax authority may clawback the refund, impose civil penalties, and pursue criminal prosecution without limitation period if fraud or misrepresentation is shown. The normal reassessment window is four years, but this is extended indefinitely where misrepresentation or fraud is established. In an export zero-rating context, the CRA will also scrutinize whether the foreign purchaser was truly non-resident, whether payment repatriation occurred within the 180-day mandate, and whether the goods left Canada permanently.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible with a free-floating exchange rate; Canada imposes no legal distinction between current-account and capital-account convertibility, and there are no quantitative limits on inbound or outbound remittances. Profit repatriation via dividends is unrestricted; the sole restraint is the 25% Part XIII non-resident withholding tax, which is reduced to 5% for a qualifying US corporate parent with at least 10% voting control under the Canada-US tax treaty and to 15% for portfolio shareholdings.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No surrender regime exists. There is no statutory timeline, mandatory conversion ratio, or repatriation quota for export proceeds. Exporters may lawfully maintain foreign-currency deposit accounts in Canada or offshore; the only compliance overlay is FINTRAC reporting under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act for cross-border currency movements of CAD 10,000 or more.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); concurrent oversight by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for bank prudential regulation and by FINTRAC for money-laundering prevention. Canada does not maintain an active central-bank exchange-control authority, the former Foreign Exchange Control Act having been repealed in 1951.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant inbound capital movements use SWIFT MT103 wires to the Quebec capital escrow or operational account. The remittance is characterized either as share capital supported by a subscription resolution or as a shareholder loan supported by a written loan agreement applying the Canada Revenue Agency prescribed interest rates. Canada imposes no statutory wire purpose codes; however, where the remitting jurisdiction mandates purpose classifications (for example Chinese SAFE categories), the remittance must be coded as cross-border equity investment or trade financing. The receiving bank verifies the legal name against the REQ registry, validates UBO and funding source, and, for escrow accounts, requires a shareholder resolution and escrow instruction letter before releasing funds.Domestic treasury transfers clear through the Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) via a Schedule I direct participant. Cross-border supplier settlements use SWIFT MT103 for plastic-vinyl raw materials, packaging, and finished-goods purchases; larger periodic imports are executed through a Commercial Letter of Credit, either sight or usance, issued by a Quebec chartered bank and often confirmed by the beneficiary bank in the manufacturing jurisdiction. USD positions route through CHIPS; CAD-denominated settlements clear over SWIFT. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility: Medium. Foreign digital EMIs such as Wise, Revolut Business, Payoneer, and Airwallex may lawfully operate in Canada only as FINTRAC-registered Money Services Businesses (MSBs), not as Bank Act Schedule I deposit-taking institutions. They are effective cross-currency clearing and payout rails for the designer toy e-commerce model, but they cannot serve as the primary corporate operational account; settlement into CAD via LVTS is indirect, and onboarding requires the same beneficial-ownership and source-of-funds evidence as chartered banks.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 in aggregate value of non-arm's-length transactions with each non-resident per taxation year triggers mandatory Form T106 filing; contemporaneous OECD-aligned Master File and Local File documentation is expected for all intercompany goods, services, financing and intangible flows above CAD 1,000,000 per related party; country-by-country report notification required for multinational groups with consolidated revenue of approximately CAD 1.125 billion (EUR 750 million).

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone Economic Substance Regulations in the offshore-centre model; however, substance is enforced through the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s. 245 ITA; s. 1079.2 Quebec Taxation Act), transfer pricing rules (s. 247), CFC attribution of foreign accrual property income, and judicial veil-piercing. A Quebec designer-blind-box corporation acting as merchant of record must maintain physical office space, employees in Quebec, inventory and warehousing arrangements in Canada, substantive board meetings, and commercial decision-making documentation. Artificially interposing a Quebec corporation solely to access treaty benefits or to avoid a foreign PE will be challenged by the CRA and Revenu Quebec.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Federal s. 239(1) ITA: fine equal to 50% to 200% of the tax evaded, imprisonment up to 2 years on summary conviction and up to 5 years on indictment. Quebec Taxation Act s. 1049: equivalent fines and imprisonment; double restitution and forfeiture of assets derived from tax evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorize or participate in tax evasion may be criminally prosecuted, receive travel bans under court order, be subjected to asset freezes, and be held personally liable for the corporation's unpaid tax, interest, and penalties through civil restitution.
Shell Company Abuse

Statutory sanction: The CRA will apply the GAAR to disregard the tax benefit, reassess the full amount of Canadian tax with interest, impose a transfer-pricing penalty of 10% of the net adjustment (minimum CAD 5,000), and add a gross-negligence penalty under s. 163(2) equal to 50% of the tax avoided.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil is pierced under Civil Code of Quebec art. 317 if the company is a fraud instrument; directors are personally liable for reassessed taxes, penalties, and interest and may be subject to a court-ordered prohibition on leaving Canada and a seizure of personal assets.
Transfer Pricing Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: Form T106 late-filing penalty of CAD 25 per day up to CAD 2,500 per information return; transfer-pricing penalty under s. 247(3) equal to 10% of the net adjustment where the taxpayer cannot demonstrate that the results of the transaction are arm's length, with a minimum penalty of CAD 5,000; interest is compounded daily on all reassessed amounts.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for unremitted withholding taxes and source deductions if the TP adjustment implicates Part XIII; in cases of gross negligence, directors may face criminal charges under the ITA and civil claims in the winding-up or bankruptcy of the corporation.
AML and FINTRAC Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, an administrative monetary penalty of up to CAD 2,000,000 for a corporation per violation; criminal penalties of up to CAD 500,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years for laundering proceeds; court-ordered forfeiture of the subject goods and bank accounts.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly direct or tolerate AML violations are subject to personal administrative penalties, criminal prosecution, travel bans under the Criminal Code, and civil restitution orders; the Quebec regulatory authorities may also suspend the corporation's business license and require the dissolution of the company.
