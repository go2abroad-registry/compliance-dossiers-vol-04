Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

A Canadian business corporation is generally taxed as a separate legal person; Canada has no general U.S.-style check-the-box election to treat a corporation as a flow-through entity. A corporation may qualify as a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) or another statutory corporation category based on ownership and control facts, but this is a status determination rather than an elective classification. A corporation may elect certain specific tax treatments, including eligible capital transactions, functional-currency reporting where permitted, amalgamation or winding-up treatments, and GST/HST or QST registration choices where statutory conditions are met. Partnerships and trusts have separate statutory regimes. Canada has anti-hybrid mismatch rules that can deny deductions, impose inclusions, or otherwise neutralize deduction/non-inclusion and double-deduction outcomes; these are not entity-classification elections.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

GST/HST: CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, excluding specified supplies, measured under the small-supplier rules over a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters. QST: generally CAD 30,000 of taxable supplies in the applicable small-supplier calculation period. The thresholds and registration analysis can differ for non-residents, specified financial institutions, registrants, voluntary registrants, and particular supply types. The headline Quebec rate is generally 5.000% GST plus 9.975% QST, producing a combined rate of 14.975% on taxable Quebec supplies.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods supplied in Canada and exported from Canada may generally be zero-rated for GST/HST if the statutory export conditions are met, including shipment by the supplier or the recipient to a destination outside Canada, or delivery to a recipient who exports the goods within the prescribed conditions. The supplier must retain objective evidence linking the sale to the export, such as commercial invoices, shipping records, carrier confirmations, customs records, and proof of delivery outside Canada. Goods merely delivered to a Canadian freight forwarder, warehouse, or intermediary are not automatically zero-rated. Services may be zero-rated only under specific place-of-supply and non-resident-recipient rules; services connected with goods or performed in Canada require separate analysis. QST zero-rating or exemption must be tested independently under Quebec legislation. Zero-rating does not eliminate import taxes in the destination country.

  • Commercial invoice identifying the supplier, purchaser, goods, value, currency, delivery terms, and destination
  • Canadian export declaration or equivalent CBSA export-reporting record, where required
  • Bill of lading, air waybill, courier record, or other carrier transportation document
  • Packing list and proof of delivery or receipt outside Canada
  • Customs entry, import declaration, or customs clearance evidence in the destination jurisdiction where available
  • Purchase order, sales contract, and payment records linking the transaction to the exported goods
  • Evidence of export timing and destination sufficient to support GST/HST and QST treatment
  • Inward remittance evidence is not a universal Canadian GST/HST or QST zero-rating requirement, but bank, payment-platform, and foreign-exchange records should be retained where relevant to substantiate the transaction and settlement

Canada and Quebec do not generally impose a universal real-time e-invoice clearance or government digital-stamping mandate for ordinary wholesale sales. Registrants must issue invoices containing prescribed GST/HST and QST information, retain books and records in an acceptable electronic or paper form, and support input-tax-credit and input-tax-refund claims. Electronic records must be reliable, accessible, readable, and retained for the statutory period. Certain government procurement arrangements, customs filings, payroll filings, and sector-specific programs may require electronic transmission, but ordinary B2B optical-frame wholesale invoices are not generally subject to real-time fiscal clearance. Import and export customs declarations are submitted through CBSA systems, including the applicable CARM and Canadian Export Reporting System processes.

Canada has no general statutory requirement that export proceeds be repatriated within 180 days. Banking, sanctions, anti-money-laundering, customs, transfer-pricing, financing, or contractual requirements may impose separate controls. If zero-rating is unsupported or incorrectly claimed, CRA or Revenu Quebec may reassess the supplier for the GST/HST or QST that should have been collected, deny related input tax credits or input tax refunds, assess arrears interest, and apply applicable penalties. False statements or omissions can trigger gross-negligence or other administrative penalties, and deliberate tax evasion may result in prosecution, fines, and imprisonment under applicable legislation. Refunds obtained through false documentation may be clawed back with interest and penalties. Directors may face derivative liability in specified circumstances, including certain unremitted GST/HST or QST amounts, subject to statutory defenses and limitation periods.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is fully convertible: Canada maintains no restrictions on current-account and capital-account transactions under IMF Article VIII. The Income Tax Act and Quebec business corporations legislation do not impose exchange restrictions on dividends, interest, royalties, branch profits, or capital repayments payable to non-residents. Such outbound distributions are instead subject to Part XIII non-resident withholding tax, at a statutory rate of 25% on dividends, generally reduced to 15% on portfolio dividends and to 10% on corporate-shareholder dividends where the recipient holds at least 10% of the payer's capital under tax treaties such as the Canada–China Income Tax Agreement; treaty caps for interest and royalties are typically 10%. Profit remittance hurdles are therefore tax and compliance matters, not convertibility matters: dividends must satisfy the corporate-law solvency test; CRS and FATCA reporting obligations apply to the Canadian payer and its financial institution; and the Investment Canada Act requires notification for acquisitions of control, although a wholesale distribution entity below the WTO-investor review threshold faces no prior approval veto.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory surrender requirement, no forced-conversion ratio, and no repatriation timeline on export proceeds. A Quebec CA_CORP exporter may retain USD or EUR proceeds indefinitely in a foreign-currency account at a Canadian Schedule I bank, apply them directly to trade payables, or convert them to CAD without a central-bank permit. No mandatory conversion ratio applies and no holding period exists. Export sales of goods shipped outside Canada are zero-rated for GST/HST and QST, provided documentary evidence of export is retained. Indirect constraints are limited to transfer-pricing documentation under section 247 of the Income Tax Act, the Canadian foreign accrual property income rules if proceeds were to be parked in a controlled foreign affiliate, and FINTRAC reporting thresholds for large wire receipts.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada). Canada maintains no centralized foreign-exchange control administration; the Bank of Canada performs the central monetary function and acts as the federal fiscal agent, while foreign-exchange policy coordination sits with the Department of Finance Canada and prudential supervision of banking counterparties is vested in the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI). AML/CTF surveillance of bank and money-services flows is administered by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Moderate. Offshore digital EMIs and fintech multi-currency wallet providers are legally usable in Canada as payment-service providers rather than as deposit-taking banks. A foreign EMI must be registered with FINTRAC as a money-services business or operate through a Canadian-registered payment partner; in Quebec, money-services businesses generally require authorization from the Autorité des marchés financiers (AMF) under the Money-Services Businesses Act. The Retail Payment Activities Act, administered by the Bank of Canada, also requires payment service providers that are not banks to register or be subject to Bank of Canada oversight where applicable. Legal constraints are material: EMIs are not members of Payments Canada, are not authorized deposit-taking institutions, are not CDIC-insured, and funds held with an EMI are unsecured contractual claims rather than bank deposits. Feasibility rating: High viability as an operational clearing rail for supplier disbursements and platform-pay collections, but Low viability as the sole treasury depository or as the receiving vehicle for inbound shareholder capital.
