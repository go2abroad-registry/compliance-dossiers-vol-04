Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no check-the-box or pass-through election for a Business Corporation. A corporation incorporated in Canada is necessarily a resident Canadian taxpayer subject to entity-level tax under the Income Tax Act and Quebec Taxation Act. Available elections are transactional rather than classification-based: section 85 rollover elections for qualifying transfers of property to a corporation; GST/HST section 156 election to have supplies between closely related registrants treated as made for nil consideration; corresponding QST election before Revenu Québec; and optional GST/HST and QST streamlined or Quick Method accounting elections where eligibility criteria are met. Canada's hybrid mismatch rules operate mechanically and do not provide a protective elective reclassification mechanism for Canadian corporations.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

C$30,000. Registration for GST/HST and QST becomes mandatory when the corporation's taxable supplies to Canadian recipients exceed C$30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over the trailing four-quarter period. For non-resident digital-economy suppliers, the same C$30,000 Canadian-recipient threshold triggers the simplified GST/HST registration and mandatory QST registration from the date the threshold is exceeded.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A Canadian corporation supplying royalty-free stock music digital licenses to non-residents may zero-rate the supply under GST/HST and correspondingly exempt it from QST where the recipient is a non-resident and the supply consists of intangible personal property or a digital service that is not related to Canadian real property or tangible personal property situated in Canada. The recipient must not be an individual ordinarily resident in Canada at the time of supply, and the transaction must be structured as an export of intellectual-property rights or digital content. Where the Canadian corporation also supplies the same digital product to Canadian-resident recipients, GST/HST and QST must be charged. Platform-based sales should be reviewed to determine whether a marketplace is deemed to make the supply, but direct B2B/B2C digital export sales generally qualify for zero-rating when the non-resident status is documented.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A/CAD-MR) where physical media is shipped outside Canada
  • Bill of Lading or waybill evidencing physical export from Canada, if applicable
  • Inward remittance certificate or bank foreign-exchange receipt showing payment in convertible foreign currency
  • Signed license agreement evidencing the licensed digital catalogue, territorial scope, and non-resident counterparty
  • Non-resident status evidence such as passport, foreign tax residence certificate, registered office certificate, or KYC records from payment processors (Stripe, PayPal)
  • Commercial invoice clearly marked GST/HST zero-rated with supplier's BN and GST/HST/QST registration numbers
  • Payment reconciliation statements from Stripe or PayPal linking each licensed transaction to the foreign recipient
  • Books and records retention plan demonstrating Canadian VAT audit trail available to CRA and Revenu Québec on request

Canada and Quebec do not impose mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction controls, governmental digital stamping, or invoice pre-approval. Electronic invoicing is permitted, including EDI, XML, PDF, and Peppol BIS 3.0 formats. Registrants must issue invoices in accordance with the GST/HST and QST rules, showing the supplier's registration number, invoice date, supplier identity, recipient identity, description, tax rate, and tax amount. Records must be retained in readable format, ordinarily in Canada or made available electronically to CRA and Revenu Québec upon audit.

Zero-rating claims are subject to rigorous audit. If CRA or Revenu Québec determines the evidence is insufficient or the recipient was in fact a Canadian resident, the supply is reassessed at 5% GST and 9.975% QST, and the previously claimed input tax credit or export refund is clawed back with interest. Misrepresentation penalties include under the Excise Tax Act the greater of C$5,000 and 50% of the net tax attributable to the false statement, plus daily interest; Quebec imposes analogous penalties under the Tax Administration Act. Fraudulent conduct or wilful evasion may lead to criminal prosecution, fines of up to 200% of the evaded tax, and imprisonment. In cases of misrepresentation on a filing, the normal reassessment limitation period remains open, permitting retroactive reassessments without time limit.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is a fully convertible fiat currency under a floating exchange rate regime; there are no exchange control restrictions on current account transactions or capital account transactions. Profit remittance, dividend distribution, and capital repatriation by non-resident shareholders are freely permitted, subject to compliance with the Income Tax Act (Canada) Part XIII withholding tax obligations (typically 25% statutory rate, potentially reduced under an applicable tax treaty such as the Canada-United States Tax Convention) and Quebec provincial reporting requirements under the Taxation Act (Quebec). No regulatory approval is required for conversion of CAD to foreign currency by a licensed financial institution.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory requirement exists in Canada for exporters to surrender export proceeds to the central bank or to convert foreign currency receipts into Canadian dollars within a prescribed timeline. Exporters may maintain foreign currency accounts with Canadian authorized financial institutions, and settlement of export invoices under ISIC 5920 digital audio licensing may be received in USD, EUR, or other major currencies without mandatory conversion. Reporting obligations are limited to anti-money laundering (Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act) large cash transaction and virtual currency reporting thresholds, and to Canada Border Services Agency trade data reporting only for physical goods; digital services royalties are not subject to customs surrender rules.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); exchange administration oversight exercised by the Minister of Finance pursuant to the Currency Act and the Financial Administration Act

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium-High for payment facilitation, Low-Medium for primary treasury custody. Legal constraints: foreign electronic money institutions (EMIs) and payment service providers operating in Canada without a domestic license face restrictions under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA), administered by the Bank of Canada, which requires payment service providers to register if providing payment services to Canadians in Canada. A foreign EMI may operate on a cross-border basis for outward disbursements but cannot lawfully hold client funds for settlement in Canada without compliance with RPAA registration and, where deposit-like activity is conducted, the Bank Act (Canada) restrictions on unauthorized deposit-taking. Inbound subscription collections via Stripe or PayPal remain permissible as commercial payment processors, but treasury concentration in an offshore EMI is disfavored for escrow purposes; funds should flow to a tier-1 Canadian chartered bank operational account to preserve insolvency protection and regulatory clarity.

Defense evidence: Documented commercial rationale for any counterparty in FATF-identified high-risk jurisdictions; enhanced due diligence questionnaires; sanctions screening results (Canadian Consolidated Autonomous Sanctions List, OFAC, UNSC); source of wealth evidence for UBOs; and remediation controls including transaction monitoring thresholds.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant wire transfer protocols for non-resident shareholder capital injection into the Canadian operational or escrow account: (i) use of SWIFT MT103 with purpose code 'EQUITY CAPITAL INJECTION' or the remitter's bank narrative referencing 'Purchase of Capital Stock and Paid-in Surplus'; (ii) transfers from a corporate bank account in the shareholder's name, not from a personal account of an unrelated third party; (iii) completion of the Bank's 'Statement of Source of Funds and Source of Wealth' documentation; (iv) notice under subsection 116(1) or applicable reporting for the Canada Revenue Agency where a tax liability on disposition may arise (not typically applicable for equity injection, but capital contribution documentation required); (v) Quebec corporate registry capitalization disclosure must be updated if authorized share capital is amended; and (vi) no exchange control permit is required, but the wire must be denominated in CAD or freely converted at the receiving bank's foreign exchange rate. For IP royalty assignments, a valuation report and transfer pricing documentation should accompany the capital contribution to prevent CRA recharacterization under section 15 or subsection 214(3) of the Income Tax Act.Clearing rails for trade settlements: (i) SWIFT MT103 for direct supplier/royalty payee disbursements, with remittance details coded as 'LICENSE ROYALTY' or 'SOFTWARE SUBSCRIPTION FEE' and beneficiary account in the payee's jurisdiction; (ii) Commercial Letter of Credit (confirmed at sight) for physical goods or high-value licensing infrastructure acquisitions involving non-resident beneficiaries, issued by a Canadian chartered bank through SWIFT MT700 series; (iii) Canadian Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) for same-day domestic CAD clearing of operational payments to Canadian counterparties; (iv) Real-Time Rail (RTR) expected production for low-value instant payments; (v) Automated Clearing Settlement System (ACSS) for batch payroll and vendor payments within Canada; and (vi) cross-border USD clearing via the Clearing House Interbank Payments System (CHIPS) or Federal Reserve Fedwire using correspondent banking arrangements through the Canadian bank's US branch or a US correspondent. For subscription payout to non-resident licensors, dual payment rails are recommended: PayPal for micro-royalties under USD 100 and SWIFT MT103 for monthly bulk royalty aggregation above the cost-efficient threshold.Deliverable FX forward contracts (CAD/USD and CAD/EUR) with a Schedule I Canadian chartered bank under an International Swaps and Derivatives Association (ISDA) or Canadian Master Agreement, for hedging forecast royalty receivables and subscription revenue up to 12-24 months forwardCross-currency swaps (CAD/USD) for long-term balance sheet hedging of foreign-currency capital injections or acquisition liabilitiesFX options (vanilla calls and puts) purchased from a Canadian authorized derivatives dealer for protection against adverse CAD appreciation on USD-denominated receivablesNon-deliverable forwards (NDFs) referencing CAD/USD or CAD/EUR, permissible for counterparties in jurisdictions with restricted currency convertibility, though not necessary for CAD given the fully convertible regimeCurrency swap agreements with clearing through the Canadian Derivatives Clearing Corporation (CDCC) or bilateral ISDA-collateralized arrangements for institutional-grade mitigation

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium-High for payment facilitation, Low-Medium for primary treasury custody. Legal constraints: foreign electronic money institutions (EMIs) and payment service providers operating in Canada without a domestic license face restrictions under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA), administered by the Bank of Canada, which requires payment service providers to register if providing payment services to Canadians in Canada. A foreign EMI may operate on a cross-border basis for outward disbursements but cannot lawfully hold client funds for settlement in Canada without compliance with RPAA registration and, where deposit-like activity is conducted, the Bank Act (Canada) restrictions on unauthorized deposit-taking. Inbound subscription collections via Stripe or PayPal remain permissible as commercial payment processors, but treasury concentration in an offshore EMI is disfavored for escrow purposes; funds should flow to a tier-1 Canadian chartered bank operational account to preserve insolvency protection and regulatory clarity.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined Canadian federal and Quebec headline rate (federal 15% after abatement and general rate reduction; Quebec general rate 11.5%)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% federal Part XIII withholding tax on gross dividends paid to a non-resident shareholder. Where the payer is resident in Quebec, Quebec's parallel non-resident withholding regime may impose a further gross-basis tax of 15% before treaty limitations; comprehensive DTAs apply to the combined statutory source-country tax.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

Tax return due within six months after the corporation's fiscal period-end. Any balance of federal Part I tax is due within two months after the fiscal period-end for a non-CCPC, or within three months for a CCPC using the small business deduction.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Return due within six months after the fiscal year-end. The Quebec tax balance is generally due within two months after year-end for a large corporation and within three months for a small or CCPC-equivalent corporation; instalments may be required annually.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000: Form T106 is mandatory when reportable non-arm's-length transactions with a non-resident exceed CAD 1 million in a taxation year; CRA's administrative contemporaneous-documentation standard and a Local File are expected at, or below, that threshold, while a Master File is expected for a multinational group using centralized IP, funding, or service models.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not impose standalone Economic Substance Regulations as in offshore financial centres, but the GAAR (subsection 245(2) ITA), Quebec's anti-avoidance provisions, and s.247 transfer-pricing rules require genuine commercial purpose, real personnel, actual decision-making, and substantive contracts. A Quebec royalty-free stock-music entity cannot be a pure conduit; its staff, servers, marketing activities, and intellectual-property ownership must have economic substance or treaty and loss benefits may be denied.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Transfer-Pricing Non-Compliance / Base Erosion

Statutory sanction: Reassessment to an arm's-length result under s.247(2); gross-negligence penalty of 10% of the net adjustment where the adjustment exceeds the lesser of CAD 5 million or 10% of gross revenue; arrears interest; denial of deduction of associated fines.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable under s.160 for transfers made to non-arm's-length persons to defeat tax; where false documents are signed, directors risk tax evasion prosecution, imprisonment, personal criminal fines, and travel restrictions.
