E-government filing portal

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not maintain a US-style check-the-box election. A Quebec limited partnership is statutorily pass-through for income tax (both federal and Quebec) provided it is not a corporation, trust, or listed entity. Relevant elections include: (i) partnership fiscal-period election under subsection 249.1(5) of the Income Tax Act and equivalent Quebec provision, allowing a non-calendar tax year; (ii) section 97(2) rollover election on property contributed to the partnership by a partner; (iii) GST/HST election under section 247 of the Excise Tax Act to register on a retroactive date or to treat an earlier supply as made in the ordinary course of business; (iv) QST equivalent election instruments under the Quebec Act respecting the Québec sales tax; and (v) for hybrid mismatch purposes, Canada's anti-hybrid rules (sections 18.15, 18.16, and 212.3 et seq.) apply to disallow deductions or reverse withholding exceptions, and no elective waiver is available, so the LP must document each partner's tax classification in its home jurisdiction.

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating eligibility for GST/HST and QST on an NVOCC/outbound ocean freight-forwarding operation includes: (a) supply of goods exported by the supplier, delivered by the supplier to a common carrier for export, or delivered to a consignee located outside Canada (Excise Tax Act s. 22 and Schedule VI Part I); (b) supply of a service provided to a non-resident person where the service is not provided in connection with real property in Canada, is not provided to an individual present in Canada, and is not in respect of tangible personal property located in Canada (ETA s. 25, Schedule VI Part V); and (c) supplies of services of arranging for or providing international freight transportation of goods to or from Canada, including ocean containerized cargo under NVOCC bills of lading, which are zero-rated under Schedule VI Parts V and IX. The zero-rating also applies to QST through Quebec's parallel provisions, provided the service is supplied in connection with goods moving outside Canada. The supplier must maintain contemporaneous evidence establishing that the goods were actually exported and that the service was supplied to a non-resident client.

  • Customs Export Declaration (via CBSA eManifest or Canadian Export Reporting System), including Container Number / SPN
  • Ocean Bill of Lading or Sea Waybill (master and house if NVOCC) showing Quebec/Canadian port of loading and foreign port of discharge
  • Commercial invoice and the freight forwarding service contract / NVOCC booking confirmation
  • Banker's inward remittance evidence (MT103/telegraphic transfer receipt) or SWIFT advice showing receipt of export consideration from the non-resident
  • CBSA export manifest / prior notice number and, if applicable, proof of marine cargo insurer's risk report

Canada does not impose a mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control, or government e-invoice stamping regime. CRA and Revenu Québec accept traditional paper and electronic invoices. Electronic invoices must contain the standard GST/HST/QST disclosure (vendor's BN/QST number, date of supply, consideration, tax charged) and must be retained in an accessible, legible format for six years. GST/HST returns must be filed through CRA's NETFILE/GST Express service; QST returns through Revenu Québec's online filing system. There is no mandatory API interconnection or pre-clearance of invoices, but the CRA operates an e-invoice interoperability pilot using PEPPOL standards for voluntary business-to-government e-procurement.

If an exporter claims zero-rating without full export documentation, CRA and Revenu Québec may reassess the supplies at 5% GST and 9.975% QST (combined 14.975%), deny corresponding input tax credits, and charge arrears interest on the net tax. Additional penalties include: (a) GST/HST gross negligence penalty under s. 285 of the Excise Tax Act, generally 25–50% of the net tax understated; (b) Quebec administrative penalty under the QST Act mirroring the federal regime; (c) Income Tax Act s. 163(2) gross negligence penalty of 50% of misreported income on related partner allocations; (d) monetary penalties under the Customs Act for false declarations in export accounting (up to CAD $25,000 per contravention); and (e) if the misrepresentation is wilful, criminal prosecution for tax evasion under s. 239 of the Income Tax Act and s. 327 of the Excise Tax Act, potentially leading to imprisonment, fines, and disgorgement of refunds. The 180-day repatriation benchmark is retained as an administrative due-diligence indicator: where export proceeds are not repatriated within 180 days, the CRA may treat the foreign receivable as never created and withdraw zero-rating, irrespective of the absence of a formal Fx-repatriation statute, and the partnership must therefore document the payment within that window.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full de jure and de facto convertibility for both current and capital account items, with no exchange controls administered by the Bank of Canada or the Quebec government. Capital injections, operational transfers, profit distributions, and partner capital withdrawals are freely remittable in CAD or any convertible foreign currency without prior government approval. Profit remittance hurdles are tax-driven rather than FX-driven: (i) distributions by the Quebec société en commandite (Limited Partnership) to non-resident limited partners may be subject to Canadian withholding tax under Part XIII and to Part XII.2 tax on partnership distributions to non-resident members unless treaty relief is established; (ii) the partnership must file T5013 information returns and the non-resident partner must obtain treaty clearance (e.g., CRA Form NR301 or equivalent competent authority certificate) before reliance on exemption; (iii) applicable UN/OSFI sanctions screening may delay or block specific wire destinations, but no general repatriation moratorium exists.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes NO export proceeds surrender obligation, NO mandatory conversion ratio, and NO statutory timeline for converting NVOCC ocean freight or freight-forwarding export receipts. Export earnings may be retained in CAD, USD, or any other convertible currency in domestic or foreign accounts at the commercial discretion of the partnership. The only compliance duties are record-keeping and reporting: (i) every incoming/outgoing electronic funds transfer of CAD 10,000 or more must be documented under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) and FINTRAC's EFT reporting rules; (ii) shipping and cargo documents (Bills of Lading, house bills, export declarations, cargo manifests) must substantiate the commercial basis of each receipt; (iii) U.S.-related export cargo movements may trigger U.S. Census AES filing requirements, which are operational, not currency-control, obligations.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as the central monetary authority; Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) as the prudential regulator licensing and supervising Schedule I and II banks; Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) as the AML/ATF and cross-border electronic funds transfer reporting regulator; for Quebec, the Autorité des marchés financiers (AMF) registers money services businesses and financial entities operating in the province.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: MEDIUM-LOW for primary treasury concentration. Canada does not recognize the European Electronic Money Institution (EMI) passport, and a UK FCA-authorised or EU-licensed EMI cannot operate as a bank in Canada. A fintech cross-border provider (e.g., Wise Business, Stripe Treasury, or a FINTRAC-registered Money Services Business) may be engaged only as a supplementary disbursement corridor, provided the provider is registered as an MSB under the PCMLTFA and, if carrying on business in Quebec, holds any applicable AMF licence. The Quebec LP must maintain its primary operating, escrow, and clearing accounts with a Canadian Schedule I bank, because offshore EMIs cannot issue Canadian settlement accounts, cannot access LVTS/ACH rails directly, and would create additional non-resident counterparty risk for the NVOCC's carrier payment obligations.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant protocol: inbound capital contributions must be received via SWIFT MT103 (or Fedwire/CHIPS for USD) using the beneficiary bank's published Standard Settlement Instructions. The remittance field 70 must include the required purpose string: 'CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION - CA_QC_LP - REQ REGISTRATION [number] - ISIC 5229 NVOCC'. No Bank of Canada approval or surrender of foreign currency is required, but each incoming EFT of CAD 10,000 or more must be logged and reportable to FINTRAC; the bank will perform enhanced due diligence and match the remittance narrative against the pre-loaded partnership agreement and source-of-funds dossier. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility Rating: MEDIUM-LOW for primary treasury concentration. Canada does not recognize the European Electronic Money Institution (EMI) passport, and a UK FCA-authorised or EU-licensed EMI cannot operate as a bank in Canada. A fintech cross-border provider (e.g., Wise Business, Stripe Treasury, or a FINTRAC-registered Money Services Business) may be engaged only as a supplementary disbursement corridor, provided the provider is registered as an MSB under the PCMLTFA and, if carrying on business in Quebec, holds any applicable AMF licence. The Quebec LP must maintain its primary operating, escrow, and clearing accounts with a Canadian Schedule I bank, because offshore EMIs cannot issue Canadian settlement accounts, cannot access LVTS/ACH rails directly, and would create additional non-resident counterparty risk for the NVOCC's carrier payment obligations.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

0% at the Quebec Limited Partnership ('LP') level: the LP is fiscally transparent and is not a corporation. Partners are taxed on allocated income; an individual partner's top combined federal–Quebec marginal rate is approximately 53.31%, and a corporate partner's combined general rate is 26.5% (federal general corporate rate 15% + Quebec general rate 11.5%).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act for dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident. The LP itself does not pay dividends; distributions to non-resident partners are partnership distributions. A non-resident partner's share of Canadian-source partnership business income is generally subject to Part I tax on a net basis if the partnership carries on business in Canada, and to Part XIII withholding at 25% on amounts that are paid or credited to the non-resident partner as income allocations where no Part I election applies. Return of contributed capital is not subject to withholding.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1 million in aggregate value of specified non-arm's length transactions with each non-resident in the year triggers mandatory CRA Form T106 (Information Return for Non-Arm's Length Transactions with Non-Residents). Contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation (OECD master file/local file) must be prepared for any controlled transaction and is required, at a minimum, for large groups with consolidated revenue meeting the country-by-country reporting threshold of EUR 750 million or its Canadian-dollar equivalent as adjusted by CRA; the CRA can require documentation for lower-threshold transactions if non-arm's length pricing risk is identified.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no standalone 'Economic Substance Regulations' code, but the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR, paragraph 245(2) ITA), the foreign accrual property income rules, and the 'central mind and management' / permanent establishment tests impose a de facto substance requirement. An LP claiming treaty benefits or charging intercompany fees to a related offshore booking company must show real operational substance in Quebec: physical office, employees responsible for freight sales and operations, contractual risk assumption, and commercial decision-making. Quebec's Registraire des entreprises also requires a real registered office and discloses partners/directors; a passive 'mailbox' LP with no staff is treated as a shell and attracts CRA recharacterization, GAAR penalties and possible denial of DTA benefits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion / false reporting (federal and Quebec)

Statutory sanction: Gross negligence penalty equal to 50% of the understated federal tax; Quebec civil penalty up to 50% of the evaded provincial tax; criminal prosecution under subsection 239(1) of the Income Tax Act and Quebec Tax Administration Act carrying fines of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Partners and directors who authorized false filings are jointly and severally liable; corporate veil does not protect a general partner; travel bans, freezing orders, and orders of civil restitution can be issued, and convicted individuals may receive imprisonment, director disqualification, and seizure of personal Canadian assets.
Shell company / treaty shopping / GAAR

Statutory sanction: CRA reassessment disregarding the LP for tax purposes, applying the GAAR, reallocating income to the beneficial owner, denying treaty relief, and imposing GAAR penalties (introduced for transactions after 2023 with an additional punitive amount) plus interest; a misused LP can also be struck from the Quebec registry for non-respect of the Companies Act.

Director personal liability: The general partner ('commandite') has unlimited liability; limited partners who engage in management or who misuse the LP as a sham can be personally liable. Relevant persons can be pursued for civil restitution, penal travel bans, and prohibition orders.
Transfer-pricing mispricing and T106 non-filing

Statutory sanction: CRA adjusts taxable income under section 247 ITA and applies a transfer-pricing penalty of 10% of the net adjustment, plus interest; failure to file T106 can incur a separate penalty of CAD 100 per day up to CAD 2,500 for each failure; Canada also applies treaty-related secondary adjustments in eligible cases.

Director personal liability: Directors can be liable for the LP's unremitted withholding and for unpaid taxes if they signed or directed false transfer-pricing documents; CRA can assess director liability under section 227.1 and impose contempt orders for non-compliance with document production orders.
Part XIII withholding failure on non-resident partners / cross-border payments

Statutory sanction: The LP is liable for the full amount of unwithheld Part XIII tax, plus penalties under section 227(9) of 10% plus interest; CRA can assess the tax even if the non-resident recipient has failed to file a Canadian return; refund of excess withholding is only available to the non-resident, not to the LP.

Director personal liability: Directors and general partners are jointly and severally liable for unremitted withholding taxes; CRA may issue named assessments, garnishment on Canadian bank accounts, and the Cour du Quebec can impose travel bans and imprisonment for wilful non-remittance.
AML / unlicensed NVOCC / financial intermediary misuse

Statutory sanction: Regulatory fines under the Canada Shipping Act and provincial consumer protection laws; FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 500,000 for individuals and CAD 2,000,000 for entities; QST/GST registration and common carrier duties apply; enhanced audit scrutiny by CRA and Revenu Quebec.

Director personal liability: Individuals responsible for laundering proceeds via freight payment rails face Criminal Code fraud/laundering charges punishable by imprisonment up to 14 years; directors can be subject to asset forfeiture, personal fines, and travel restrictions pending extradition/prosecution.
