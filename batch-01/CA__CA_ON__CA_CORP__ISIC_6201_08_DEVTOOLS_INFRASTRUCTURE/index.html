Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (Agence du revenu du Canada), Business Registration Online portal at https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses.html and GST/HST account registration via My Business Account or paper RC1 form.

E-government filing portal

Incorporate federally or provincially, obtain a federal business number (BN) through the Canada Revenue Agency; the BN is the common identifier for corporate income tax, GST/HST, payroll, and import/export accounts. A non-resident corporation carrying on business in Canada or engaged in commercial activity in Ontario must register: (1) obtain a BN via an RC1 or online business registration; (2) open a separate GST/HST account if annual worldwide taxable supplies exceed the $30,000 registration threshold, or if the entity is a non-resident making supplies in Canada, unless zero-rated exports only; (3) where applicable, open a payroll deductions account and/or customs account. Ontario corporate income tax is administered federally as part of the corporate tax return T2, so no separate Ontario tax ID is required, but the entity must file the Ontario Corporate Tax allocation and the Ontario registration is captured through the BN.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a check-the-box regime analogous to the U.S.; corporations are per se CFCs under the Income Tax Act. Available statutory elections include the late-filing election under section 216 for non-resident corporations with rental income from Canadian real estate, the exemption election for a non-resident corporation under section 219 for branch tax purposes, and the partnership or joint venture election mechanisms where applicable. For hybrid mismatch purposes, Canadian tax law applies the OECD hybrid mismatch rules in Part VI.1 of the Income Tax Act, affecting hybrid instruments and hybrid transfers, but no broad entity classification election exists for a business corporation; the default is taxable Canadian corporation status.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies other than financial services over a calendar quarter and the four preceding calendar quarters; non-resident persons carrying on business in Canada may have a lower nexus threshold where they are engaged in a commercial activity in Canada.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods are zero-rated under Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act when the supplier exports the goods and the exporter satisfies the documentary evidence requirements, including proof of export within the time frame prescribed by the CRA. Services provided to non-residents and certain intangible supplies are generally zero-rated if they are not performed in Canada and are not for use in Canada; cloud-based infrastructure services, data storage, and monitoring services supplied to non-residents are zero-rated where the recipient is a non-resident and the service is not delivered to a person in Canada. The supplier must retain evidence that the recipient is a non-resident and the service is an exported service, including contracts, invoices, and payment documentation. GST/HST registration is required to claim input tax credits and to claim zero-rating, but a person making only zero-rated exports may be relieved from registration unless the person voluntarily registers; however, voluntary registration enables recovery of input tax credits on costs related to export activities.

  • Canada Customs Coding Form B3-3 or electronic export declaration through the Canadian International Trade Single Window (if required for controlled or tracked goods)
  • Commercial invoice issued by the Canadian exporter
  • Bill of Lading or air waybill showing Canadian origin and factual export
  • Packing slip and delivery confirmation
  • Proof of payment and inward remittance record showing foreign currency settlement from the non-resident customer
  • Executed contract containing export sale terms and the non-resident status of the purchaser
  • Foreign exchange conversion record supporting the value of the exported supply

There is no national mandatory real-time e-invoicing clearance or digital stamping regime in Canada; however, for GST/HST purposes, registrants must produce invoices in a prescribed format containing mandatory data, retain electronic records in an accessible and readable format, and make them available to the CRA upon demand. Ontario participates in the federal GST/HST system through the HST; no separate provincial sales tax registration is required for the 13% HST rate. Certain cloud-based digital supplies may be subject to the Simplified GST/HST registration regime for non-resident suppliers under sections 240.1 and 242.1, with an optional streamlined registration and return process, but full electronic fiscal clearance and real-time API transmission are not currently required.

The CRA may assess uncollected GST/HST, interest, and penalties where zero-rated treatment is disallowed due to failure to retain adequate proof of export, false statements, or wilful misrepresentation. Penalties under the Excise Tax Act include a minimum of CAD 250 and not more than CAD 25,000 for knowingly making a false or misleading statement; civil penalties can reach 10% of the amount assessed in certain circumstances. Fraudulent avoidance of federal taxes may additionally attract criminal prosecution under the Income Tax Act and Excise Tax Act, including fines up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment. Retrospective clawback periods are generally four years for ordinary assessments and extended to ten years for misrepresentation, negligence, or wilful default, plus compound interest accruing at prescribed rates.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is a freely convertible currency with no legal distinction between capital and current account convertibility for non-resident investors. Profit remittances (dividends, interest, royalties) are unrestricted, subject to Canadian non-resident withholding tax under the Income Tax Act and any relevant treaty relief (e.g., Canada–US Tax Treaty). Capital injections, shareholder loans, and repatriation of invested capital are permitted without prior exchange control approval. Banks are obligated to report certain cross-border transactions over CAD 10,000 for statistical and AML purposes, and FINTRAC requires reporting of large virtual currency and funds transfers, but such reporting does not constitute exchange control.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory surrender, mandatory conversion, or repatriation timeline for export proceeds. Export receipts may be retained indefinitely in foreign currency at a Canadian or foreign financial institution. Exporters are required to maintain proper books and records for tax purposes, and Canadian banks must report cross-border wire transfers exceeding CAD 10,000 to FINTRAC. Statistical submissions may be required under the International Transactions Reporting System on a monthly/quarterly basis, but this is informational only and does not mandate conversion into CAD.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); no dedicated foreign exchange control administration as Canada maintains full liberalisation of capital and current account transactions. The Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) supervises bank operations; Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) enforces AML/CTF obligations; Statistics Canada administers the International Transactions Reporting System for statistical data.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Medium feasibility for offshore digital EMI and fintech treasury rails (e.g., Mercury, Stripe, Wise). A non-resident-owned Ontario corporation may access such platforms only through a partnership with a Canadian licensed financial institution or a registered Money Services Business (MSB) under FINTRAC. An offshore EMI cannot directly settle on Canadian clearing rails without a FINTRAC registration or a Canadian bank sponsorship. Legal constraints include the Bank Act (restrictions on deposit-taking without a Schedule I/II license), the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, and OSFI guidance requiring clear segregation of customer funds. Practical limitation: Canadian Schedule I banks typically require a local Canadian operational bank account for payroll, GST/HST remittance, and provincial tax obligations.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% (15% federal + 11.5% Ontario general corporate rate)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Income Tax Act on taxable dividends paid by a Canadian corporation to non-resident shareholders.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

T2 must be filed within 6 months after the taxation year-end. Corporate income tax instalments are due on the 1st, 3rd, 6th, 9th, and 12th months of the taxation year; balance-due date for non-CCPCs is the 3rd month following year-end, for CCPCs the 2nd month.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 5,000,000 in aggregate annual non-arm's-length transaction value; this triggers mandatory T106 reporting and a practical expectation that contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation, including OECD-aligned Master File and Local File, will be prepared and retained.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have a discrete 'Economic Substance Regulations' regime; however, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (ITA s.245) denies tax benefits from transactions lacking economic substance, and ITA s.247(2) allows CRA to recharacterize artificial or sham intercompany transactions in transfer pricing adjustments.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Statements

Statutory sanction: Civil gross negligence penalty under ITA s.163: 50% of the understated tax; criminal tax evasion under ITA s.239: fine ranging from 50% to 200% of the evaded tax and imprisonment up to 5 years (indictable) or 2 years (summary).

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate may be personally liable for civil fraud restitution; as controlling officers they may be criminally indicted; travel bans and extradition may be triggered by an international arrest warrant.
Transfer Pricing Audit and Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing adjustment plus penalty under ITA s.247(4) of 10% of the gross adjustment if no reasonable attempt to establish arm's length price; documentation failure penalties of $1,000 per month (maximum $95,000) or $24,000 per failure.

Director personal liability: Directors who adopt or approve deficient transfer pricing policies may be personally assessed for withholding tax remittance liabilities under ITA s.227.1; shareholder oppression remedies can impose restitution obligations on directors.
Anti-Money Laundering (PCMLTFA)

Statutory sanction: FINTRAC administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 500,000 for corporations and CAD 100,000 for individuals per violation; criminal offences on indictment: fines up to CAD 2,000,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Directors can face personal FINTRAC penalties for failing to designate a compliance officer or knowingly allowing unregistered services; serious violations may lead to criminal conviction and imprisonment.
Shell Company / Sham Transaction / Veil Piercing

Statutory sanction: GAAR may disallow treaty benefits and assess the true beneficial owner; transfer pricing or sham doctrine empowers CRA to ignore the corporate entity; additional tax plus interest and civil penalties up to 25% of the tax benefit may apply.

Director personal liability: CRA may treat directors as joint tortfeasors if they fraudulently procured the sham; directors may be personally liable for illegal dividends or unremitted corporate taxes; travel bans may be imposed on non-resident directors during criminal investigation or extradition proceedings.
