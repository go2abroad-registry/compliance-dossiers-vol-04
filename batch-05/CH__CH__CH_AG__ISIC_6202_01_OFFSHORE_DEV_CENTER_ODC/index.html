Competent corporate registry

Formation / constitutional filings

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

AG statutory minimum share capital is CHF 100,000; at least 20% of the nominal value of each share, and in aggregate no less than CHF 50,000, must be paid up in cash before registration. The paid-up tranche must be escrowed in a blocked bank account (Sperrkonto / compte bloqué) held at a licensed Swiss bank or securities firm and is released only after the commercial register entry is effected.

Funding & unlock path: Fire safety clearance is provided by the landlord's building certification and verified by the cantonal fire inspection service; structural load inspection is not applicable to office use; occupational workplace clearance is granted under the Swiss Labour Act (Arbeitsgesetz) and SUVA regulations — the office must meet lighting, ventilation, ergonomic, sanitary and emergency-exit standards, and the employer must report all employees to SUVA before the start of work.

Paid-in capital rule: AG statutory minimum share capital is CHF 100,000; at least 20% of the nominal value of each share, and in aggregate no less than CHF 50,000, must be paid up in cash before registration. The paid-up tranche must be escrowed in a blocked bank account (Sperrkonto / compte bloqué) held at a licensed Swiss bank or securities firm and is released only after the commercial register entry is effected.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Switzerland maintains full and unrestricted convertibility on both current and capital accounts. The Swiss franc (CHF) is a freely floating, fully deliverable G10 currency with no exchange-control regime, no central bank approval requirement for cross-border transfers, and no statutory distinction between current and capital transactions. Profit remittance, dividend distribution, loan repayments, and intra-group service fees may be transferred to non-residents without SNB authorization; the only applicable friction is the 35% Swiss withholding tax on dividends (reducible under double taxation treaties via refund or notification procedure), which is a tax matter, not an FX control. The sole administrative obligation for banks is statistical reporting of cross-border payment transactions to the SNB under the Swiss foreign payments statistics (Aussenwirtschaftsstatistik). A Swiss AG may therefore freely convert, retain, and disburse any currency.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Switzerland imposes no export proceeds surrender or mandatory conversion rules. There is no statutory timeline, no minimum conversion ratio, and no requirement to repatriate foreign currency income into CHF. An ODC exporter may hold export and service receipts indefinitely in USD, EUR, GBP, or any other freely chosen currency and convert to CHF only when commercially advantageous. Cross-border receipts above the reporting threshold are captured by the receiving bank for SNB balance-of-payments statistics; no client filing or planning is required.

Central bank supervision: Swiss National Bank (SNB / Schweizerische Nationalbank), Bern/Zurich, with prudential supervision of banks and financial intermediaries exercised by the Swiss Financial Market Supervisory Authority (FINMA / Eidgenössische Finanzmarktaufsicht).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Feasibility rating: MEDIUM as a supplementary collection and disbursement rail, but LOW as the primary treasury platform. Legal constraints: Wise (Wise Europe SA / Wise Payments Ltd) and Payoneer (Payoneer Europe Ltd) hold foreign e-money institution (EMI) licences (Luxembourg CSSF, UK FCA, or equivalent), not a Swiss banking licence under the Swiss Banking Act. They cannot issue native CH-prefixed IBANs, cannot clear through the Swiss Interbank Clearing (SIC) system, and are not covered by the Swiss depositor protection scheme (esisuisse, CHF 100,000 limit) nor by Swiss bank-client secrecy. They are legally usable for USD/EUR inbound receivables from offshore ODC clients and for small vendor payouts, but Swiss payroll (AHV/IV/EO), unemployment insurance (ALV), occupational pension (BVG), direct taxes, VAT (MWST), and social security (SUVA) contributions must be settled through a FINMA-licensed Swiss bank. Mitigation: maintain the licensed Swiss bank account as the master treasury node, use EMIs only as pass-through gateways subject to automatic daily sweeping to the Swiss bank, and ensure the EMI's underlying partner-bank arrangements are compliant with the Swiss Foreign Payments Ordinance.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Capital contribution by the foreign parent is executed via SWIFT MT103 (or SEPA for EUR) to the Swiss AG's capital deposit account, referencing the notarized articles of incorporation and share subscription. The receiving Swiss bank applies the SNB foreign-payments statistics classification for direct investment equity contribution (capital code under 'Direktinvestitionen – Aktienkapital'); the client should instruct the parent to state the purpose as 'Einzahlung Aktienkapital' with the entity name and incorporation registry reference. No prior SNB or FINMA authorization is required. The bank will request: (i) certified copy of the notarized deed of incorporation, (ii) share register, (iii) bank confirmation form (Bestätigung der Bank über die Einzahlung) to evidence the minimum paid-up capital, and (iv) identification of the beneficial owner(s). Inbound capital exceeding CHF 10 million may require statistical disclosure but does not trigger sectoral FDI screening for a software ODC. The minimum paid-in amount must be credited in CHF and must remain blocked/earmarked until the commercial register (Handelsregister) registration is completed.Primary clearing rails: (i) CHF domestic payments via SIC (Swiss Interbank Clearing) operated by SIX, including express CH-IPS for time-critical obligations (payroll, tax, social security); (ii) EUR payments across the EEA via SEPA Credit Transfer using the Swiss-prefixed account IBAN through a SEPA participant bank, with MT103/COVER for non-SEPA EUR destinations; (iii) USD, GBP, and other non-European currencies via SWIFT MT103 through the bank's foreign nostro correspondent network; (iv) Commercial Letters of Credit issued by the relationship bank under ICC UCP600/eUCP for hardware procurement or subcontracting where the ODC applicant requires documentary security — SWIFT MT700 issuance and MT710 amendments; (v) bulk supplier payouts via ISO 20022 pain.001/pain.003 messages to the Swiss bank's host-to-host channel for high-volume ODC vendor disbursement. All outbound payments are freely permitted; banks apply statistical coding and sanctions screening, but no license, permit, or SNB consent is required. For settlements above CHF 100,000 equivalent, the bank may require underlying invoice documentation under FINMA circular AML obligations.Outright deliverable FX forwards in USD/CHF, EUR/CHF, and GBP/CHF with tenors up to 24 months, documented under the Swiss Master Agreement for OTC Derivatives or ISDA 2002 Master Agreement with a credit support annex (CSA) where margin is required.FX swaps and forward-forward structures for rolling settlement of recurring ODC receivables and matched disbursements in different currencies.Non-Deliverable Forwards (NDFs) on soft or restricted currencies (e.g., INR, CNY, IDR, VND, BRL) where the ODC invoices clients in such currencies, cash-settled in CHF against agreed reference rates — necessary because the underlying currency is not freely deliverable in Switzerland.Cross-currency swaps (CCS) under ISDA 2002 for longer-term intercompany shareholder loans or treasury consolidation between CHF and USD/EUR.Vanilla European FX options (call/put) on USD/CHF and EUR/CHF for downside protection on large anticipated receipts, including zero-premium participating forwards offered by Swiss banks.FX rolling spot and tom-next swaps for short-term liquidity management across the multi-currency account.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

8.5% federal statutory rate on taxable profit (effective federal rate approx. 7.83%); combined cantonal/communal/federal effective headline rates generally range from approx. 11.9% to 21.1%, with lower-tax locations such as Zug around 14.6% and Zurich around 19.7%.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

35% Swiss federal withholding tax (Verrechnungssteuer / impôt anticipé) on dividends distributed by a Swiss AG; treaty rates as low as 0%–15% may apply to qualifying non-resident shareholders.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The cantonal tax authority sets the filing deadline. For calendar-year companies the common deadline is 31 March of the following year, with extensions commonly allowed to 30 June or 30 September if represented by a professional adviser. For non-calendar fiscal years, the return is generally due within six months of the financial year-end; federal direct tax follows the cantonal assessment.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Switzerland has no explicit statutory Master File/Local File monetary threshold; however the Swiss tax administrations routinely require transfer pricing documentation for cross-border related-party transactions exceeding approximately CHF 10,000,000 per annum. Country-by-Country Reporting is mandatory for Swiss constituent entities of groups with consolidated annual revenue of at least CHF 900,000,000. An offshore development centre performing routine software engineering services must maintain contemporaneous evidence of costs, headcount, functions, risks and benchmark rates.

Economic substance scope: Switzerland does not apply the Cayman-style Economic Substance Regulations, but economic substance is decisive under Swiss GAAR, withholding tax anti-abuse practice and treaty entitlement rules. A Swiss ODC must demonstrate real office premises, local managerial decision-makers, operational employees, equipment, business risk, bank accounts and actual provision of IT services in Switzerland. A letterbox or cash-box entity without substantive domestic functions is re-characterised as tax transparent, denied treaty benefits, subjected to 35% withholding tax on deemed distributions, and exposed to criminal tax investigations.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Falsification of Corporate Tax Return

Statutory sanction: Administrative fine of 25%–300% of the evaded tax plus default interest (Art. 175 DBG); if false documents or misleading records are used, tax fraud under Art. 186 DBG carries imprisonment of up to three years or a monetary penalty and confiscation of illegal proceeds.

Director personal liability: Directors and de facto officers who sign false returns or cause non-declaration may face criminal conviction, personal fines, disgorgement and imprisonment. Civil liability to the company and creditors arises under Art. 754 CO; in insolvency, directors may be personally liable for the shortfall caused by their fault.
Withholding Tax (Verrechnungssteuer) Misappropriation or Refund Fraud

Statutory sanction: Default interest, criminal prosecution, fines and imprisonment; the company remains liable for the full withholding tax amount and the tax authority may levy enforcement on corporate assets.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly fail to collect or pay withholding tax are personally jointly liable under the Swiss Withholding Tax Act; willful abuse exposes them to criminal sanctions, personal asset seizure, and for non-Swiss directors, entry bans on Swiss territory.
Artificial Shell Company / Treaty Shopping / Missing Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Denial of Swiss beneficial ownership and treaty entitlement; re-characterisation of cash flows as hidden profit distributions; retroactive 35% withholding tax; refusal of tax residence advantages; administrative fines for incomplete reporting.

Director personal liability: Nominee directors can be prosecuted for false certification and aiding tax evasion. Under Swiss veil-piercing doctrine, personal liability may be imposed only for abuse of legal form, commingling of assets or deliberate undercapitalisation; in practice, directors plus foreign beneficial owners face criminal investigations and mutual legal assistance proceedings.
Transfer Pricing / Hidden Profit Distribution

Statutory sanction: Upward adjustment to Swiss taxable income, disallowance of deductions, re-characterisation of excess amounts as constructive dividends subject to 35% withholding tax, plus late interest and evasion penalties of up to 300% of additional tax.

Director personal liability: Directors must ensure arm's length outcomes; culpable participation in artificial pricing schemes may lead to personal criminal liability, restitution claims after insolvency, and debarment from Swiss board positions.
AML / Financial Intermediary and International Enforcement Red Flags

Statutory sanction: Swiss anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorism laws impose administrative fines and criminal penalties; willful money laundering under Art. 305bis StGB carries imprisonment of up to five years and asset confiscation; FINMA may intervene where regulated intermediaries are involved.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly execute suspicious transactions or fail to implement basic KYC controls may be criminally liable, personally fined, subject to confiscation orders and, in serious cases, subjected to pre-trial detention and travel restrictions.
Unpaid Taxes / Enforcement Proceedings / Bankruptcy Circumvention

Statutory sanction: Swiss tax enforcement may issue debt-collection summonses, seize bank and share assets, and ultimately initiate bankruptcy proceedings; directors can be prosecuted for bankruptcy fraud if they conceal assets.

Director personal liability: In insolvency, the board must notify the court and protect employee and tax claims. Directors who violate capital preservation rules are liable under Art. 725 and Art. 754 CO for losses; unpaid federal and cantonal tax debts may result in personal recovery only where directors culpably caused the damage.
