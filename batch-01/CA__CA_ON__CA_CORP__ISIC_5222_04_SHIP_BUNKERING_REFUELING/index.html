Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); official business portal: My Business Account via canada.ca (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/digital-services-businesses.html)

E-government filing portal

For a business corporation incorporated under the Canada Business Corporations Act or the Ontario Business Corporations Act, the statutory tax identifier is the CRA Business Number (BN). The corporation must apply to the CRA using Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number), the 'Register for a BN/GST/HST account' online e-service, or the integrated federal-provincial Business Registration Service; the CRA issues the 9-digit BN with program accounts (e.g., RT for GST/HST, RC for corporate income tax, RM for import/export, RP for payroll). Non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada may obtain a BN and register for GST/HST using Form RC1 or RC405 (Non-Resident GST/HST Registration) and must register where they make taxable supplies in Canada and exceed the CAD 30,000 small-supplier threshold or otherwise satisfy the 'carrying on business in Canada' nexus test; registration must precede the issuance of GST/HST invoices and the claiming of input tax credits.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — a Canadian federal or provincial business corporation is a separate legal taxpayer under Part I of the Income Tax Act (a 'taxable Canadian corporation'), subject to federal corporate income tax and Ontario corporate income tax, and is a separately registered person for GST/HST. There is no United States-style check-the-box entity classification; classification is statutorily fixed by incorporation.

Available election forms

Canada has no check-the-box election. Relevant statutory elections for cross-border corporate groups and hybrid mismatch arrangements include: (i) GST/HST group registration election under section 156 of the Excise Tax Act (Forms GST25 / GST25.1) enabling related corporations to file consolidated GST/HST returns and transfer credits among group members; (ii) section 85 Income Tax Act rollover election for tax-deferred transfers of Canadian property to a corporation; (iii) functional currency reporting election under section 261 of the Income Tax Act; (iv) capital dividend account election under section 83 of the Income Tax Act; and (v) ordering and subordination elections available under Canada's hybrid mismatch rules (e.g., under section 18.4 of the Income Tax Act) to recharacterize or subordinate deduction/non-inclusion outcomes on hybrid financial instruments and hybrid entities.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 — the GST/HST small-supplier threshold under sections 148-149 of the Excise Tax Act. A corporation, including a non-resident carrying on business in Canada, must register once its worldwide taxable supplies (other than zero-rated supplies, such as qualifying exported marine fuel) exceed CAD 30,000 in any single calendar quarter or in a trailing four-calendar-quarter period. Non-residents that make taxable supplies in Canada and are deemed to carry on business in Canada may have mandatory registration obligations irrespective of the threshold, and all registrants are required to collect HST on taxable supplies in Ontario at 13%.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Marine bunker fuel and lubricants are zero-rated under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act where the supplier makes a supply to a non-resident and the goods are exported from Canada, or where the fuel/lubricants are supplied for use by a ship engaged in international commercial carriage of passengers or goods and are delivered for use on a voyage extending outside Canada. The supplier must hold evidence establishing the non-resident status of the recipient, the delivery of the fuel to the vessel or the foreign destination, the vessel's international itinerary, and the exportation of the goods; bunkering intended for purely domestic/coastwise voyages remains taxable HST (13%) in Ontario and may attract additional excise/fuel-tax obligations. Eligible zero-rated exporters may claim input tax credits on inputs attributable to the zero-rated supply and may apply to the CRA for refunds of the GST/HST component.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA Form B13A/B13B export declaration) evidencing the export of the bunker fuel/lubricants from Canada and the foreign destination
  • Bill of Lading (marine/ocean or barge bill of lading) evidencing delivery of the marine fuel to the vessel for the international voyage
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (bank/foreign-exchange record evidencing receipt of export proceeds from the non-resident purchaser and repatriation to Canada)

Canada imposes no mandatory national real-time e-invoicing clearance, digital stamping, or government pre-approval/API validation regime for GST/HST invoices. Tax invoices may be issued in paper or electronic form if they contain the prescribed particulars (subsection 169(4) of the Excise Tax Act and section 3 of the Excise Tax Act Regulations), including the supplier's Business Number and GST/HST registration number, date of invoice, recipient particulars, description of the supply, and the tax charged or rate applied. The Government of Canada promotes PEPPOL-based e-invoicing for public procurement, and the CRA accepts digital records; however, no clearance mandate applies. Separate excise-licence monthly reporting to the CRA is required for licensed fuel suppliers under the Excise Act, 2001 for marine diesel and other fuel categories.

CRA audit programs specifically target fraudulent export zero-rating, including fabricated B13 export declarations, fictitious bills of lading, shell non-resident purchasers, and failure to repatriate export proceeds. Penalties and clawback consequences include: (i) the Excise Tax Act gross-negligence penalty equal to the greater of CAD 100 and 50% of the tax/refund understated or overclaimed; (ii) a minimum CAD 500 false-statement penalty per infraction; (iii) criminal liability — summary conviction fines up to CAD 25,000 and/or two years' imprisonment, or indictment fines up to CAD 100,000 and/or five years' imprisonment — for knowingly making false or deceptive statements; (iv) retroactive reassessment beyond the normal four-year limitation period where misrepresentation, carelessness, neglect, or wilful default is found, with full recovery of zero-rated refunds, input tax credits, unpaid GST/HST, and arrears interest; (v) suspension or revocation of Excise Act, 2001 fuel licences for non-compliance; and (vi) director's joint and several liability for unreported and unremitted GST/HST amounts.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current and capital account convertibility. The Canadian dollar is freely floating and no exchange control regulations restrict the repatriation of dividends, interest, royalties, or capital. Profit remittance is subject only to standard withholding tax compliance under the Income Tax Act (Canada) and, where applicable, an Ontario corporate tax clearance. Foreign-owned Canadian corporations may repatriate funds through normal banking channels without Bank of Canada consent, provided the underlying transactions comply with anti-money laundering (AML) reporting under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA).

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory export proceeds surrender requirement in Canada. Bunkering and marine fuel export receivables may be retained in foreign currency accounts, converted to Canadian dollars at market rates, or held offshore at the exporter's discretion. No mandatory conversion ratio or timeline applies. Reporting obligations are limited to FINTRAC large transaction reports (transactions of CAD 10,000 or more) and, for goods exported, Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) export declarations under the Customs Act.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada / Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for currency transaction reporting; no foreign exchange control authority or central bank approval is required for currency conversion under the Currency Act or the Bank of Canada Act.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Foreign fintechs and electronic money institutions (EMIs) have limited viability for primary treasury operations. Canada requires a bank or federally regulated financial institution charter to offer deposit-taking, clearing, and payment services to Canadian businesses. Digital-only EMIs may operate as payment service providers only if registered as money services businesses (MSBs) with FINTRAC and, where applicable, licensed under the Retail Payment Activities Act. For ship bunkering operations involving maritime liens, standby letters of credit, and large SWIFT wires, tier-1 domestic banks remain the legally robust counterparties; EMIs are rated Low-to-Medium for intermediate clearing and cannot replace bank-issued guarantees.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital for equity injection is routed via SWIFT MT103 or wire transfer to the corporate operating account or designated capital escrow account. The remitter must reference the Canadian corporation legal name and unique Ontario Business Registry number, and the payment narrative must clearly state 'equity subscription' or 'share capital contribution' to permit bank compliance and tax characterization. Supporting verification includes the unanimous shareholder resolution, subscription agreement, share issuance register, and certificate of incorporation. Canada imposes no Barclays-style purpose code system; however, FINTRAC large cash transaction reporting and FINTRAC EDD apply to transfers exceeding CAD 10,000. Banks may require a sworn source-of-funds declaration if the remitter is a non-resident entity.Outbound disbursements for bunker fuel procurement, lubricant replenishment, port fees, and vessel charter hire are executed through SWIFT MT103 for direct transfer, Commercial Letters of Credit (documentary credit) for counterparty payment security, and Standby Letters of Credit as performance guarantees. Settlement in USD is typically cleared through correspondent banks in New York using Fedwire or CHIPS; CAD transactions clear through Payments Canada's Large Value Transfer System (LVTS). Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return must be filed no later than six months after the corporation's fiscal year-end. For a December 31 year-end, the filing deadline is June 30 of the following year. A non-resident corporation with a Canadian permanent establishment is subject to the same six-month rule.

Ontario also imposes a late filing penalty of 5% of the unpaid provincial tax plus 1% per month (max 12%).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada does not prescribe fixed statutory amounts for the Local File and Master File, but the CRA's administrative practice mandates contemporaneous documentation when the aggregate value of controlled transactions between a Canadian taxpayer and a non-resident related party exceeds CAD 1,000,000 per year; Master File requirements generally align with OECD guidance and are expected for multinational groups with consolidated revenues of at least CAD 750,000,000 (Country-by-Country reporting threshold).

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) equivalent to those in OFC jurisdictions, but the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (section 245 of the Income Tax Act) denies tax benefits from transactions that are primarily tax-motivated and lack bona fide economic substance. For marine bunkering operations, the CRA will scrutinize the existence of a real operative office, maritime assets, and operational decision-making in Canada. Additionally, section 250(4) test contemplates entity residency; a foreign-incorporated entity that is managed and controlled from Canada may be considered resident, while a Canadian-incorporated entity with no substantive activity may be viewed as a resident shell subject to GAAR reassessment.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Under subsection 238(1) of the Income Tax Act, the evader is liable to a fine of 50% of the tax sought to be evaded plus an additional penalty up to CAD 10,000; criminal prosecution under subsection 240(1) may result in imprisonment for up to five years. The CRA also imposes civil penalties equal to the greater of CAD 5,000 and 50% of the understated tax.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for the corporation's unpaid tax amounts under section 227.1 if they failed to deduct, withhold, or remit amounts; criminal conviction can trigger travel bans, asset freezes, personal restitution orders, and imprisonment. Willful participation in tax evasion can pierce the corporate veil.
Transfer Pricing Adjustment

Statutory sanction: A 10% transfer pricing penalty under subsection 247(3) is levied on the amount of the net transfer pricing adjustment (increased by any third-party adjustment) where it exceeds the lesser of 10% of gross revenue or CAD 5,000,000. In addition, the CRA may reassess taxable income and apply interest and arrears penalties under subsections 161(1) and 162(2).

Director personal liability: Directors who sign inaccurate returns or direct the manipulation of transfer pricing may be jointly liable for penalties under subsection 227.1 or under the common law duty of care. A finding of intentional double-dipping or collusion may expose directors to criminal liability for tax evasion and to orders for restitution.
AML/Shell Company Violation

Statutory sanction: Under the PCMLTFA, failure to register or implement a compliance program is subject to administrative monetary penalties up to CAD 1,000,000 for a corporation and CAD 500,000 for individuals per violation. The CRA may also counteract sham arrangements under GAAR and levy significant gross-negligence penalties. The Ontario government may dissolve a corporation that has not carried on business for three consecutive years under the Ontario Business Corporations Act, leading to forfeiture of assets.

Director personal liability: Directors of a shell company may be personally liable for debts and statutory penalties if the corporation is used as a vehicle for fraud or money laundering (piercing of the corporate veil). They may be excluded from acting as directors for up to 10 years, subjected to asset forfeiture proceedings, and, if convicted, sentenced to imprisonment and placed on a travel ban list.
