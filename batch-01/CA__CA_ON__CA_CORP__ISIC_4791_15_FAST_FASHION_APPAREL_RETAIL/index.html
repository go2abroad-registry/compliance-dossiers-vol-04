Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada (ARC); electronic portal: CRA My Business Account (MyBA) and Business Registration Online (BRO), integrated with the Province of Ontario's One Business Registration service (Ontario OneStop).

E-government filing portal

A Business Corporation incorporated under the Canada Business Corporations Act or the Ontario Business Corporations Act obtains its statutory tax identifier, the 9-digit Business Number (BN), by filing federally through CRA Business Registration Online or provincially through the Ontario One Business Registration portal. The single BN is then used to open program accounts: RC (income tax), RT (GST/HST), RP (payroll), and Ontario EHT. For a non-resident corporation establishing a Canadian branch, Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts) must be filed with the CRA Non-Resident Income Tax Division, accompanied by certified articles of incorporation from the home jurisdiction, proof of business identity (certificate of incumbency or equivalent), and an executed Power of Attorney naming a Canadian resident agent for service. Registration for GST/HST is completed on the same BN application by selecting the RT account. Online registrations are typically effective immediately; paper applications take 5-10 business days. The GST/HST registration effective date is the earlier of the date of first taxable supply in Canada or the registration application date; the small supplier may elect to register early to reclaim input tax credits.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation (no pass-through); Canadian-resident corporations are taxed on worldwide income under Part I of the Income Tax Act (federal 15% general rate after provincial abatement) and Part III of the Taxation Act, 2007 (Ontario 12% general rate), for a combined headline corporate rate of 27%; the Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) small business rate is combined 12.2% (federal 9% + Ontario 3.2%) on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income. Non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada through a permanent establishment are subject to Part I tax on Canadian-source business income plus a 25% Part XIV branch tax (reduced by treaty). Territorial_Scope does not apply to active business income; foreign affiliate dividends are excluded under the exempt surplus rules. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation (no pass-through); Canadian-resident corporations are taxed on worldwide income under Part I of the Income Tax Act (federal 15% general rate after provincial abatement) and Part III of the Taxation Act, 2007 (Ontario 12% general rate), for a combined headline corporate rate of 27%; the Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) small business rate is combined 12.2% (federal 9% + Ontario 3.2%) on the first CAD 500,000 of active business income. Non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada through a permanent establishment are subject to Part I tax on Canadian-source business income plus a 25% Part XIV branch tax (reduced by treaty). Territorial_Scope does not apply to active business income; foreign affiliate dividends are excluded under the exempt surplus rules. Tax_Exempt_Zone not applicable.

Available election forms

Canada does not offer a US-style check-the-box election. Available statutory elections: (i) Income Tax Act (ITA) s.85 income-tax-free rollover election for property transferred to a Canadian corporation; (ii) ITA s.125(6) associated-company election allocating the small business deduction among associated CCPCs; (iii) Excise Tax Act (ETA) s.156 closely-related corporation election to zero-rate intercompany taxable supplies for GST/HST; (iv) ETA s.150(2) non-resident election permitting a single GST/HST return for multiple Canadian branches; (v) ETA s.211.32 election by a foreign supplier of digital products to use simplified GST/HST registration and remittance; (vi) Ontario Employers' Health Tax (EHT) associated-employer election to allocate the CAD 490,000 exemption among related employers.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000: a corporation (resident or non-resident) becomes a mandatory GST/HST registrant when its worldwide taxable supplies (including those of associated persons) exceed CAD 30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters. For non-resident e-commerce suppliers of retail goods or digital services to Canadian consumers, the threshold is measured on Canadian-source revenue; sales collected by a Canadian fulfillment centre or Logistics Service Provider may create deemed Canadian supply nexus. Voluntary registration is available to non-residents making taxable supplies in Canada to recover input tax credits; registration is mandatory once the threshold is exceeded and carries a failure-to-register penalty of the greater of CAD 500 and 1% of the registrant's net tax remittances.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of tangible personal property (including fast-fashion apparel and footwear) are zero-rated under Section 1 of Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act at 0% GST/HST, permitting the supplier to claim input tax credits on all Canadian input costs. Zero-rating applies where the property is: (i) delivered by the supplier or by a common carrier engaged by the supplier to a destination outside Canada; (ii) processed in Canada and then exported under customs control; or (iii) exported by a purchaser as accompanied baggage provided the supplier obtains verifiable proof of export (Customs B3, airline receipt, boarding pass) within 60 days of delivery. Offshore services relating to tangible personal property exported from Canada are zero-rated under Section 7 of Part V of Schedule VI where the recipient of the service is a non-resident. For e-commerce subsections, zero-rating is denied where goods are sold to a Canadian resident for export by the buyer's own arrangement; in such cases 13% HST must be charged. A valid zero-rating claim requires the supplier to retain documentary evidence of export for 6 years.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA Form B13A / CAED G49 electronic export reporting) bearing the exporter's BN and the valid Export Control List classification
  • Commercial invoice / sales contract stating 0% HST zero-rated supply and INCOTERM
  • Bill of Lading (ocean, air waybill, or ICC CMR truck waybill) showing final foreign destination and carrier details
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank wire/swift confirmation issued by a Canadian Schedule I bank evidencing receipt of foreign currency export proceeds
  • Foreign customs import declaration or delivered-at-destination proof signed by the offshore customer
  • Exporter of Record identification (matched to the CRA BN) and, where applicable, the Canadian carrier's transportation manifest used for income tax GST attribution

Canada does not impose a real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or per-invoice CRA pre-approval regime. The CRA operates the Pan-Canadian Trust Framework and has adopted the Peppol (V1/V2) e-invoicing interoperability standard on a voluntary, opt-in basis. Mandatory electronic filing applies to GST/HST registrants with annual taxable revenue exceeding CAD 10 million and to all non-resident registrants. Returns must be filed through CRA GST/HST NETFILE with the registered BN and program account; payments are due by the last day of the following month for monthly filers or the last day of the following month for quarterly filers (one-month extension for annual filers). Suppliers using marketplace facilitators (e.g., Shopify Marketplace or Klarna BNPL settlement partners) remain jointly and severally liable for amounts not properly collected or remitted; no CRA API pre-clearance exists for invoices issued in the normal course.

CRA may retrospectively deny zero-rating and reassess 13% HST with interest where the exporter cannot substantiate physical export or receipt of payment. Penalties: (i) ETA s.280 failure to register/remit — greater of CAD 500 or 1% of net tax remittances; (ii) ETA s.285.1 false statement or omission — 20% of the tax that would have been evaded or of the misstated net tax; (iii) Income Tax Act s.163(2) gross negligence penalty — 50% of the understated tax attributable to knowingly or grossly negligent conduct; (iv) ETA s.327 criminal fine and imprisonment — fine up to 200% of the tax evaded, imprisonment up to 5 years, or both, for fraudulent export zero-rating schemes; (v); (vi) director's joint and several liability under ETA s.323 for unremitted HST; (vii) suspension of refunds and offsets of export rebates against any tax arrears. The 180-day repatriation period is a commercial and customs norm; failure to repatriate export proceeds within this window may trigger transfer-pricing and foreign-accrual-property-income scrutiny under ITA s.247 and s.91, though no explicit GST/HST statutory deadline exists.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar (CAD) is fully convertible under a free-floating exchange rate regime. The Currency Act and the Bank of Canada Act establish legal tender and monetary policy, but no exchange control legislation restricts capital or current account convertibility. Dividend and interest remittances to non-residents are permitted without prior regulatory approval, subject to Part XIII withholding tax under the Income Tax Act and applicable tax treaties. For e-commerce retail cross-border receipts, conversion between CAD and foreign currency is freely executable through authorized financial institutions. The only de facto hurdles are anti-money laundering documentation thresholds (CAD 10,000 reporting requirements), corporate law solvency tests for dividends, and transfer pricing documentation obligations under section 247 of the Income Tax Act.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory timeline or mandatory conversion ratio for export proceeds. Exporters may retain, convert, or repatriate foreign currency receipts at their discretion through licensed banks. There is no surrender requirement under the Bank Act or any Canadian regulation. For corporate tax computation, the Income Tax Act requires converting foreign currency income into Canadian dollars using the Bank of Canada published rate for the relevant taxation year, but this does not compel actual currency conversion. Treasury policy may nevertheless adopt a voluntary repatriation and conversion strategy to manage FX exposure.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) as monetary authority; Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) as banking regulator; Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for AML/ATF supervision; Canada does not operate a dedicated foreign exchange control authority.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Offshore digital electronic money institutions (EMIs) such as Wise, Revolut, and Payoneer are not licensed as banks under the Bank Act (Canada). They cannot hold Canadian deposit accounts unless they become authorized foreign banks or acquire a Canadian banking subsidiary. The Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) will introduce a federal licensing regime for payment service providers, but as of the current regulatory stage these platforms operate under limited exemptions. Feasibility is rated 'Medium' — they may be used for auxiliary settlement, supplier disbursements, and multicurrency treasury float, but not for capital escrow, core liquidity, or statutory corporate accounts. Legal constraints include FINTRAC registration as a money services business (MSB), the requirement to identify and report large virtual currency transactions, and enhanced due diligence on source of funds from such platforms.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity or loan injections for a Canadian corporation must be transmitted by SWIFT MT103 (single payment) or MT202 (cover payment) to a designated Canadian bank account. The remitter shall use ISO payment purpose codes such as 'P01' (capital investments not subject to monetary authority control), 'C11' (loans), or 'D01' (dividends) in the payment details. A bank will require the underlying transaction documents: board resolutions approving the capital contribution, subscription agreement or shareholder loan agreement, minutes of directors, and a copy of the Certificate of Incorporation. If the investor is non-Canadian, the transaction may trigger a notification under the Investment Canada Act if it exceeds the threshold for review; otherwise, no pre-clearance is required. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined general corporate income tax rate for Ontario: 15% federal and 11.5% Ontario. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access a combined 12.2% small-business rate on eligible active business income up to the applicable CAD 500,000 federal business limit, subject to associated-corporation, capital, passive-income, and eligibility rules.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Canadian withholding tax on taxable dividends paid or credited by a Canadian resident corporation to a non-resident, subject to treaty reduction. Under the Canada-United States tax treaty, the rate is generally 5% where the beneficial owner is a qualifying company owning at least 10% of the voting power, and 15% in other treaty cases. Other treaties commonly reduce the rate to 5%, 10%, or 15%, depending on ownership and treaty conditions.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general monetary threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is unnecessary. Section 247 of the Income Tax Act requires contemporaneous documentation supporting arm's-length terms for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions. Form T106 generally applies where reportable non-arm's-length non-resident transactions exceed the applicable aggregate CAD 1,000,000 threshold or another statutory filing condition is met. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to a Canadian ultimate parent or surrogate parent with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million in the preceding fiscal year, reported in the prescribed Canadian-dollar equivalent. Canada does not impose a universally mandatory standalone Master File threshold comparable to every OECD jurisdiction; CRA may request relevant group and local documentation.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no separate general Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to the United Arab Emirates or certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance is nevertheless relevant through the arm's-length principle, the general anti-avoidance rule, specific anti-avoidance provisions, beneficial-ownership analysis, thin-capitalization rules, foreign-affiliate and foreign-accrual-property-income rules, treaty entitlement, and the principal-purpose or limitation-on-benefits provisions of applicable treaties. CRA may challenge arrangements lacking commercial purpose, personnel, functions, decision-making, risk control, or assets proportionate to the claimed income. E-commerce substance should be supported by documented Canadian functions, inventory control, customer-service activity, marketing decisions, platform contracts, payment flows, and personnel.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Deliberate False Reporting

Statutory sanction: Under Income Tax Act section 239, conviction may result in a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded, imprisonment for up to five years, or both, in addition to the underlying tax, interest, and civil penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorize, assent to, or participate in the conduct may face personal criminal exposure. The corporation remains liable for tax and civil penalties; corporate status does not shield personal criminal conduct. Travel restrictions may arise through criminal process or enforcement measures but are not an automatic tax penalty.
Gross Negligence, Fraudulent Tax Adjustment, or False Statement

Statutory sanction: The gross-negligence penalty is generally 50% of the understated tax or the overstated or improperly claimed credit. Fraud-related conduct may also be prosecuted under the Income Tax Act and Criminal Code.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure where they knowingly direct or participate in false filings. Personal liability is not created merely by holding office; evidence of authorization, participation, or statutory director-responsibility failure is required.
Failure to Withhold and Remit Non-Resident Tax

Statutory sanction: The payer may be assessed for the unremitted withholding tax, arrears interest, and applicable penalties. Penalties may apply for failure to deduct, remit, file NR4 information returns, or provide accurate slips. Treaty benefits may be denied where residence, beneficial ownership, or entitlement is not supported.

Director personal liability: Income Tax Act section 227.1 can impose personal liability on directors for unremitted source deductions, subject to statutory due-diligence defenses and limitation periods. The liability is statutory and does not require piercing the corporate veil in the ordinary common-law sense.
GST/HST Collection and Remittance Failure

Statutory sanction: The corporation may owe unremitted GST/HST, interest, late-filing penalties, gross-negligence penalties, and penalties for false claims. Imported goods may also incur customs duties, import GST, reassessments, and customs penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unremitted GST/HST and certain payroll-related amounts under statutory director-liability provisions, subject to due-diligence defenses. Personal criminal liability may arise for deliberate tax fraud.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess income under Income Tax Act section 247, deny or adjust deductions, impose transfer-pricing penalties generally equal to 10% of the adjustment in qualifying circumstances, and charge interest. Recharacterization and withholding-tax consequences may also arise.

Director personal liability: Directors generally face corporate reassessment rather than automatic personal transfer-pricing liability. Personal exposure may arise for knowing participation in fraud, false statements, source-deduction failures, or criminal tax evasion.
Thin Capitalization and Excessive Related-Party Financing

Statutory sanction: Interest deductions may be denied or limited, and the denied amount may be treated as a dividend subject to Canadian withholding tax. General interest-relief limitations and transfer-pricing adjustments may apply in addition.

Director personal liability: Ordinary thin-capitalization reassessments are normally corporate liabilities. Directors may incur personal liability only through separate statutory source-deduction obligations or culpable participation in fraud or evasion.
Shell Company, Beneficial Ownership, or Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: CRA may deny treaty relief, assess withholding tax, interest, penalties, and apply the general anti-avoidance rule or treaty anti-abuse provisions. False beneficial-ownership declarations can also create tax-evasion and information-reporting exposure.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal civil and criminal exposure where they knowingly approve sham arrangements or false declarations. Corporate veil piercing is not automatic; statutory director liability and participation-based liability are the principal routes.
Anti-Money-Laundering and Proceeds-of-Crime Compliance

Statutory sanction: The Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act provides administrative monetary penalties that can reach CAD 1,000,000 for serious violations. Criminal proceeds-of-crime and money-laundering offences may carry fines and imprisonment, including imprisonment of up to five years on indictment for specified offences; exact sanctions depend on the offence and prosecution route.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, and employees may be prosecuted personally for knowing participation, facilitation, obstruction, or false reporting. Corporate penalties do not prevent individual criminal charges, asset restraint, forfeiture, or court-ordered restrictions.
Customs, Origin, Valuation, and Import Documentation

Statutory sanction: The Canada Border Services Agency may reassess duties and import taxes and impose monetary penalties under the Administrative Monetary Penalty System. Serious customs fraud can lead to seizure, forfeiture, prosecution, and additional tax consequences.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal criminal or regulatory exposure for knowing customs fraud. Corporate ownership does not protect an individual who personally makes or authorizes false customs declarations.
Corporate Record, Beneficial Ownership, and Filing Failure

Statutory sanction: The corporation may face provincial or federal corporate penalties, compliance orders, loss of good standing, dissolution procedures, and tax reassessment based on inadequate books and records. Tax-record failures can also trigger penalties under the Income Tax Act and Excise Tax Act.

Director personal liability: Directors may face statutory fines or liability for knowingly failing to maintain required records or for authorizing misleading corporate filings. A general corporate veil piercing is exceptional and requires a recognized legal basis such as fraud or improper conduct.
Unlawful Profit Distribution and Insolvent Repatriation

Statutory sanction: The distribution may be voidable or recoverable, and directors may face statutory contribution, repayment, or civil liability under applicable corporate insolvency and Ontario or federal corporate-law rules. Tax recharacterization, shareholder-benefit, withholding, and interest consequences may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorize unlawful distributions may be ordered to repay amounts or contribute to corporate liabilities. Travel bans and imprisonment are not automatic consequences of an unlawful dividend but may follow separate fraud, insolvency, or criminal proceedings.
