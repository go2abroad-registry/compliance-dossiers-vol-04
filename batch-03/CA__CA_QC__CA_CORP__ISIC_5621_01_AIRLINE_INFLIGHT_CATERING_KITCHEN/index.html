Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital is required for a Quebec business corporation; at least one common share must be issued to a substantive shareholder. Payment for subscribed shares must be received in full before issuance; for non-cash consideration, the board must assign fair value and receive the asset or service before certificates are delivered. No escrow is required for ordinary cash subscriptions, but board resolution must record receipt and allocation of consideration.

Funding & unlock path: Fire safety certificate from the municipal fire prevention bureau after inspection of commercial kitchen hood suppression, fire exits, and electrical load; structural load inspection certificate from the Régie du bâtiment du Quebec (RBQ) for ovens and industrial equipment; and CNESST occupational health clearance confirming employee safety program, emergency evacuation plan, and workplace sanitary standards.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — Federal tax administration; Revenu Québec — Quebec provincial tax administration. Portals: CRA My Business Account (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/e-services-businesses/business-account.html) and Revenu Québec MonService (https://mon.affaires.revenuquebec.ca/).

E-government filing portal

Foreign non-resident corporations must first register with CRA using Business Registration Online (BRO) or Form RC1 to obtain a federal Business Number (BN) and select a corporate income tax account (RC account) and GST/HST account (RT account). The BN is the Canadian TIN. Immediately thereafter, the corporation must register with Revenu Québec through MonService to receive a Quebec enterprise number (NEQ) and a QST/TVQ registration number. If the corporation has employees in Quebec, it must also open a CRA payroll account (RP) and register for Quebec source deductions and employer contributions. Registration should occur before the first taxable supply or before making a claim for zero-rated exports.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not have a check-the-box election; a Business Corporation is an entity-level taxpayer under Part I of the Income Tax Act and the Taxation Act (Quebec). Relevant elections include: (i) a s. 216 election for a non-resident corporation to compute Canadian rental or property income on a net basis; (ii) allocation of taxable income among provinces on the T2 schedule; and (iii) QST/GST input-tax-credit allocation elections. Hybrid mismatch rules under ss. 18.2/18.3 apply automatically and cannot be overridden by a domestic entity election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

$30,000 CAD (small-supplier threshold) of taxable supplies in the current and immediately preceding four calendar quarters; equivalent QST threshold under Quebec law. A non-resident carrying on business in Canada or making taxable supplies to Canadian recipients must obtain GST/HST and QST registration when the threshold is met, and may register voluntarily to obtain input tax credits and zero-rating.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available under Schedule VI, Part V, section 20 of the Excise Tax Act and the QST regulation for tangible personal property exported from Canada and for certain services supplied to non-residents. Airline in-flight catering supplies qualify where the goods are purchased by an airline for use on an international departure flight, are physically loaded onto the aircraft from the bonded catering kitchen, are exported from Canadian tax territory as aircraft stores, and the supplier retains documentary evidence of the foreign destination. Services forming part of the catering arrangement are zero-rated only if the beneficiary is a non-resident and the service is provided for use entirely outside Canada or is ancillary to the export.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CBSA export report or approved air cargo declaration/manifest)
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill evidencing international departure and aircraft stores movement
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank settlement statement confirming repatriation of export proceeds
  • Commercial invoice with zero-rated export notation and GST/HST/QST registration numbers of supplier and buyer
  • Bonded catering kitchen transfer/assignment document linking the inventory release to the aircraft flight
  • Flight manifest or aircraft catering load sheet showing destination outside Canada
  • Online export record retained in the CRA-prescribed format for at least six years

No real-time fiscal clearance or digital stamping mandate exists in Canada. CRA requires that an invoicing document contain the prescribed supplier/recipient, description, tax, and total data under ss. 221-229 of the Excise Tax Act; Revenu Québec has parallel requirements under the Quebec Sales Tax Act. Invoices may be paper, PDF, EDI, or APIs so long as records retain integrity. Filing and payment of GST/HST/QST returns are electronic for most registrants, but no continuous transaction control is used.

CRA and Revenu Québec audit zero-rating claims by reconciling invoices, air waybills, CBSA export data, and bank receipts. Absent complete contemporaneous proof, the supply is reclassified as taxable in Canada and the unpaid GST/HST/QST is assessed, plus interest and arrears penalties. Failure to repatriate proceeds within 180 days may be treated as a red flag, though it is not itself a statutory deadline in Canada. Fraudulent or grossly negligent claims trigger (i) a 50% gross-negligence penalty under s. 163.2 of the Income Tax Act and parallel QST/GST penalty provisions, (ii) denial and clawback of input tax credits and QST refunds previously claimed, and (iii) criminal prosecution for wilful tax evasion punishable by fines and imprisonment.

Stage 04

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canadian law (Bank of Canada Act, Currency Act) imposes no exchange controls on current or capital account transactions. The Canadian dollar floats freely; residents and non-residents may transfer funds in and out without licence, permit, or central bank approval. Profit remittance from a Quebec catering corporation is subject only to Part XIII non-resident withholding tax (statutory 25%; commonly reduced under income tax treaties, e.g., 5-15% under the Canada-US Convention depending on shareholding) and, where applicable, the thin capitalization and excessive interest and expense rules of the Income Tax Act. No repatriation hurdles exist beyond standard financial reporting; funds may be held, converted, and remitted in any currency at market rates.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Zero surrender obligation. Canada does not require export receipts to be converted into, or repatriated in, Canadian dollars, and imposes no statutory timeline for bringing foreign-currency proceeds onshore. The only export-related duties are documentary: an export declaration (B13A via the CBSA G7 export process / Canadian International Trade Single Window) for qualifying goods leaving Canada (generally shipments over CAD 2,000) and statistical reporting under the Customs Act and the Export and Import Permits Act. Cross-border electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more must be reported to FINTRAC by the financial institution, a compliance threshold rather than a surrender rule.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada) - monetary and systemic authority; Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) - federal prudential bank supervisor; Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) - national AML/CFT supervisor. Canada operates no separate foreign exchange administration because the capital account is fully liberalized.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Ramp Fit: High

Medium, and only as a supplementary payment corridor; never as the primary liquidity host. Offshore digital EMIs are not licensed deposit-taking institutions under the Bank Act (Schedule I/II) and cannot operate as banks in Canada. If they remit money or provide payment services to Canadians, they must either register as a Money Service Business with FINTRAC under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act and as a Payment Service Provider under the Retail Payment Activities Act (Bank of Canada regime in force 2024-2025), or confine their activity to foreign suppliers. The decisive limitation is that EMIs lack direct access to the Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) and the Automated Clearing Settlement System (ACSS), hold no CDIC deposit insurance, and cannot credibly support named-airline ramp payments, CBSA bonded-warehouse settlements, or Quebec payroll attachments. For this inflight-catering treasury, an EMI may be used only for low-value vendor disbursements or employee card programs, always backed by a tier-one Schedule I operating account.
