Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) / Agence du revenu du Canada, with Ontario corporate income tax administered by CRA under the federal-provincial tax collection agreement. Main portal: CRA My Business Account (MyBA) and Business Registration Online (BRO); customs matters are administered by the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA).

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity obtains its statutory tax identifier by applying to CRA for a Business Number (BN), through Form RC1 or CRA's Business Registration Online service. The BN is a 9-digit identifier to which CRA attaches program accounts: RP for corporate income tax, RT for GST/HST, PD for payroll, and RM for import/export. Non-resident corporations carrying on business in Canada must register for a BN and may simultaneously register for GST/HST program accounts. Non-resident suppliers of digital services or intangible property can use the simplified GST/HST registration mechanism for non-resident vendors. Ontario issues no separate tax registration number; federal and Ontario corporate taxes and Ontario HST are administered through the same CRA BN.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not provide a U.S.-style check-the-box election. A Business Corporation incorporated under the Canada Business Corporations Act or the Ontario Business Corporations Act is a separate taxpayer subject to federal corporate income tax under the Income Tax Act (Canada) and Ontario corporate income tax under the Ontario Corporations Tax Act; pass-through treatment is not available. Hybrid mismatch rules under the Income Tax Act apply automatically to deny or recharacterize deductions arising from hybrid mismatch arrangements; there is no elective opt-in. Certain narrow elections exist for non-residents under subsection 216(1) of the Income Tax Act to file Canadian returns on a net basis for rental income or timber royalties, but these do not alter corporate entity classification.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in annual worldwide taxable supplies for the GST/HST small-supplier threshold. For non-resident digital economy suppliers, the registration threshold is CAD 30,000 in gross supplies made to Canadian recipients. A Canadian corporation exceeding the threshold must register and charge HST; Ontario does not impose a separate registration threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available under Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act where goods are exported from Canada or where a supply of services or intangible personal property is made to a non-resident for consumption, use or enjoyment outside Canada. For a no-code website builder or low-code SaaS platform, cross-border digital subscriptions, software access, templates, and related online services supplied to non-resident customers are generally zero-rated if the recipient is not resident in Canada, the place of supply is offshore, and the underlying digital product or intellectual property is not used in Canada. The supplier must maintain contractual, payment, and delivery evidence proving non-residence of the recipient and the offshore place of use.

  • CBSA export declaration (Form B13A/B13B or CAED electronic filing) for physical goods exceeding the CAD 2,000 reporting threshold; not required for pure digital or cloud-based deliveries
  • Commercial invoice / sales order / end-user licence agreement identifying the non-resident buyer and the place of supply
  • Bill of lading or airway bill showing physical export of goods, where applicable; for digital services, server logs, download records, usage logs, or email/access delivery evidence
  • Bank inward remittance certificate / SWIFT MT103 showing settlement of export proceeds
  • GST/HST export evidence worksheet or internal substantiation file retained for six years under CRA record-keeping rules

Canada does not currently mandate real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction controls, or digital stamping of invoices. Electronic invoicing is accepted and permitted. GST/HST returns must be filed electronically where the registrant exceeds CRA's mandatory e-filing threshold. There is no mandatory tax authority invoice clearance API; instead, CRA relies on registrant records and GST/HST NETFILE reporting. Registrants must retain complete books and records in Canada, or make them available to CRA on request, for at least six years.

CRA may reassess GST/HST within four years of the relevant reporting period, with no limitation period where false or wilful statements are involved. If export evidence is inadequate, CRA may reverse the zero-rating and assess tax at the 13% HST rate, disallow related input tax credits, charge daily interest, and impose civil penalties under Part IX of the Excise Tax Act, including gross-negligence penalties based on the amount of tax sought to be evaded. Wilful evasion may also trigger criminal prosecution and director or officer liability. Canada has no explicit statutory export proceeds repatriation deadline; the 180-day value is set as a conservative internal documentation benchmark, while the substantive evidence obligation is governed by the six-year CRA record-retention requirement.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada provides full, unrestricted convertibility on both current account and capital account transactions. There are no exchange-control approvals for inbound equity subscription, loans, dividend remittance, service royalties, or repayment of capital. Profit remittance is subject only to tax; the Income Tax Act imposes 25% Part XIII withholding on dividends to non-resident shareholders, reducible by applicable tax treaties to rates such as 5% for significant corporate holders under the Canada-US Tax Treaty. Ontario levies no separate provincial withholding. CAD and all major foreign currencies may be freely held, transferred, and converted without any Certificate of Eligibility or Bank of Canada permission.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada imposes no statutory surrender of export proceeds, no mandatory conversion into CAD, and no repatriation deadline. Exporters may retain foreign currency proceeds abroad or in Canadian foreign-currency bank accounts indefinitely. Cross-border obligations are limited to indirect reporting: CBSA export declarations under the B13A process for physical goods, FINTRAC large international electronic funds transfer reports for wires at or above C$10,000, and Income Tax Act record retention for six years. No conversion ratio exists.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (BOC) is the monetary authority; Canada does not maintain a specialized foreign exchange control administration. The Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) supervises federally chartered banks, and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) enforces anti-money laundering and counter-terrorist financing requirements. The Bank of Canada can theoretically intervene in FX markets only with government authorization, but Canada has no standing exchange-control regime.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility: Medium, conditional only. Canada has no EMI licence equivalent; deposit-taking is reserved to institutions authorized under the Bank Act, generally Schedule I banks. An offshore digital EMI cannot serve as the sole operational bank account for the Canadian entity because CAD payroll, GST/HST, and CAD settlement must be cleared through a domestic bank. An offshore EMI may be used as a supplementary cross-border payout rail if it is FINTRAC-registered as a Money Services Business (MSB) in order to serve Canadian clients, and if it complies with Ontario's Money Services Business Act where applicable. If the EMI merely holds funds outside Canada and does not solicit Canadian residents, it can receive company remittances, but it still lacks authority to provide Canadian banking services.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound shareholder capital must be remitted by SWIFT MT103 through a Schedule I bank's global payments network. The beneficiary must be the Canadian corporation's exact legal name and Canadian operating account using Canadian transit and institution numbers; for USD funding, the bank's USD correspondent details are required. The SWIFT Field 70 remittance information should state 'Capital subscription / paid-up share capital / FDI' so the bank can classify the inflow as a foreign direct investment and avoid mischaracterization as trade proceeds. The bank will require a source-of-funds pack, executed subscription agreement, board resolution, and account authorization documents before clearing the first large deposit. FINTRAC reporting is triggered automatically for any international EFT of C$10,000 or more. Where CAD liquidity is ultimately needed, conversion should be executed only through the bank's spot FX desk under a signed treasury mandate, and all capital documentation must be retained to support Statistics Canada foreign-direct-investment surveys and the corporation's tax records.Trade settlements should use SWIFT MT103 for single open-account payments, SWIFT MT102 for batch vendor/cloud-hosting disbursements, and commercial letters of credit under UCP 600 via SWIFT MT700/MT760/MT799 for documentary or supply-chain transactions. MT202/COVER is available for bank-to-bank reimbursement legs. CAD domestic clearing uses the Large Value Transfer System (LVTS) and the Real-Time Rail (RTR); cross-border USD payments clear by SWIFT gpi, Fedwire, and correspondent banking channels. For the low-code website builder vertical, outbound flows will principally fund software licensing, cloud infrastructure, API services, overseas contractors, and digital advertising; all outbound international wires at or above C$10,000 must comply with FINTRAC large-EFT reporting and the bank's enhanced due diligence rules for payee verification.FX Forwards — plain vanilla deliverable CAD/USD and CAD/EUR forwards with a Schedule I bank under an ISDA 2002 Master Agreement; used only to hedge identified transactional or translation exposure.Cross-Currency Swaps — CAD/USD or CAD/EUR swaps to re-exchange notional and interest flows for balance-sheet or refinancing hedging.FX Options — European-style and OTC vanilla options purchased to protect budget rates while preserving upside; sold options require an explicit board-approved treasury policy.Non-Deliverable Forwards (NDFs) — legally permissible when hedging an illiquid or non-deliverable foreign currency and settling in CAD; NDFs against CAD itself are unnecessary because CAD is freely deliverable.CME-cleared CAD/USD FX futures and standard exchange-traded FX instruments, subject to OSFI and Canadian Securities Administrators trade-reporting thresholds and the corporation's hedging mandate.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 (Form T106 filing trigger for non-arm's-length cross-border transactions); CRA expects contemporaneous TP documentation for all material transactions regardless of threshold; Country-by-Country Reporting applies at C$750,000,000 consolidated group revenue.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not impose a standalone Economic Substance Regulation; CRA applies common-law substance-over-form and statutory GAAR under s.245. A Canadian corporation must demonstrate actual management and control in Canada; a non-resident claimant must have a permanent establishment with meaningful personnel and decision-making substance to avoid Canadian re-characterization.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion — Failure to File and False Statements

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act s.238: failure to file — C$1,000–C$25,000 fine plus up to 12 months imprisonment on summary conviction. Income Tax Act s.239(1): evasion — fine equal to 100%–200% of tax evaded and imprisonment up to 5 years on indictment. CRA also imposes s.163(2) penalty of 50% of unreported income.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly liable under s.227.1 for unremitted withholdings or Part XIII tax. Where the corporation is a sham, courts pierce the veil, exposing directors to civil restitution. Travel bans may be imposed as a bail condition or court order in criminal tax proceedings; imprisonment can result upon conviction.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: CRA reassessments under s.247(2); s.247(3) penalty of 10% of the pricing adjustment if the taxpayer does not provide requested records and principal documents within prescribed timelines; interest and multi-year reassessments apply.

Director personal liability: Personal liability arises for signing false information returns or knowingly causing the corporation to underpay tax. Directors may be liable under s.227.1 if the adjustment creates unremitted foreign withholding; travel bans may be imposed if criminal charges are laid; negligence claims can pursue personal assets.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: CRA can disregard the shell under sham or GAAR (s.245) and reallocate income to the true economic owner; a 50% serious misstatement penalty applies under s.163.2; Ontario may dissolve the corporation under the Ontario Business Corporations Act; Part XIII tax and gross penalties can be assessed on re-characterized distributions.

Director personal liability: Directors who sanction the facade can be personally liable for clawed-back distributions; Ontario courts may impose director-management bans; civil restitution orders can compel personal payment; travel bans may accompany criminal counts.
AML / Proceeds of Crime (Payment Rail Materialization)

Statutory sanction: Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act: administrative penalties up to C$500,000; Criminal Code s.462.31 money laundering: imprisonment up to 10 years; asset forfeiture orders and restraint orders under s.462.32; CRA can collect arrears from frozen assets.

Director personal liability: Directors who direct or participate in money laundering are criminally liable as principals and can be personally fined or imprisoned; travel bans and extradition proceedings may be used; personal property may be restrained and confiscated.
