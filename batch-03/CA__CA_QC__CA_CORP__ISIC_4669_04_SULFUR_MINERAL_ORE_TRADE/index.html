Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) for GST/HST and federal tax; Revenu Québec for QST and provincial tax. Portals: CRA 'My Business Account' and Revenu Québec 'Mon dossier d'entreprise'.

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity must first obtain a federal/Quebec enterprise registration, then apply to the CRA for a Business Number (BN) and GST/HST account using Form RC1 or online through the Business Registration Online service. For Quebec, the entity must register with Revenu Québec for QST and provincial obligations using Form LM-101-V (GST/HST and QST complex registration), providing Quebec Business Identifier (NEQ). Non-resident corporations may also require an income tax TIN through Form RC214 or a Canadian branch registration.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation with territorial application: non-resident corporations are taxable on Canadian-source business income; GST/HST applies to taxable supplies made in Canada and QST applies to taxable supplies made in Quebec.

Available election forms

For GST/HST: GST/HST registration group election (Excise Tax Act s.156), closely related corporation election (s.150), and joint election for supplies of exempt property. For income tax: non-resident corporation can elect under subsection 216(1) for rental income; no US-style check-the-box election exists, but provincial QST grouping elections with Revenu Québec mirror the GST/HST group rules.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of cumulative worldwide taxable supplies (threshold under Division II of the Excise Tax Act for GST/HST and under the Quebec QST Act for QST; registration becomes mandatory once exceeded if supplying in Canada/Quebec).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supplies of goods that are exported from Canada, and services rendered to non-residents (or wholly performed outside Canada), quality for zero-rating under the Excise Tax Act and QST Act. The exporter must prove the goods were physically delivered to a destination outside Canada, that no resident acquired the supply before export, and maintain contemporaneous export evidence such as customs documentation, carrier transport records, sale contracts, and payment evidence. For QST, the qualifying supply must be exported from Quebec/Canada to be zero-rated.

  • Valid commercial invoice evidencing the sale of granular sulfur
  • CBSA Customs Export Declaration (Form B13A or Canada Export Reporting System record)
  • Bill of Lading / multimodal transport document evidencing export
  • Carrier or freight forwarder certificate of export / destination confirmation
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (IRC) or bank SWIFT/letter of credit payment record from the non-resident buyer
  • Contract specifying international commercial terms (Incoterms) and transfer of ownership
  • Customs export control reports (e.g., CERS transaction filing confirming customs release)

No national real-time fiscal clearance or tax-authority digital stamping applies. CRA requires business records in a specified electronic or readable format (ECM standards). Quebec mandates electronic invoicing for suppliers billing Quebec public authorities and imposes specific invoicing content requirements; general B2B e-invoicing follows Revenu Québec's prescribed XML/EDI standards but without continuous transaction controls.

CRA and Revenu Québec may disallow zero-rating and reassess net tax, with compound interest, if documentation is insufficient or the exporter has not demonstrated physical export. Fraudulent zero-rating claims attract penalties of 25% or more for gross negligence (Excise Tax Act s.280), 200% penalties and criminal fines/imprisonment for wilful evasion (s.281 and Income Tax Act), and extended reassessment periods up to four years or indefinitely for fraud. QST fraud penalties mirror federal exposure with interest and surcharges imposed by Revenu Québec under the Tax Administration Act.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a floating exchange rate regime with full convertibility for current account transactions under IMF Article VIII. Capital account transactions are generally permitted without prior approval for corporate activities. Profit remittance to foreign shareholders is unrestricted, subject only to Canadian tax withholding at source and beneficial ownership identification under the Income Tax Act.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No mandatory surrender of export proceeds to the central bank is required under Canadian law. Exporters must receive full payment for goods. All export receipts exceeding CAD 10,000 must be reported to FINTRAC; banks may request supporting documents such as commercial invoices and bills of lading for verification.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Offshore digital EMIs face significant legal constraints in Canada due to FATF compliance and anti-money laundering obligations. Cross-border fintech platforms must comply with Canadian Payment Services Regulations; remote onboarding is restricted for high-volume trade finance, favoring traditional banks for sulfur ore trade settlements.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

General combined federal and Quebec corporate income tax headline rate: 26.5%, comprising 15% federal general corporate rate after provincial abatement and 11.5% Quebec general corporate rate.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Part XIII of the Canadian Income Tax Act on dividends paid by a Canadian corporation to a non-resident shareholder. This rate is determined before any applicable tax treaty reduction.

The balance-due date for federal tax remittance is two months after year-end, extended to three months for eligible qualified Canadian-controlled private corporations.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Form T106 (Information Return for Non-Arm's Length Transactions) must be filed with the CRA when the aggregate fair market value of reportable cross-border transactions with each non-resident related party exceeds C$1 million. CRA's T4117 transfer-pricing guidelines require a comprehensive Local File and Master File (or equivalent combined file) when controlled transactions exceed C$2 million in a taxation year; documentation should be contemporaneous and completed before the filing deadline.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not deploy a stand-alone Economic Substance Regulation statute akin to UAE ESR. However, economic substance is central to the 'real risk and control' functional analysis applied under Section 247 of the Income Tax Act and to the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) under Section 245. A Quebec trading entity with no fixed business premises, no local employees involved in ocean-charter negotiation, no cargo risk, and no substantive decision-making functions will be vulnerable to treaty denial under the Principal Purpose Test (OECD MLI, Article 7) and to reassessment on the ground that the Canadian vehicle is a conduit with no commercial rationale. The CRA may reattribute the trading income to the foreign principal and assess the Canadian entity as a mere agent.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Willful Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Under section 239 of the Income Tax Act and section 101 of the Quebec Taxation Act: a fine of 50% to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for up to two years. Gross negligence penalties may amount to 50% of understated income. Failure to remit source deductions under section 227.1 activates strict civil liability equal to the unremitted amount plus interest and penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers are personally liable for unpaid employee source deductions and withholding tax held in trust, regardless of the corporate veil. Where the corporation is a sham or veneer used to conceal foreign beneficial ownership, courts may pierce the veil and impose joint and several civil restitution on directors. CRA criminal investigation can recommend travel-ban orders, asset-freezing seizures, and jail terms of up to two years for every month of ongoing evasion.
Transfer Pricing Violation

Statutory sanction: Section 247(3) transfer-pricing penalty equal to 10% of the net transfer-pricing adjustment where the adjustment exceeds the lesser of C$5,000,000 and 10% of gross revenue for that year. The CRA may also impose Section 161(1) arrears interest, Section 162 late-information penalties, and reassess open years. In addition, a false T106 entry carries a specific penalty under Section 162(10).

Director personal liability: Directors who sign a knowingly false T106 or TP documentation may be co-assessed under the due-diligence provisions; criminal liability attaches for forgery or fraud. The Quebec Revenue Board may allow the CRA to recover from directors where the corporation is insolvent and the director exercised effective control over pricing. Travel bans are possible where the underlying conduct is prosecuted as a criminal offence.
Shell Company / Economic Substance Failure

Statutory sanction: GAAR under Section 245 permits the CRA to deny the tax benefit and re-assess the entire transaction series. Misrepresentation penalties under Section 163(2) can add 50% of the understated tax. The competent authority may deny reduced treaty withholding rates under the OECD Multilateral Instrument where obtaining the treaty benefit was a principal purpose and substance is absent. Revenu Québec can similarly issue an 'artificial transaction' penalty and impose gross negligence fines.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for corporate tax where the corporation is used as a sham to defeat taxes; Canadian courts may impose individual liability for knowing participation in a fraudulent scheme. A Director of a non-resident parent signing Canadian filings is considered a 'resident director' for service of process, and CRA may obtain a travel-ban against the individual through the Federal Court in fraud proceedings.
AML Compliance Deficit (FINTRAC / Proceeds of Crime)

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act: administrative monetary penalty up to C$100,000 for an individual and C$500,000 for a corporation for a first serious violation; criminal prosecution can impose imprisonment up to five years and unlimited fines. FINTRAC issues public sanctions and may name the corporation on its non-compliance list.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers can be personally fined and imprisoned for consenting to or permitting the AML violation; corporate veil protection is stripped for individuals who participate in concealing beneficial ownership. Travel bans may be ordered during an active criminal investigation if the individual is a flight risk. The 'responsible person' nominated with FINTRAC is exposed to personal civil restitution for funds laundered through the trading entity.
