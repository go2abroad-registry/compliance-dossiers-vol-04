Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), GST/HST Registration Portal via CRA My Business Account (MyBA) and Business Registration Online (BRO)

E-government filing portal

A foreign limited partnership that carries on business in Ontario through a permanent establishment must apply for a CRA Business Number (BN) and GST/HST account before the first taxable supply. The application form is RC1 (Request for a Business Number and Certain Program Accounts), filed online via CRA Business Registration Online or by mail/fax. The general partner must provide the partnership’s legal name, Canadian address for service (or agent for non-resident partners), description of business activities, and estimated annual global taxable supply amount. For partnerships, each partner’s social insurance number (for individuals) or BN (for entities) must be disclosed. The CRA will issue a 9-digit BN plus a 2-digit program account suffix 'RT' for GST/HST. If the LP is not required to register because its worldwide taxable supplies do not exceed the $30,000 threshold, it may still voluntarily register to claim input tax credits; voluntary registration requires approval and generally remains in effect for at least one year.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible

Available election forms

Canada does not have a check-the-box election. A limited partnership is automatically treated as a fiscal pass-through entity for both income tax and GST/HST purposes; the liability for GST/HST may be accounted for by the partnership itself (with each partner jointly and severally liable to the extent of partnership property) or, in appropriate cases, by a designated partner under an election. Under the Income Tax Act, the partnership can elect under subsection 10(1) of the Income Tax Application Rules to defer Canadian federal and provincial income tax on certain pre-1972 property, but for a newly formed medical device manufacturing LP, no material statutory election is required. For U.S. tax purposes, if the LP has U.S. investors, the relevant 'check-the-box' election is made on IRS Form 8832, not in Canada, and determines only the U.S. federal classification of the Canadian LP (e.g., as a partnership or disregarded entity). Canada also recognizes hybrid mismatch arrangement provisions under the Income Tax Act, but these rules apply to deny deductions for payments resulting in non-taxation, not as elective mechanisms. The LP may, however, file an election to have a partner, rather than the partnership, report and remit GST/HST on behalf of the partnership where all partners agree (CRA Form RC4590 is not applicable; this is an internal partnership allocation election).

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in worldwide taxable supplies (excluding zero-rated exports) over a 12-month period, or CAD 30,000 where a non-resident makes taxable supplies in Canada; once exceeded, GST/HST registration is mandatory.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply of goods is zero-rated as 'zero-rated export' under section 1 of Part V of Schedule VI to the Excise Tax Act when the supplier has evidence that the goods are exported from Canada (including by way of sale to a non-resident before shipment) and are delivered to a destination outside Canada within a reasonable time. For supplies of services, zero-rating applies where the service is provided to a non-resident person and the service is supplied wholly in respect of tangible personal property situated outside Canada, or where the supply of an intangible is made to a non-resident who is not a Canadian GST/HST registrant. Ultra-HD 4K minimally invasive rigid endoscopes and associated laparoscopy tower components qualify for zero-rating when sold to customers outside Canada, including to U.S. hospitals, so long as the documentary substantiation demonstrates the goods leave Canada and are not consumed or used in Canada. The supplier must also be a GST/HST registrant to claim input tax credits attributable to the export; if the export invoice exceeds CAD 30,000, mandatory reporting codes apply in Box 104 of the GST/HST return.

  • Customs Export Declaration (B13A Export Declaration submitted via the Canadian Integrated Customs System / Canadian International Trade Single Window, or proof of forced inclusion in the Export Reporting Program)
  • Commercial invoice and bill of lading / air waybill evidencing the actual shipment of goods to an overseas consignee
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or equivalent bank documentation (e.g., SWIFT MT103 wire transfer credit advice) proving the foreign purchaser's payment was received in Canadian currency or foreign currency within the prescribed time frame
  • Packing list, certificate of origin (if required under the applicable free trade agreement), and proof of delivery at the export destination (e.g., signed delivery receipt or customs entry documentation from the destination country)

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or continuous transaction control (CTC) regime exists at the federal or Ontario level. However, registrants must issue GST/HST-compliant invoices containing prescribed information: supplier name and business number, date of invoice, consideration, tax rate, and amount of tax. Invoices must be retained and supplied to the recipient for standard-rated, zero-rated, and exempt supplies. Electronic invoicing in any structured format (e.g., EDI, PDF, XML) is allowed with the recipient's consent. Filing and remittance of GST/HST returns must be done electronically through CRA My Business Account, EFT, or a CRA-approved third-party filing API for registrants with certain thresholds. No cryptographic seal or government pre-clearance is required. Ontario’s Ministry of Finance does not impose a separate provincial sales tax for the HST; the federal CRA administers the Ontario HST portion on behalf of Ontario under a single unified return.

The CRA may deny zero-rating and reassess the exporter for unremitted GST/HST at 13% on the full invoice amount, plus interest compounded daily, where the documentary evidence does not establish that the goods were exported. Under the Excise Tax Act, penalties include a fine of up to 25% of the amount of tax that the exporter attempted to evade, a minimum fine of CAD 100, and imprisonment for up to two years on summary conviction or up to five years on indictment for fraudulent zero-rating claims. Additionally, the exporter may be subject to a 'gross negligence' penalty under section 285 of the Excise Tax Act equal to 25% of the amount of any tax credit or refund claimed in excess of the true amount. Retroactive clawbacks can extend to all prior returns within the normal reassessment period (four years), and the period is extended to eight years in cases of misrepresentation or fraud. The exporter must maintain all export documentation for six years; failure to produce records on request triggers a penalty of CAD 100 per day (maximum CAD 5,000) and may result in an adverse inference against the exporter's eligibility for zero-rating.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates under a free-floating exchange-rate regime with no general exchange-control legislation: the Canadian dollar is fully convertible for both current-account transactions (export settlement, trade disbursements, service fees) and capital-account transactions (limited-partnership contributions, profit distributions, loan repayments, liquidation proceeds). An Ontario limited partnership may remit profits to non-resident limited partners without any central-bank authorization or FX licensing. The only material hurdles are: (i) Part XIII Income Tax Act non-resident withholding tax of 25% on Canadian-source distributions, which may be reduced by an applicable income tax treaty (e.g., Canada-US Tax Treaty, Canada-EU treaties) where the limited partner is treaty-eligible; (ii) the Investment Canada Act notification or net-benefit review where a non-Canadian acquires control of the Canadian business through the LP, applying only where the acquired units carry a majority of partnership profits and a majority of dissolution assets; and (iii) FINTRAC reporting of cross-border electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more.

Export proceeds surrender rules

The federal statutory framework does not impose export-proceeds surrender requirements, mandatory repatriation deadlines, or prescribed conversion ratios. Export receipts from medical-device sales may be retained indefinitely in USD, EUR, or other currencies. Regulatory compliance is nevertheless required on four points: foreign-currency export revenue must be reported in CAD for income-tax purposes on the partnership's T5013 income tax return using the Bank of Canada daily exchange rate; export goods declarations (B13A) must be filed with Statistics Canada and the Canada Border Services Agency for shipments exceeding CAD 2,000 per item or prescribed thresholds; cross-border funds transfers exceeding CAD 10,000 must be reported to FINTRAC; and bank-held proceeds are subject to standard OFSI-compliant deposit and capital-restriction terms.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), acting in coordination with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for banking prudential supervision, FINTRAC for anti-money-laundering and cross-border financial reporting, and Global Affairs Canada for economic sanctions and trade controls under the Export and Import Permits Act.

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium for auxiliary collection and payable automation; Low for primary treasury concentration. Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound subscription proceeds are routed via SWIFT MT103 in commercial payment format from the non-resident limited partner's bank to the Canadian Schedule I bank's correspondent network (e.g., RBC New York, TD Bank US, or Scotiabank New York). Field 50K must identify the remitter exactly as scheduled in the Partnership Register; Field 33B states the foreign-currency amount; Field 72 narrative must state 'LP Capital Contribution — ISIC 3250 Endoscopy Manufacturing Escrow'; and the remitter's bank is required to include a reference to the escrow agreement in field 77B. The receiving bank converts to CAD at the Bank of Canada daily reference rate and credits the Capital Escrow Account within one business day. FINTRAC cross-border EFT reporting is triggered at CAD 10,000; for aggregate subscriptions above that threshold the bank requires the funding letter and source-of-funds statement described above. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% (federal 15.0% + Ontario provincial notional 11.5% on active business income eligible for the federal general rate reduction; Ontario's statutory provincial rate is 11.5% for general corporate income, but the combined federal-provincial headline rate for a non-manufacturing active business is approximately 26.5% before any credits or surtaxes)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII tax on dividends paid to non-resident shareholders of a Canadian corporation; however, if the Canadian LP is treated as a corporation for tax purposes (e.g., a 'public corporation' or an entity deemed resident), the statutory rate applies on dividends. For a Canadian LP that is a true partnership, profit distributions are not dividends; instead, a non-resident partner's allocable share of Canadian-source business income is subject to Part I tax if the partner carries on business in Canada through a PE, or Part XIII withholding on 'income from a partnership' under Regulation 805. The general statutory withholding rate on non-resident partner distributions from Canadian-source income is 25% unless a treaty reduces it (typically 5% for corporate shareholders holding at least 10% of voting shares, and 15% for other portfolio shareholders under Article 10 of the Canada–U.S. Tax Convention).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 (or higher if cross-border transactions with non-arm's-length non-resident related parties exceed CAD 1,000,000 in gross revenue for the year); the CRA TP documentation rules require that a participant resident in Canada or a partnership with a non-resident member file a Form T106 (Information Return of Non-Arm's Length Transactions with Non-Residents) if the total value of all reportable transactions with non-resident related parties exceeds CAD 1,000,000. The Local File must be prepared and maintained contemporaneously if the total transactions exceed the threshold; the Master File is required if the multinational group has aggregate revenue of EUR 50 million (or CAD 70 million) and the Canadian entity has annual revenue above CAD 1 million. The CRA also requires a 'Country-by-Country Report' (CbC) for multinational groups with consolidated revenue of EUR 750 million or more.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) in the same form as the UAE or EU/CRS regimes; however, Canada applies the 'economic substance' concept through the GAAR (General Anti-Abuse Rule) under Section 245 of the Income Tax Act, the 'anti-avoidance' rules for transactions that lack a genuine business purpose (e.g., the 'nil consideration' / 'artificial transaction' doctrine), and the 'agency PE' / 'dependent agent permanent establishment' rules under Article 5 of Canadian DTAs. For a Canadian Limited Partnership, the CRA examines whether the partnership has real substance (e.g., actual manufacturing facility, payroll, inventory, and decision-making presence in Ontario) before recognizing it as a separate taxpayer for withholding and treaty entitlement. The Ontario Ministry of Finance also applies the 'Ontario Business Research Institute' rules for R&D credits, which require the work to be performed in Ontario. In addition, the OECD's Pillar Two Global Anti-Base Erosion (GloBE) rules, as incorporated into Canada's Income Tax Act (effective for fiscal years commencing on or after January 1, 2024 for large multinational groups, with the domestic minimum top-up tax), require economic substance-based income allocation; a manufacturing LP with real fixed assets, inventory turnover, and local employment will meet the substance threshold.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (Criminal Code / Income Tax Act)

Statutory sanction: Under Section 327 of the Excise Tax Act and Section 239 of the Income Tax Act, tax evasion is an indictable offence punishable by a fine of up to CAD 200,000 and imprisonment for up to 2 years (summary conviction) or up to 5 years (indictable); in addition, the CRA may levy civil penalties of 50% of the tax evaded (gross negligence penalty under subsection 163(2)), plus interest and reassessment of all taxes, and may deny treaty benefits. The Ontario Provincial Offences Act may impose additional fines for failure to remit provincial taxes.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers of a Canadian LP's general partner (if the general partner is a corporation) are personally liable under Section 227.1 of the Income Tax Act for any corporate tax, interest, and penalties withheld or collected but not remitted to the CRA (e.g., GST/HST, Part XIII withholding, and employee source deductions). For partnerships, the general partner itself is jointly and severally liable with the partnership for the LP's tax debts; if the general partner is a shell company, CRA may pierce the veil and impose personal liability on the natural-person directors under the 'directors' liability' provisions. In cases of fraud or fraudulent conveyance, corporate veil piercing is available under common law (e.g., 'fraud exception'); directors may face civil restitution orders, travel bans under the Income Tax Act (the CRA can seek a court order to prevent a director from leaving Canada if tax arrears exceed CAD 50,000), and criminal prosecution resulting in imprisonment.
Shell Company and Sham Partnership (Anti-Avoidance / GAAR)

Statutory sanction: The CRA may apply the General Anti-Abuse Rule (GAAR) under Section 245 to disregard the partnership for tax purposes, reassess the income to the real economic owner, and deny the benefit of any tax treaty, resulting in the re-characterization of the income as Canadian-source business income taxable on a gross or net basis. Penalties: 25% of the tax benefit (Section 245(1)) in addition to tax reassessed, plus interest. Under Ontario's Tax Act, the Ontario Ministry of Finance may impose comparable reassessments.

Director personal liability: Directors of the general partner who knowingly participate in a GAAR-avoidance scheme or the operation of a shell LP may be deemed personally liable under the 'awareness' test of Section 227.1(1) (they must have known or ought to have known of the failure to remit), and under the 'corporate veil' when the partnership is used as a mere facade for fraud or when the directors have personally guaranteed the entity's tax obligations. Personal liability extends to the amount of withheld taxes, plus interest and penalties, and civil lawsuits by the CRA; in egregious cases, directors may be charged with fraud or conspiracy to evade taxes under the Criminal Code, which carries imprisonment up to 10 years.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing (Section 247 of the Income Tax Act)

Statutory sanction: Under Section 247(2), the CRA has authority to adjust the transfer price to an arm's-length amount, and if the adjustment results in additional tax, the taxpayer is liable for a transfer-pricing penalty of 10% of the adjusted amount (minimum CAD 1,000, maximum CAD 100,000) when the mispricing exceeds CAD 1 million and the documentation is not contemporaneous. Additionally, foreign accrual property income (FAPI) and the 'foreign affiliate' rules may re-attribute income. The Ontario provincial share of the reassessment will be applied. If a treaty provides for a mutual agreement procedure (MAP), the CRA may also deny relief if the taxpayer fails to cooperate.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in implementing a transfer-pricing scheme that is not arm's-length and cause the partnership to fail to remit the reassessed tax are jointly and severally liable for corporate tax debts (Section 227.1); in addition, the 'directors' liability' applies to the T2 and Part XIII obligations. Criminal liability arises under Section 239(1) for 'evasion' where the director makes false statements in the T106 return or underlying invoices; penalties include up to 5 years imprisonment and restitution orders. CRA may also issue an 'assets freezing order' preventing the director from disposing of property.
Anti-Money Laundering (AML) Violations – Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA)

Statutory sanction: Under the PCMLTFA and its Regulations, administrative monetary penalties (AMPs) up to CAD 2,000,000 for corporations (CAD 500,000 for individuals) for non-compliance; a Further Notice of Violation may result in a penalty of up to CAD 1,000,000 per violation; criminal penalties for knowingly engaging in a transaction involving proceeds of crime can result in imprisonment up to 10 years (Section 462.31 of the Criminal Code). The FREIGHT forwarder or payment processor may be subject to a separate FINTRAC compliance order.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for the entity's failure to comply with PCMLTFA if they knowingly or recklessly fail to implement the compliance program; the Federal Court may issue a compliance order against the director personally. Under the Criminal Code, directors who participate in laundering funds face imprisonment for up to 10 years and personal fines; the court may also issue a restitution order to the victims. Directors' personal assets can be frozen under an asset-forfeiture order.
Failure to File and Remit Employee Payroll Deductions (Source Deductions)

Statutory sanction: Failure to remit source deductions is a criminal offence under subsection 227(1.1) of the Income Tax Act; the employer is liable to pay a fine of up to 100% of the amount withheld and not remitted, plus interest at the prescribed rate (compounded daily). The CRA can also impose a 10% penalty on the employer's remuneration for failure to prepare the T4 information returns. Ontario's Employer Health Tax Act imposes a 1% surcharge on unremitted EHT; directors are personally liable for EHT under the Ontario Employer Health Tax Act.

Director personal liability: Directors of the general partner (or of the corporate employer) are absolutely liable for all unremitted source deductions, including the employer's share of CPP/EI and the employee's withheld income tax, regardless of whether the directors received the funds; the CRA can pursue the directors personally without first exhausting the corporation's assets (subsection 227.1(2) provides limited due diligence defence). In addition, the Canada Revenue Agency may obtain a 'director's liability certificate' and file a certificate in the Federal Court, enabling seizure of personal bank accounts and real estate. Criminal penalties for non-payment of payroll deductions can reach up to 5 years imprisonment.
Egregious Cross-Border Tax Avoidance – Treaty Shopping / Abuse of LP Status

Statutory sanction: The CRA may apply the doctrine of 'sham' or has no legal force if the documents misrepresent the true nature of the transaction; reassess under the GAAR to deny treaty benefits; the tax consequence includes reclassification of the income as Part I taxable at the combined federal-Ontario rate, inclusion of foreign accrual property income, and the imposition of a 50% gross negligence penalty if the misrepresentation is made knowingly. Interest is charged at the prescribed rate (9%–10%) compounded daily. The Ontario appeal process does not suspend the filing of a statutory demand or the issuance of a collection action.

Director personal liability: Directors of a foreign parent that are found to have orchestrated a treaty-shopping or sham arrangement may be treated as having personal liability under the 'conduit' doctrine; the Federal Court may pierce the corporate veil and render a director liable for the Canadian tax debts, plus a penalty of up to 25% of the tax benefit. Criminal prosecution under Section 239 may result in a fine up to 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment; travel bans under Section 224.1 are available for tax arrears exceeding CAD 50,000.
