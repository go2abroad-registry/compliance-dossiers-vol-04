Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) — Business Registration Online / My Business Account portal; Ontario Ministry of Finance for Ontario provincial corporate tax and Ontario HST administration through CRA under the harmonized system.

E-government filing portal

An Ontario business corporation obtains a 9-digit federal Business Number (BN) from the CRA either through Business Registration Online after incorporation or by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number). Non-resident corporations must also file a certified certificate of incorporation, a certificate of incumbency, proof of registered office, and a completed Canada Revenue Agency account questionnaire, with any required agent-for-service information. If the corporation supplies taxable goods or services in Canada and is not a small supplier, a GST/HST account (RT program account) is added to the same BN via the BCRA. Foreign persons may register for GST/HST using Form RC1 and, where applicable, Form RC1FP (non-resident specific) with supporting documentation. The BN is the statutory tax identification number for federal GST/HST, income tax, payroll deductions, and provincial taxes collected by CRA.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box election is available in Canada. A business corporation incorporated under the Ontario Business Corporations Act is automatically classified as a taxable Canadian corporation. Domestic hybrid mismatch rules (ss. 18.2, 18.4, 12.6–12.7 of the Income Tax Act) apply mandatorily to deny deduction for dual-consistency amounts or where a branch mismatch exists, subject to treaty overriding provisions. Available elections relevant to tax classification include election under subsection 256(1) regarding associated corporations for loss utilization, a partnership-versus-corporation status is not elective, and GST/HST grouping election under s. 156 ETA is available for corporations resident in Canada.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 in taxable supplies over any single calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters; providers of e-commerce / digital services to Canadian consumers are also required to register under the GST/HST if they have revenue from Canadian supplies exceeding CAD 30,000, with special simplified registration for non-resident digital services providers.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

For a Canadian-registered corporation supplying public web scraping / proxy API gateway services to non-residents, the supply of intangible personal property to a non-resident person is zero-rated under Part V, Schedule VI, Excise Tax Act, unless (i) the non-resident is registered for GST/HST and receives the supply through a Canadian establishment, (ii) the supply relates to real property situated in Canada or tangible personal property situated in Canada, or (iii) the supply is a prescribed supply such as a service performed on tangible property physically located in Canada. For exported goods, zero-rating applies where the goods are exported directly from Canada by the supplier, the purchaser or a facilitating carrier and documentary evidence of exportation is retained. Automated or offshore API access to Canadian subscribers may also be zero-rated as a supply of

  • Canada Customs Export Declaration (B13A) where goods are shipped in commercial quantities or export permits are required
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill or equivalent transport document showing physical export from Canada
  • Commercial invoice, service contract or master agreement establishing the supply of digital services to the non-resident customer
  • Evidence of payment, including bank transfer / inbound remittance confirmation showing settlement in CAD or agreed foreign currency
  • Proof of non-resident status of the customer (e.g., certificate of incorporation, tax registration evidence, registered business address outside Canada), provisional source document

No real-time B2B e-invoicing or fiscal clearance infrastructure is mandatory in Canada. CRA does not require digital stamping or API pre-clearance of invoices. Invoices must show the supplier's BN and GST/HST registration number, taxable amount, tax rate, and total amount if claiming input tax credits. Pan-Canadian adoption of the PEPPOL / X-Border e-invoice standard is being developed on a voluntary basis for B2B, while CRA accepts EDI, XML, PDF or paper records that meet informational requirements. For Ontario, no provincial fiscal clearance or digital stamping applies to the supply of web scraping / data pipeline API services.

CRA may reassess GST/HST net tax within four years after the end of the reporting period; if non-compliance is attributable to misrepresentation, fault, neglect or fraud, reassessments are not statute-barred. Denial of zero-rating results in retroactive reassessment at 13% HST (Ontario component 8%, federal 5%) with arrears interest. Penalties may include significant VAT/general response penalties under s. 280 ETA for failing to register, gross negligence penalty under s. 285 ETA of 25% of the net tax, and criminal penalties for intentional evasion up to CAD 500,000 plus imprisonment. Exporters claiming zero-rating for services without sufficient documentary support or deliberately misrepresenting offshore consumer status are subject to aggressive tax avoidance / GAAR challenges, transfer-pricing penalties, and denial of input tax credits with a reclassification from sale to Canadian use or resale.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada operates a fully floating exchange-rate regime with no general exchange-control statute. Current-account transactions and capital-account flows are freely convertible; there is no central-bank authorization requirement, quantitative limit, or ring-fencing for inbound FDI, trade settlements, or profit repatriation. Non-resident profit remittances are unrestricted from an FX perspective but remain subject to Part XIII withholding tax (statutory rate 25%, treaty-reduced, e.g., to 15% or 5% under the Canada–US Tax Treaty) and to CRS/FATCA account reporting. CAD/USD conversion is executed at negotiated market rates with Canadian chartered banks or approved dealing institutions.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canada imposes no statutory surrender timeline, mandatory conversion ratio, or repatriation obligation for export receipts. Exporters and SaaS/data-API vendors may retain foreign currency in offshore accounts or Canadian domiciliary foreign-currency accounts; conversion into CAD is entirely voluntary at market rates. Reporting obligations are limited to FINTRAC large-transaction reports (CAD 10,000 or more) and Statistics Canada direct-investment surveys above statutory thresholds.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada); market operations and currency issuance under the Bank of Canada Act; prudential supervision of banks by the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI); AML/ATF supervision by the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC); wholesale settlement oversight via Payments Canada (Lynx and Real-Time Rail).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium as a software/payment-facilitation layer; Low as the primary treasury account holder. A non-resident fintech or electronic money institution (EMI) cannot lawfully offer deposit-taking or clearing-member settlement services in Canada unless authorized under the Bank Act or operating through a Canadian chartered bank partnership. An offshore EMI must register with FINTRAC as a money services business (MSB) and, in Ontario, obtain an MSB licence from FSRA under the Money Services Business Licensing Act, 2019. USDC-related payment activity requires a FINTRAC-registered VASP/trader-in-crypto designation to remain compliant.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined general corporate income tax rate for an Ontario corporation: 15% federal and 11.5% Ontario. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally access a combined 12.2% small-business rate on eligible active business income up to the applicable CAD 500,000 business limit, subject to federal and Ontario eligibility rules, associated-corporation allocation, and passive-income grind rules.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding on taxable dividends paid or credited to a non-resident, subject to an applicable tax treaty. The Canada-Ontario corporation must generally remit the withholding by the 15th day of the month following the month of payment or credit and file Form T5 for Canadian-resident recipients or Form NR4 for non-resident recipients. Treaty rates commonly reduce dividends to 15%; a 5% rate may apply where the treaty conditions for a qualifying corporate parent are satisfied. Under the Canada-United States treaty, the principal reduced rates are generally 5% for a qualifying corporate shareholder meeting the ownership conditions and 15% in other eligible cases.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The T2 return is due within six months after the end of each taxation year. Any unpaid balance is generally due two months after year-end. A qualifying Canadian-controlled private corporation may generally pay the balance within three months after year-end if the statutory conditions are satisfied. Tax installments are due on the applicable monthly or quarterly installment dates.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Canada has no general monetary threshold below which contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation is automatically unnecessary. Section 247 of the Income Tax Act requires contemporaneous documentation for non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents, with specified content and timing requirements. Form T106 reporting generally applies when the aggregate amount of reportable non-arm's-length transactions with non-residents exceeds CAD 1,000,000, subject to the statutory rules and exclusions. A Master File is not generally a standalone Canadian filing requirement for every corporation. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to an ultimate parent or surrogate parent resident in Canada that is part of an MNE group meeting the approximately EUR 750 million consolidated-revenue threshold, with a generally 12-month post-year-end filing deadline.

Economic substance scope: Canada has no broad standalone economic-substance regulation equivalent to the United Kingdom or United Arab Emirates ESR regimes. Economic substance remains relevant through the general anti-avoidance rule, transfer-pricing rules, treaty principal-purpose or anti-abuse provisions, beneficial-ownership analysis, sham and series-of-transactions doctrines, and characterization of arrangements according to their commercial and legal substance. The corporation should maintain Canadian decision-making, personnel or outsourced operational capability, technical controls, contracts, risk management, customer records, source-data governance, cybersecurity controls, and evidence that pricing reflects functions performed, assets used, and risks assumed. A Canadian corporation that merely invoices foreign customers while all development, control, contracting, and risk functions occur offshore faces permanent-establishment, transfer-pricing, treaty-benefit, and GAAR exposure.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Reporting

Statutory sanction: Under the Income Tax Act, conviction for tax evasion may result in a fine generally ranging from 50% to 200% of the tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to five years. Civil gross-negligence penalties are generally 50% of the understated tax or overstated credit, in addition to tax, interest, and reassessment. Criminal Code fraud provisions may also apply to fraudulent conduct.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face criminal prosecution, personal fines, and imprisonment where they participated in, authorized, or acquiesced in the conduct. The corporate veil is not automatically pierced, but personal liability can arise under specific statutes, for knowing participation, fraud, dishonest conduct, or director-liability provisions. A court may impose restitution or a probation order; travel restrictions are fact-specific and not an automatic tax penalty.
Failure to Remit Trust Amounts

Statutory sanction: The corporation may owe the unremitted amount, interest, and penalties. Directors may be assessed personally under section 227.1 of the Income Tax Act for source deductions and under section 323 of the Excise Tax Act for GST/HST, subject to statutory limitation and due-diligence defenses. Failure to deduct or remit Part XIII tax may attract payer liability, penalties, and interest.

Director personal liability: Personal assessments may apply to directors who were directors when the corporation was required to remit, generally subject to a two-year statutory limitation after cessation of directorship and an available due-diligence defense. Bankruptcy or dissolution of the corporation does not automatically eliminate director liability. Imprisonment is generally associated with separate wilful criminal conduct, not ordinary assessed director liability.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: The CRA may reassess under section 247, deny or adjust deductions, impute income, recharacterize transactions, and apply a transfer-pricing penalty generally equal to 10% of the adjustment where the statutory conditions are met and reasonable efforts were not made. Interest and withholding-tax consequences may follow.

Director personal liability: The primary assessment is against the corporation. Directors may incur personal exposure for knowingly participating in false records, tax evasion, fraud, trust liabilities, or other statutory offences. Ordinary corporate transfer-pricing exposure does not by itself pierce the corporate veil.
Treaty Abuse and Beneficial-Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: The reduced treaty rate may be denied, resulting in reassessment of withholding to the 25% domestic rate, interest, penalties, and possible GAAR or treaty anti-abuse application. False certifications may create civil or criminal exposure.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be personally investigated or prosecuted for knowingly false certifications, fraud, or wilful tax evasion. Corporate treaty denial alone does not automatically create personal tax liability.
Shell Company and Substance Deficiency

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include denial of deductions, transfer-pricing adjustments, treaty-benefit denial, permanent-establishment findings, GAAR reassessment, payroll or GST/HST assessments, and penalties and interest. There is no general Canadian shell-company penalty merely for having limited operations, but sham or abusive arrangements may trigger statutory sanctions.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure where they knowingly participate in sham transactions, false records, tax evasion, or trust-remittance failures. Civil veil piercing is exceptional and requires statutory or equitable grounds; imprisonment is not an automatic consequence of low substance.
Cryptocurrency and Payment-Rail Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Income tax, GST/HST, payroll, information-return, interest, and gross-negligence consequences may apply. Where the business exchanges, transfers, or deals in virtual currency for clients or performs qualifying payment services, FINTRAC registration and reporting obligations may apply. Administrative penalties for applicable AML violations can be substantial, and serious criminal offences may attract imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be personally liable for knowing participation in tax evasion, AML offences, fraud, or trust-remittance failures. FINTRAC monetary penalties are generally imposed on the reporting entity, while criminal responsibility depends on individual conduct and proof.
Anti-Money-Laundering and Sanctions

Statutory sanction: If the entity is a reporting entity under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, failures may result in administrative monetary penalties, compliance orders, publication of violations, registration consequences, and criminal prosecution for laundering, terrorist financing, or sanctions-related conduct. Canadian economic sanctions under the Special Economic Measures Act and related legislation may impose asset-freezing, dealing prohibitions, fines, or imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Individuals who direct, authorize, facilitate, or knowingly participate may face prosecution, fines, imprisonment, asset seizure, and restrictions on dealing with sanctioned persons. Travel bans are not a routine statutory consequence but may arise through criminal bail, probation, arrest, or immigration measures.
Failure to File Information Returns

Statutory sanction: Separate fixed or escalating penalties may apply depending on the form, duration, number of failures, and whether the omission was deliberate. The CRA may also reassess income, deny deductions, impose withholding liability, and apply interest or gross-negligence penalties.

Director personal liability: Personal liability is generally not automatic for a corporate information-return penalty. Directors may nevertheless face prosecution or personal assessments where the failure forms part of wilful tax evasion, fraud, false statements, or trust-remittance misconduct.
Data-Protection, Unauthorized Scraping, and Unlawful Commercial Conduct

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include privacy investigations, compliance orders, damages, contractual claims, injunctions, breach-notification obligations, administrative penalties under applicable federal or provincial privacy legislation, and Criminal Code offences for unauthorized computer access or fraud. Tax compliance does not legalize unauthorized data acquisition.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal criminal or civil exposure for directing or knowingly participating in unauthorized access, fraud, or unlawful data use. Corporate separateness generally remains effective for ordinary contractual liability, but it does not protect individuals from their own torts or crimes.
Corporate Records and Beneficial-Ownership Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: The corporation may face corporate-law penalties, inability to maintain good standing, director or officer offences, fines, investigative orders, and tax or AML information failures. Concealment linked to tax evasion, money laundering, or fraud may produce substantially higher criminal sanctions.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be fined or prosecuted for statutory breaches or knowingly false corporate records. Travel bans or imprisonment are not routine for an isolated records defect but may arise from related criminal proceedings.
