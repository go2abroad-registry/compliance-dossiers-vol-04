Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) for federal GST/HST and income tax administration; Revenu Québec for Quebec provincial QST and corporate income tax administration. Portals: CRA 'My Business Account' at canada.ca and Revenu Québec 'Clic Revenu' at revenuquebec.ca.

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity carrying on business in Canada must first obtain a Canadian federal Business Number (BN) through CRA, generally by filing Form RC1 (Request for a Business Number) or through the CRA online registration service. Where taxable Canadian supplies exceed the small supplier threshold (CAD 30,000), the entity must register for GST/HST under Part IX of the Excise Tax Act. For Quebec, the entity must separately register for QST with Revenu Québec using the same BN prefix and a QST account, typically through the Registraire d'entreprises or Form LM-1/VF. The corporation should also file a federal corporate income tax account (T2) and, if carrying on business in Quebec, a Quebec provincial corporate tax account (CO-17). A non-resident corporation must also complete Form RC258 or applicable waiver filings to establish its Canadian reporting obligations.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada does not have a check-the-box regime comparable to the United States. A Canadian-incorporated Business Corporation is automatically a taxpayer resident in Canada and is taxed at entity level on worldwide income. Available statutory elections include: (i) the Section 216 Income Tax Act election for a non-resident to file a Canadian return on net rental income; (ii) the Section 219 election for a non-resident corporation regarding taxable Canadian earnings; (iii) the GST/HST 'closely related corporation' election under Section 156 Excise Tax Act, permitting certain intercompany supplies to be deemed for nil consideration; and (iv) the QST-related elections under Quebec's Taxation Act and QST legislation harmonized with the Excise Tax Act. No hybrid mismatch elections are available; Canada and Quebec apply specific anti-hybrid rules.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14.975%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (Canadian dollars) in worldwide taxable supplies in a calendar quarter or over four consecutive calendar quarters for GST/HST; the same CAD 30,000 threshold applies to taxable supplies made in Quebec for QST registration, with QST applying on Quebec supplies at 9.975% on the selling price excluding GST. Digital magazine subscriptions and sponsored media placement revenues are generally taxable supplies subject to registration once the threshold is exceeded, subject to specific place-of-supply rules.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A registered Canadian/Quebec supplier may zero-rate the supply of digital magazine subscriptions and online sponsored media placement services when the supply is made to a non-resident who is not registered for GST/HST/QST and who is not present in Canada at the time the supply is made, and when the intangible property or service is not used in Canada. For digital publications and e-media, the supplier must establish that the recipient's ordinary place of abode or business is outside Canada and that the supply is not related to Canadian real property or tangible personal property located in Canada at the time of supply. The zero-rating applies to intangible personal property and services supplied to non-residents under Schedule VI, Part V and Part X, Excise Tax Act, and to corresponding QST provisions in the Quebec Taxation Act. Proof of non-resident status must be retained in the taxpayer's books, including IP address verification, billing address, subscriber registration data, contract terms, and payment settlement records.

  • Customs Export Declaration
  • Bill of Lading
  • Inward Remittance Certificate
  • Commercial invoice with explicit reference to zero-rated supply
  • Non-resident subscriber or advertiser registration and address records
  • Digital delivery logs and IP address verification for online transmissions
  • Contract or terms of service evidencing supply to a non-resident recipient

Canada and Quebec do not currently mandate real-time electronic invoicing, fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or government API validation for GST/QST invoices. However, registrants must retain electronic records and invoices in a readable format, and GST/HST returns must be filed electronically through CRA's GST/HST NETFILE when gross revenue exceeds CAD 1.5 million. Revenu Québec requires mandatory electronic filing of QST returns for large businesses and for those required to remit through the EFT system. Standard business-to-business invoicing must contain the registrant's BN/GST/HST registration number and, where applicable, the QST registration number, but no real-time clearance or approval is required.

CRA and Revenu Québec may reassess a zero-rated export claim if documentary evidence is incomplete, contradictory, or unavailable. Misclassification of domestic taxable supply as zero-rated export attracts reassessment of unpaid GST/HST/QST, interest, and penalties. Gross negligence penalties under Section 285 Excise Tax Act and Section 1051 Quebec Taxation Act may be up to 200% of the tax sought, and criminal tax evasion penalties include fines up to CAD 1 million or 200% of the tax evaded and imprisonment for up to five years. Historical clawback for unrepatriated foreign currency proceeds, failure to meet the 180-day inward remittance requirement, or failure to substantiate the non-resident status of the recipient may result in full reversal of the zero-rating, loss of input tax credits, and additional penalties for false statements or omissions under Sections 327 and 328 Excise Tax Act and corresponding Quebec provisions.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Canadian dollar is freely convertible for current- and capital-account transactions; Canada has no general capital controls or requirement for prior central-bank approval of ordinary cross-border investment, borrowing, dividends, or loan repayments. Banks must apply sanctions, anti-money-laundering, beneficial-ownership, and transaction-monitoring rules. A Canadian corporation may remit dividends or other lawful payments abroad subject to corporate-law solvency requirements, board authorization, contractual restrictions, and applicable Canadian withholding tax. Treaty relief may reduce withholding on qualifying payments when beneficial-ownership and residence documentation is provided. Interest, royalties, service fees, and related-party payments require appropriate legal, transfer-pricing, and tax support; tax filings and withholding obligations may apply. Quebec incorporation does not create a distinct currency-control regime.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Canada has no general statutory deadline requiring exporters to repatriate export proceeds and no mandatory foreign-currency conversion or surrender ratio. Export receipts may be retained in foreign-currency accounts or converted at the account holder's discretion, subject to contractual, sanctions, tax, and financial-reporting obligations. Canadian financial institutions may request invoices, contracts, shipping evidence, and payment-purpose information for compliance.

Central bank supervision: The Bank of Canada is Canada's central bank and oversees monetary policy and key payment-system functions. The Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions supervises federally regulated banks. The Autorité des marchés financiers regulates financial services within Quebec, including relevant provincial financial-market activities. Canada has no general foreign-exchange administration imposing exchange controls.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Medium for payment collection or payouts and Low as a substitute for a Canadian corporate bank account. Canadian payment service providers must comply with applicable federal retail-payment supervision requirements where in scope, and money-services businesses may require FINTRAC registration. An offshore electronic-money institution is not automatically authorized to provide regulated services in Canada or to hold deposits. Confirm the provider's Canadian permissions, safeguarding arrangements, client-fund protections, CAD settlement capability, and correspondent-bank access. Stripe and Google AdSense payouts are subject to provider eligibility, account-name matching, tax documentation, and supported payout methods; neither replaces the corporation's own banking and tax-compliance obligations.

Mandatory Audit Threshold Mandatory Audit for All Entities

Turnover: No asset or revenue threshold applies at the private-company level. A statutory audit is mandatory when: (i) the corporation is a reporting issuer or distributes securities to the public; (ii) shareholders holding at least 10% of votes (QBCA) or 5% (CBCA) validly demand an audit; or (iii) shareholders do not unanimously waive the auditor appointment at the annual meeting. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1,000,000 for local file; CAD 50,000,000 consolidated group revenue for master file

Economic substance scope: Canada applies general anti-avoidance rules (GAAR) and substance over form principles; no mandatory Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to EU ESR

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction with fines up to CAD 1,000,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years

Director personal liability: Directors personally liable for penalties, restitution, and potential criminal charges
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Adjustments to income, penalties up to 10% of transaction value, and interest

Director personal liability: Potential personal liability for negligence or willful misconduct
Failure to File or Late Filing

Statutory sanction: Penalty of 5% per month of unpaid tax, up to 12 months, plus interest

Director personal liability: Directors can face personal liability for corporate tax debts
Shell Company and Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: GAAR application, recharacterization of transactions, and penalties

Director personal liability: Piercing the corporate veil, civil claims, and potential criminal sanctions
