E-government filing portal

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box election exists in Canadian federal law; every CBCA or OBCA business corporation is a separate taxpayer subject to ITA Part I at entity level. Available statutory elections include: (i) GST/HST Quick Method for qualifying small suppliers and simplified input tax credit (ITC) accounting; (ii) ITA s. 85(1) tax-deferred rollover election for transferring property into the corporation; (iii) ITA s. 216 election for non-resident owners of Canadian rental real property to compute net-basis tax; (iv) ITA s. 216.1 election for certain non-residents carrying on business in Canada; (v) treaty-based elections and permanent establishment determinations under bilateral income tax conventions which can modify the Canadian filing footprint. Hybrid mismatch outcomes are addressed by specific legislative allocation rules; no elective classification regime is available.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 (small supplier threshold measured over four consecutive calendar quarters of taxable supplies made in Canada; non-resident corporations compute this threshold solely on Canadian-source taxable supplies; mandatory GST/HST registration applies once exceeded).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available under ETA Schedule VI, Parts V and VI when: (1) POD apparel goods are actually exported from Canada, either by the supplier, a common carrier, or a consignee acting on behalf of the supplier, and the supplier maintains contemporaneous evidence of physical export; or (2) services are supplied to a non-resident for consumption wholly outside Canada and the place-of-supply rules confirm an offshore destination. The supplier must document that consideration was paid from foreign sources. Supplies fulfilled and delivered to Canadian-resident consumers attract the full 13% HST and cannot be zero-rated.

  • B13A Export Declaration and CBSA Export Reporting System (CES) confirmation, required for direct export shipments exceeding CAD 2,000; low-value POD international parcels are formally exempt but must retain courier shipment logs
  • Commercial Invoice with HS classification (6109/6110 apparel categories), unit value, date of export, and declared Canadian origin
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill or courier consignment note (UPS, FedEx, DHL, Canada Post) evidencing physical exportation from Canada
  • POD production and fulfillment records (print-ready asset files, batch production log, finished-good item number) linking the shipment to the zero-rated sale
  • Payment settlement evidence (Stripe or PayPal payout statements, bank SWIFT/EFT records, foreign-currency conversion receipts) demonstrating foreign-issuer payment card funding or non-resident remittance

Canada does not impose a real-time fiscal clearance or government digital stamping regime. CRA mandates electronic filing of GST/HST returns for registrants whose annual gross revenue exceeds CAD 1.5 million, and all registrants must remit taxes by electronic funds transfer at the prescribed frequency. Every tax invoice must state the supplier's Business Number and GST/HST registration number, invoice date, a unique invoice number, the total consideration and the tax charged. Canada is progressively adopting voluntary PEPPOL-based e-invoicing in public procurement, but this does not affect B2C POD retail. Books, records and source documents must be retained for six years in English or French, in a CRA-acceptable machine-readable format.

CRA applies heightened audit scrutiny to zero-rated export claims. If contemporaneous export documentation is missing, inconsistent or insufficient, the supply is reclassified as taxable at 13% HST, with reassessment interest calculated from the original reporting date. False or careless statements attract a penalty of not less than CAD 250 and up to 25% of the overstated tax amount under the Excise Tax Act, and gross negligence penalties of up to 50% of the understated federal corporate tax under ITA s. 163(2). CRA may extend the ordinary four-year reassessment limitation period to eight years where misrepresentation, negligence or a lack of reasonable documentation is established, allowing retrospective clawback of input tax credits and refunds previously allowed on the export transaction.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains full current- and capital-account convertibility as an IMF Article VIII member since 25 March 1953. There are no exchange controls, no inward or outward currency licenses, and no Central Bank pre-approval for cross-border payments. The Canadian dollar trades in a free float as a liquid G10 currency. Profit remittance hurdles are non-existent at the convertibility layer; however, dividends, interest, royalties and management fees paid to non-residents remain subject to Part XIII withholding tax (statutory 25%, treaty-reduced, e.g., 5% or 15% under the Canada-US treaty depending on shareholding), with NR4 reporting and remittance to the CRA. Non-resident ownership is unrestricted for an Ontario Business Corporation. Mechanical compliance only: cross-border electronic funds transfers of CAD 10,000 or more are reportable to FINTRAC, and foreign property with aggregate cost exceeding CAD 100,000 must be reported on CRA Form T1135.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None applicable. Canada imposes no statutory surrender timeline and no mandatory conversion ratio on export receipts. The Foreign Exchange Control Act was repealed in 1951, and neither the Bank of Canada nor the CRA requires repatriation, conversion, or escrow of export proceeds. Export revenue may be retained in the invoicing currency (typically USD), held in a domestic USD business account, or held offshore/with a FINTRAC-registered EMI, subject to T1135 foreign-property reporting. For GST/HST, exported print-on-demand merchandise is zero-rated under section 7 of Part V of Schedule VI of the Excise Tax Act; the exporter must retain contemporaneous export documentation (commercial invoice, courier airway bill, Canadian customs export declaration form B13A or shipment records) to sustain the zero-rating, although this documentation is not a surrender rule.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada). The Foreign Exchange Control Act was repealed in 1951, so the Bank of Canada exercises no exchange-control licensing or approval authority. Ancillary regulators: Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) for AML/CFT supervision, Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for bank prudential regulation, and Payments Canada for the Lynx LVTS / Real-Time Rail clearing infrastructure.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

26.5% combined federal-provincial (15% federal Part I general corporate rate + 11.5% Ontario general corporate rate for a permanent establishment located in Ontario)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% Part XIII withholding tax on dividends paid by a Canadian resident corporation to non-resident shareholders, subject to reduction under applicable tax treaties. Under the Canada-US DTA, the rate is 5% where a US corporate recipient beneficially owns at least 10% of the voting shares of the Canadian payer, and 15% in most other cases.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

T2 must be filed within 6 months after the corporation's fiscal year-end (e.g., for a December 31 year-end, by June 30 of the following year). The balance-due date is 2 months after fiscal year-end (3 months for a CCPC eligible for the SBD); instalments are due quarterly based on estimated tax.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Due within 6 months of the fiscal year-end; mandatory when the aggregate value of transactions with a non-resident related party exceeds C$1,000,000 in the taxation year.

A non-resident corporation with a permanent establishment in Ontario allocates Ontario taxable income on Schedule 5 using the prescribed PE formula; if there are permanent establishments outside Ontario, the allocation percentage must be prorated among provinces. Late filing penalty under s.162(1) ITA: 5% of the unpaid balance plus 1% per full month of delinquency, maximum 12 months; repeated failure under s.162(2) increases penalties; prescribed daily compound interest accrues on unpaid tax balances. Failure to file Schedule 5 or other required schedules may result in additional penalties; failure to file a T106 transfer-pricing information return triggers C$25-per-day penalties (minimum C$100, maximum C$2,500; C$100 per day, maximum C$12,500, for knowing gross negligence).
Line 580 (Total Balance of Tax Payable) Aggregate federal and Ontario corporate tax payable after all credits and instalments; determines final balance due or refund for the taxation year. Non-resident corporations must add the Part XIV branch tax of 25% on after-tax business profits to the balance-due calculation unless a tax treaty provides a reduced branch tax rate (e.g., Canada-US treaty relief), and must file applicable schedules to certify the treaty rate. Late filing penalty under s.162(1) ITA: 5% of the unpaid balance plus 1% per full month of delinquency, maximum 12 months; repeated failure under s.162(2) increases penalties; prescribed daily compound interest accrues on unpaid tax balances. Failure to file Schedule 5 or other required schedules may result in additional penalties; failure to file a T106 transfer-pricing information return triggers C$25-per-day penalties (minimum C$100, maximum C$2,500; C$100 per day, maximum C$12,500, for knowing gross negligence).
Section A - Identification of the Taxpayer and Non-Resident Related Parties Identifies the Canadian filer, the non-resident related parties, their relationship, and the jurisdictions of residence. The Canadian entity files the T106; the non-resident counterparty does not file but must maintain supporting books, records, and written documentation to corroborate the Canadian entity's transfer prices. Failure to file T106 on time: penalty of C$25 per day (minimum C$100, maximum C$2,500); if filed under circumstances of knowing, false, gross negligence: C$100 per day (minimum C$100, maximum C$12,500). TP reassessment penalties under s.247(8) of 10% of the net upward transfer-pricing adjustment may additionally apply.
Schedule II - Summary of Transactions Classifies transactions by type: purchases, sales, royalties, interest, management fees, or cost-sharing contributions with non-resident related parties. Each transaction type must be identified with a separate line; for a POD operation, common entries include purchasing of blank apparel from related foreign manufacturers, trademark or design royalties paid to a foreign affiliate, and service fees paid to offshore logistics providers. Failure to file T106 on time: penalty of C$25 per day (minimum C$100, maximum C$2,500); if filed under circumstances of knowing, false, gross negligence: C$100 per day (minimum C$100, maximum C$12,500). The Canadian entity must document why the chosen method yields the most reliable arm's length result and provide comparable data from Canadian or foreign markets; non-resident support documentation should be delivered through a formal TP policy file. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: C$1,000,000 aggregate non-arm's length transactions with non-resident related parties in a single taxation year (CRA administrative threshold); below this threshold, contemporaneous documentation is not mandatory, but the arm's length principle and reasonable efforts remain required.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not administer separate Economic Substance Regulations like Gulf jurisdictions; however, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s.245 ITA) voids tax-avoidance transactions lacking commercial or economic substance. For Ontario corporate tax, a permanent establishment and real business presence determine nexus and allocation. Treaty-reduced withholding requires beneficial ownership and substance; a shell Canadian corporation with no employees, premises, or real operational activity will be vulnerable to GAAR, treaty-shopping denial, CCPC-status recharacterization, and s.163.2 false-statement penalties.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Intentional Misreporting

Statutory sanction: Under s.239(1) ITA: fine of 50% to 200% of the amount of tax sought to be evaded and imprisonment for up to 5 years. Civil reassessment with arrears interest plus a gross-negligence penalty of 50% of the understated tax (s.163) may be imposed even in the absence of criminal conviction.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who authorize, participate in, or knowingly direct the false filing are jointly engaged in the offence and may be prosecuted personally; CRA may issue director liability assessments for unremitted amounts, and the issuance of an arrest warrant can trigger CBSA travel restrictions and passport flagging.
Failure to Remit Payroll Deductions and GST/HST

Statutory sanction: Late or non-remittance penalties under the Excise Tax Act and ITA: 10% late-remittance penalty plus prescribed daily compounded interest (currently 10%+ on arrears); director liability assessments may recover the full unremitted amount from directors personally.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for all unremitted payroll source deductions and collected GST/HST under s.227.1 ITA and s.323 ETA without proof of criminal intent, provided the director knew or ought to have known of the failure and did not exercise due diligence; personal assets, bank accounts and cross-border travel are at risk during CRA collection enforcement.
Transfer Pricing Violations with Non-Resident Related Parties

Statutory sanction: s.247(2) ITA revaluation of the taxpayer's price to the arm's length amount; s.247(8) penalty equal to 10% of the net upward adjustment where the taxpayer did not make reasonable efforts; interest accrues from the statutory due date; GAAR may additionally deny any tax benefit.

Director personal liability: TP penalties are primarily corporate, but a director who signs a false T106, produces fabricated comparables, or orchestrates a sham transaction may be assessed personally under s.163.2 for false statements and prosecuted criminally if fraudulent intent is proven.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance for Treaty Benefits

Statutory sanction: CRA may reassess to impose full 25% Part XIII withholding on outbound distributions, deny the SBD and CCPC status, apply GAAR (s.245 ITA) to recharacterize the arrangement, and assess gross-negligence penalties under s.163.2; treaty relief may be denied on a beneficial-ownership or principal-purpose analysis.

Director personal liability: Non-resident controlling shareholders and record directors may be jointly liable for the resulting unpaid Part XIII tax; where criminal charges follow a sham arrangement, court-imposed travel restrictions list the individual as an international enforcement target.
Anti-Money Laundering (AML) Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act, administrative monetary penalties for corporations can reach C$500,000 per violation (higher tiers for serious, deliberate, or repeated non-compliance); criminal fines and imprisonment for up to 5 years apply for obstructing FINTRAC audits or knowingly making false statements.

Director personal liability: Officers, directors and senior managers who fail to take reasonable steps to implement AML controls may be subject to individual penalties of up to C$250,000 per violation; willful blindness or intentional evasion of FINTRAC obligations can result in personal criminal exposure and associated travel restrictions.
