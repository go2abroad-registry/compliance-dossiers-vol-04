Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

Minimum share capital of CHF 100,000 (approximately USD 112,000), of which at least 20% of the nominal capital and in all cases no less than CHF 50,000 must be fully paid up before registration. The paid-up funds must be deposited with a FINMA-supervised bank in Switzerland and blocked until the Commercial Register confirms the company's legal personality, evidenced by a bank capital-block confirmation letter (Sperrbestätigung).

Funding & unlock path: Pre-occupancy fire safety inspection and certificate issued by the Cantonal Fire Police (Feuerpolizei) of Zug, including emergency exit and extinguisher certification; structural load and workplace occupancy certificate issued by the municipal building inspectorate under the Cantonal Building Code; occupational safety and ergonomics clearance for screen-based workplaces registered with Suva and the cantonal labour inspectorate in accordance with Ordinance 3 of the Labour Act (SR 832.311); no civil-defense shelter construction or retrofitting obligation is triggered for office-only leasehold premises.

Paid-in capital rule: Minimum share capital of CHF 100,000 (approximately USD 112,000), of which at least 20% of the nominal capital and in all cases no less than CHF 50,000 must be fully paid up before registration. The paid-up funds must be deposited with a FINMA-supervised bank in Switzerland and blocked until the Commercial Register confirms the company's legal personality, evidenced by a bank capital-block confirmation letter (Sperrbestätigung).

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Swiss Federal Tax Administration (Eidgenössische Steuerverwaltung – ESTV / Administration fédérale des contributions – AFC), with exclusive administration of Swiss VAT, and shared administration of direct federal, cantonal, and communal corporate taxes through the cantonal tax authorities (e.g., the Tax Administration of the Canton of Zug for the Zug crypto valley). Primary federal portal: www.estv.admin.ch; electronic filing via the ESTV e-services gateway and the VAT registration desk.

E-government filing portal

Every Swiss Aktiengesellschaft automatically receives the statutory business identification number (UID / IDE / IDI) upon Commercial Register entry under Art. 4 of the Federal Act on Business Identification Numbers (UIDG), in the format CHE-XXX.XXX.XXX; this UID functions as the statutory TIN for all federal, cantonal, and municipal tax purposes and becomes the VAT identifier upon registration for Swiss VAT, with the suffix 'MWST / TVA / IVA' appended. VAT registration is filed electronically with the ESTV once taxable turnover from Swiss-taxable supplies exceeds CHF 100,000 per annum. A foreign entity not yet registered in Switzerland that acquires a fixed place of business (permanent establishment) or otherwise triggers Swiss VAT obligations must procure a UID through the competent cantonal Commercial Register or via the ESTV non-established taxpayer desk, appoint a Swiss-based fiscal representative/agent (Art. 67 Swiss VAT Act), file certified constitutional documents, a board power of attorney, and proof of the foreign legal form, and then complete the simplified VAT registration. Suppliers of digital services to Swiss consumers may register under the non-established simplified online procedure; once Swiss consumer (B2C) turnover exceeds CHF 100,000, registration is mandatory. The UID must appear on all invoices, customs declarations, and tax correspondence.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — the AG is a juridical person taxed separately from its shareholders on its worldwide profits (federal corporate income tax at 8.5% flat on after-tax profit, plus cantonal and communal profit tax and capital tax). No pass-through or partnership transparency results from the Commercial Register legal form; territorial carve-outs are achieved only through the participation exemption (Beteiligungsabzug), the domestic exemption for foreign permanent establishments and foreign immovable property, and treaty tie-breaker rules. The entity is subject to the 35% Swiss anticipatory withholding tax on dividend distributions, with recovery or credit available to qualifying shareholders.

Available election forms

Switzerland does not provide a US-style 'check-the-box' election or a hybrid mismatch entity election; tax classification follows the statutory Commercial Register legal form. Available statutory elections and administrative toggles include: (i) Swiss VAT group election (organschaftliche Gruppe / groupe pour la TVA, Art. 11 Swiss VAT Act), consolidating multiple Swiss entities and permanent establishments into a single VAT taxpayer for group-level input-tax optimization; (ii) the participation-deduction election claimed annually in the corporate tax return, exempting qualifying dividend and capital-gain income from shareholdings of at least 10% or CHF 1 million market value; (iii) optional cantonal group-tax status (Steuertransparente Gruppenbesteuerung) under the 2020 Federal Tax Reform and AHV Financing Act (STAF / TRAF), elected for tax-transparent treatment of wholly owned Swiss group members; (iv) real-estate valuation and step-up elections on disposal or deemed liquidation at cantonal level; and (v) the partial taxation procedure for qualifying individual shareholders of Swiss dividend income. Hybrid financing arrangements remain subject to Swiss anti-abuse and withholding-tax rules without elective hybrid classification.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

8.1%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CHF 100,000 of worldwide turnover from taxable supplies of goods and services with a Swiss place of supply per accounting year, as set out in Art. 10 para. 1 Swiss VAT Act. Foreign digital-service suppliers (LegalTech SaaS, e-signature, cloud automation) whose B2C supplies to Swiss private consumers exceed CHF 100,000 must register under the mandatory non-established supplier procedure; B2B supplies from abroad are subject to the Swiss reverse-charge mechanism and do not alone trigger registration.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

For goods, the 0% export rate (exemption with input-tax deduction) applies under Art. 23 para. 1(1) Swiss VAT Act when the supplier exports the goods itself or for its own account to a foreign country or customs-bonded warehouse and proves physical removal from Switzerland; ancillary transport, handling, loading, and commission services directly related to the exported goods are zero-rated when billed to the foreign principal. For LegalTech SaaS cross-border services (ISIC 6201), Art. 8 place-of-supply rules prevail: B2B digital services delivered to a recipient entrepreneur established abroad are placed abroad and fall outside Swiss VAT scope (no zero-rating invoice is required), and B2C digital services to non-resident consumers likewise fall outside Swiss VAT; such foreign-scope supplies do not count toward the CHF 100,000 registration threshold. Mixed supplies of hardware or physical documentation must be supported by full customs proof to capture the 0% rate.

  • Official Swiss customs export declaration — the Ausfuhrbegleitpapier / document de douane d'exportation, or the electronic e-dec export record bearing a Swiss Customs Movement Reference Number (MRN)
  • Bill of Lading, CMR road consignment note, air waybill, or postal dispatch receipt evidencing physical exit of the goods from Switzerland to a foreign country or customs-bonded area
  • Commercial invoice and packing list addressed to the foreign consignee stating contract terms, Incoterms, and export delivery date
  • Bank statement or SWIFT/payment-rail confirmation evidencing inward remittance in the invoiced currency, with an Inward Remittance Certificate issued by the Swiss bank retained in the export substantiation file

Switzerland does not operate a real-time fiscal clearance, API e-invoicing clearance, or digital stamping mandate. E-invoicing is voluntary and legally equivalent to paper invoices when the recipient consents and the content satisfies the minimum statutory invoice elements of Art. 34 Swiss VAT Act. Since 30 June 2020, the Swiss QR-bill (QR-Rechnung) is mandatory for Swiss-issued invoices accompanied by a payment slip, embedding a Swiss QR Code that standardizes creditor, debtor, and payment data for banking rails; this is operationally relevant for LegalTech document-automation and contract-invoicing platforms. eBill and Peppol-based exchange are market standards but remain unassociated with any governmental clearance obligation.

Swiss VAT audits and ESTV practice require the exporter to maintain conclusive books, contracts, and transport records proving that goods physically left Switzerland for a foreign destination; incomplete or inconsistent documentation triggers retrospective reclassification of the supply as taxable at the 8.1% standard rate, reversal of input-tax deductions, and late-payment interest. The general statutory limitation for VAT claims is five years after the end of the calendar year in which the claim arose (Art. 82 Swiss VAT Act), with limitations suspended or extended where deliberate tax evasion is established. Penalties include: fraudulent VAT evasion under Art. 96 Swiss VAT Act with a fine of up to four times the evaded tax; negligent violation under Art. 97 Swiss VAT Act with a fine of up to CHF 8,000; criminal fraud or forgery under Art. 146 and Art. 251 of the Swiss Criminal Code with custodial sentences of up to five years; and personal penal and civil liability for board members and the Swiss fiscal representative.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Switzerland maintains unrestricted convertibility for both current-account and capital-account transactions under IMF Article VIII obligations. There is no exchange-control legislation, no prior approval requirement for profit repatriation, dividend remittances, or foreign direct investment liquidation. However, outbound dividend distributions trigger ordinary Swiss withholding tax of 35% unless reduced by treaty, and corporate law requires an audited annual financial statement and a shareholder resolution before remittance. For capital account flows, neither the SNB nor the CFI authority imposes notification thresholds; banks independently apply contractual source-of-funds verification under FINMA circulars.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No Swiss statutory provision requires residents to surrender export receipts or repatriate foreign currency to the SNB. Swiss exporters and service providers holding a Swiss AG account may retain proceeds in foreign currency indefinitely and may freely convert to CHF at onshore market rates without mandatory timeline or conversion ratio. The SNB only collects statistical balance-of-payments data, and banks report aggregate transactions for monetary statistics but exercise no administrative control over the currency composition of corporate cash.

Central bank supervision: Swiss National Bank (SNB), Schweizerische Nationalbank, in conjunction with the Federal Department of Finance (FDF) and the Swiss Financial Market Supervisory Authority (FINMA) for prudential enforcement; Switzerland operates a free-floating exchange rate regime without exchange controls.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low direct Feasibility for offshore digital e-money institutions (EMIs) and virtual account providers. A foreign EMI without a FINMA licence does not have a statutory passport into Switzerland. For a CH AG, using an offshore EMI is acceptable only as a sub-agent of a Swiss-regulated bank or treasury software layer, not as the primary transactional account. Alternatively, the Swiss EMI/fintech category ('FinTech licence') permits limited public deposit-taking up to CHF 100 million without interest-bearing current accounts, subject to dedicated Swiss licensing and AML compliance; such an entity should be considered only if its banking back-end is a FINMA-regulated bank. Overall viability rating: Medium only with a Swiss EMI licence, otherwise Low.

Inbound equity and quasi-equity capital for the CH AG must be cleared through the Swiss banking channel. Standard protocol: (i) shareholder resolution or board resolution authorising a capital increase and each contribution; (ii) instructions requiring the originating bank to use a clear SWIFT MT103 with a purpose code that corresponds to an inward direct investment (e.g., 'Credit to share capital account', purpose code under BOP Direct Investment or 'Capital Contribution'); (iii) use of a Swiss receivables account and a notarial record for the capital increase at the Commercial Register; (iv) for intra-group debt, a cross-border back-to-back term sheet with correct interest rate and documentation to avoid recharacterisation as equity; (v) no SNB approval is required, but Swiss banks must secure documentary evidence of the source of funds and the reason for the payment under anti-money-laundering due diligence. Alternatively, the Swiss EMI/fintech category ('FinTech licence') permits limited public deposit-taking up to CHF 100 million without interest-bearing current accounts, subject to dedicated Swiss licensing and AML compliance; such an entity should be considered only if its banking back-end is a FINMA-regulated bank. Overall viability rating: Medium only with a Swiss EMI licence, otherwise Low.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

8.5% Swiss federal direct tax on net profit after tax (effective pre-tax equivalent 7.83%); combined federal plus cantonal and communal effective rate ranges from approximately 11.9% to 21.0% depending on the Canton of residence (e.g., Zug ~11.9%, Zurich ~19.7%).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

35% Swiss federal withholding tax (Verrechnungssteuer) on dividend distributions, deemed profit distributions (hidden equity distributions), and interest on bond-like instruments; no domestic exemption applies to outbound distributions to foreign shareholders.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Switzerland has no codified CHF documentation threshold in statute; however, the ESTV and cantonal tax administrations expect OECD-aligned contemporaneous documentation (Master File and Local File) for any material related-party transaction. Administrative practice triggers mandatory file preparation where intercompany transactions exceed approximately CHF 1 million per transaction category, or where group size approaches the EU-style EUR 750 million consolidated-revenue benchmark; absent or defective documentation shifts the burden of proof to the taxpayer and exposes the company to estimated assessments with penalty interest.

Economic substance scope: Switzerland does not apply UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations, but economic substance is decisive across four axes: (i) entitlement to treaty benefits under the MLI Principal Purpose Test; (ii) Swiss corporate law (Art. 620 et seq. CO) requiring real administration, effective board management, a registered office, and a Swiss banking relationship; (iii) the Swiss transparency register requiring disclosure of all beneficial owners, preventing shell and bearer-like structures; and (iv) substance-over-form re-characterisation of sham vehicles in anti-abuse rulings. A LegalTech SaaS AG must maintain at least one Swiss-resident effective director, retain qualified personnel performing core functions, keep board minutes evidencing Swiss decision-making, hold a Swiss bank account, and document commercial substance locally.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (Steuerhinterziehung)

Statutory sanction: Administrative surcharge of up to 200% of the evaded federal tax (Art. 175 DBG), up to 300% in several cantons, plus default interest at 4% p.a., publication of final sanctions, and, in aggravated cases of tax fraud, criminal fines of up to CHF 250,000.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false returns are jointly and severally liable for the surcharge and interest; personal assets may be attached, travel restrictions and passport seizures may be imposed, and aggravated fraud carries imprisonment of up to three years (Art. 186 DBG); de facto directors cannot shield behind the corporate shell.
Withholding-Tax Fraud (Verrechnungssteuerbetrug)

Statutory sanction: On-demand recovery of the full 35% withholding tax amount plus penalty interest of 5% p.a.; criminal sanctions under Art. 63 VStG with fines of up to three times the evaded tax and confiscation of the undeclared funds.

Director personal liability: Representative liability under Art. 12(3) VStG renders the board personally liable for unpaid withholding tax; each director faces criminal investigation as an accomplice, attachment orders on personal bank accounts, and disqualification from board mandates.
Transfer-Pricing Adjustments and Hidden Profit Distributions

Statutory sanction: Tax assessment on adjusted income, penalty interest, estimation surcharge of approximately 20%, and re-characterisation of the excess as a hidden dividend distribution triggering full 35% WHT liability on the Swiss entity.

Director personal liability: Directors approving non-arm's-length contracts in board minutes are jointly liable for the resulting WHT shortfall and revised corporate tax; deliberate structuring that supports evasion attracts the evasion surcharge and parallel criminal sanctions against the responsible officers.
Shell Company / Sham Structure

Statutory sanction: Disregard of the legal entity for tax purposes, denial of treaty benefits, forced liquidation or bankruptcy declaration, blocking of refund claims, and administrative dissolution proceedings by the commercial register authority.

Director personal liability: Under Swiss veil-piercing case law and Art. 754 CO, directors are personally liable to the company's creditors for damages; personal exposure includes social-security arrears (AHV/ALV/UV), unpaid VAT and WHT, and, in insolvency, personal bankruptcy, travel bans, and criminal proceedings for culpable bankruptcy under the Swiss Criminal Code.
Money Laundering & Beneficial-Ownership Reporting

Statutory sanction: Administrative fines of up to CHF 100,000 for transparency-register violations; money laundering under Art. 305bis CP is punishable by up to three years' imprisonment, or five years in qualified cases; seizure and confiscation of proceeds; regulatory de-banking and exclusion from payment rails such as Stripe and card processors.

Director personal liability: Directors acting as AML officers face personal criminal conviction with imprisonment, prohibition from serving as board members, and civil liability to defrauded counterparties; compliance failures lead to termination of banking and PSP relationships, freezing ordinary SaaS operations and triggering immediate clawback of assets by creditors.
Social Security and Wage Remittance Failure

Statutory sanction: Compulsory recovery of unpaid contributions plus 5% interest and administrative penalties; criminal complaint for fraudulent debt relief; exclusion from public procurement and financing facilities; retroactive reassessment of social-security contributions for up to five years.

Director personal liability: Under Art. 52 AHVG, directors are personally and unconditionally liable for unpaid social-security contributions; Swiss courts routinely pierce the corporate veil and issue personal insolvency proceedings, travel bans, asset seizures, and criminal convictions against directors of failing AGs.
