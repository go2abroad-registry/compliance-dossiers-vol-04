Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), including Business Registration Online, My Business Account, Represent a Client, and the CRA telephone and paper registration channels. The Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) administers customs export reporting through the Canadian Export Reporting System (CERS). Ontario corporate registry matters are administered separately through the Ontario Business Registry and are not substitutes for federal tax registration.

E-government filing portal

A corporation carrying on business in Canada, resident in Canada, or required to file a Canadian corporate income tax return must obtain a nine-digit Business Number (BN) from the CRA. A non-resident corporation may apply through CRA Business Registration Online where eligible, by submitting Form RC1 or the applicable non-resident registration request, or through an authorized representative. The corporation must provide its legal name, jurisdiction and date of incorporation, registered and mailing addresses, directors, principal business activity, accounting period, authorized contact, and details of Canadian business activities. The corporation then opens the applicable CRA program accounts, including the RC corporate income tax account and, where required or elected, the RT GST/HST account, RP payroll account, and RM import-export account. GST/HST registration is mandatory when the entity is not a small supplier and its worldwide taxable supplies, including those of associated persons where applicable, exceed the statutory threshold. Registration may be voluntary before the threshold is exceeded. A provincial Ontario corporation must also maintain its Ontario registry records, but Ontario does not issue a separate general corporate income tax identification number equivalent to the CRA BN; Ontario corporate income tax is generally administered through the federal T2 return.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Canada has no U.S.-style check-the-box election for corporations. A Canadian corporation is generally a separate taxpayer subject to federal and applicable provincial corporate income tax. A non-resident corporation remains subject to Canadian tax on income earned from carrying on business in Canada, subject to treaty relief and other statutory rules. Partnerships are generally flow-through for income tax purposes, while trusts are subject to separate trust taxation rules and may have beneficiary allocation mechanisms. An employee stock ownership or employee share incentive trust is not automatically tax-exempt and must be analyzed under the Income Tax Act provisions applicable to trusts, employee benefits, share-based compensation, designated employee benefit arrangements, and any applicable employee ownership trust rules. A corporation may make specific elections under the Income Tax Act, including elections concerning tax-deferred transfers, capital property, reorganizations, and certain trust or partnership transactions, but these are not entity-classification elections. Hybrid mismatch outcomes may arise under anti-avoidance, foreign affiliate, treaty, and hybrid instrument rules; there is no general elective hybrid classification regime.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

13%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

CAD 30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies, including zero-rated supplies, measured under the applicable small-supplier rules over the relevant single-quarter or consecutive-four-quarter period. The threshold generally applies to the person and associated persons where statutory association rules require aggregation. Certain public service bodies have a CAD 50,000 threshold. Registration may be voluntary below the threshold. A non-resident may be required to register where it makes taxable supplies in Canada and is carrying on business in Canada or otherwise falls within the GST/HST registration rules, subject to special rules for non-resident digital-economy suppliers and qualifying goods, services, or platforms.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Canada are generally zero-rated where the statutory GST/HST export conditions are satisfied, including delivery or transfer of possession outside Canada or shipment by the supplier to a destination outside Canada, together with prescribed documentary evidence. The supplier must establish that the goods left Canada and must retain commercial, shipping, customs, and delivery records. Services may be zero-rated where supplied to a non-resident and the specific place-of-supply and zero-rating conditions are met. The service must not fall within exclusions for services relating to real property or tangible personal property situated in Canada, services performed in respect of persons or property in Canada, passenger transportation, or other statutory exclusions. Certain advisory, administrative, financial, and plan-administration services supplied to non-residents can qualify, but the analysis depends on the recipient, use, performance, place of supply, and applicable exclusions. Zero-rated supplies support input tax credits, but zero-rating is not automatic merely because the customer is non-resident or payment is received from outside Canada.

  • Canadian Export Declaration or applicable CERS export report, where required by the Export and Import Permits Act, customs legislation, or the value and destination rules
  • Commercial invoice identifying the supplier, purchaser or consignee, description, quantity, value, currency, delivery terms, and destination
  • Bill of lading, air waybill, courier record, or equivalent transportation document
  • Packing list and proof of shipment or delivery outside Canada

Canada and Ontario do not currently impose a general real-time electronic invoice clearance mandate, universal tax-authority invoice pre-approval, or mandatory digital tax stamp for ordinary domestic business-to-business transactions. GST/HST registrants must issue invoices or receipts containing sufficient prescribed information when requested and must retain books and records for the statutory retention period. Electronic records are acceptable if they remain reliable, accessible, and reproducible. GST/HST returns and remittances are generally filed through CRA electronic services where electronic filing is required by the registrant's circumstances or elected. Export declarations for reportable commercial goods are filed electronically through CBSA CERS. Payroll, customs, and sector-specific electronic filing obligations may apply independently.

Canada has no general statutory requirement to repatriate export proceeds within 180 days and no general foreign-exchange surrender rule for export receipts. CRA may deny zero-rating or input tax credits where the supplier cannot prove export, non-resident status, qualifying use, place of supply, or compliance with statutory conditions. The denied GST/HST may become payable retroactively, together with arrears interest and applicable penalties. False statements, gross negligence, knowingly false claims, or inflated refund claims may result in penalties that can reach 50% of the understated tax or improperly claimed credit or refund, in addition to interest. Failure to register, collect, remit, file, or maintain records may trigger additional administrative penalties. Deliberate fraud or tax evasion may lead to prosecution, fines, and imprisonment. Incorrect or non-compliant customs export reporting may result in CBSA Administrative Monetary Penalty System penalties, reassessment, seizure, or other customs enforcement measures. Documentation should be retained for the applicable CRA statutory period, generally at least six years from the end of the relevant tax year.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Canada maintains a fully liberalized exchange-control regime; the Foreign Exchange Control Act was repealed in 1951, and no restrictions exist on current or capital account transactions. The Canadian dollar is freely convertible, with no licensing, surrender, or prior-approval requirements. Profit remittance is unrestricted subject to solvency tests under the Canada Business Corporations Act (or the Ontario Business Corporations Act) and Part XIII withholding tax on dividends, interest, or royalties payable to non-residents, as reduced by applicable tax treaties.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Canadian export receipts are not subject to any mandatory surrender timeline or conversion ratio. Exporters may freely retain, convert, or remit foreign currency proceeds without central bank consent. Only FINTRAC cross-border currency reporting obligations apply for physical or wire transfers exceeding CAD 10,000.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Canada (Banque du Canada), operating under the Bank of Canada Act, in coordination with the Department of Finance Canada (policy authority), the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) for bank supervision, and the Financial Transactions and Reports Analysis Centre of Canada (FINTRAC) under the Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA) for AML/compliance.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low feasibility for offshore EMIs as a primary banking relationship. Canada permits non-bank payment service providers under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA) with mandatory FINTRAC registration, but EMIs cannot accept deposits or provide direct clearing access without partnering with a prudentially regulated bank. Offshore EMIs face constraints under the Bank Act's deposit-taking monopoly and OSFI safeguarding requirements; they are not a viable substitute for domestic clearing rails.

Box-by-Box Return Breakdown (non-resident focus)

BoxField Non-resident guidanceTax-evasion pitfall
Box 1 Total revenue (gross receipts from all sources) Report only Canadian-source revenue if non-resident carrying on business in Canada. Late filing penalty: 5% of balance owing plus 1% per month up to 12 months; interest on late payments.
Box 2 Total expenses (deductible business expenses) Claim only expenses reasonably allocable to Canadian business operations. Late filing penalty: 5% of balance owing plus 1% per month up to 12 months; interest on late payments.
Box 3 Taxable income (revenue minus expenses and adjustments) Apply Canadian tax rules; non-residents may be subject to Part XIV tax on certain income. Late filing penalty: 5% of balance owing plus 1% per month up to 12 months; interest on late payments.

Box 5 Provincial tax payable (Ontario rate 11.5%) Ontario tax applies to income attributable to permanent establishment in Ontario. Late filing penalty: 5% of balance owing plus 1% per month up to 12 months; interest on late payments.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: CAD 1 million for goods, CAD 500,000 for services, CAD 250,000 for other transactions; Local File required if total revenue > CAD 50 million or transactions exceed thresholds; Master File if total revenue > CAD 500 million and cross-border transactions > CAD 5 million.

Economic substance scope: Canada does not have specific ESR, but GAAR and substance requirements apply to treaty benefits; corporations must have real economic activity to avoid anti-avoidance rules.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for penalties and interest if they knowingly participate.
AML

Statutory sanction: Fines up to CAD 2 million for individuals, imprisonment for individuals.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable for fines if they knowingly participate.
